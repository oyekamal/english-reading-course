# Non-Synthetic Reading Methods — Fair Assessment & Salvage Report

Scope: analytic phonics, whole language, balanced literacy (Fountas & Pinnell, Lucy Calkins),
three-cueing/MSV, leveled readers/guided reading, Reading Recovery, sight-word/Dolch/Fry vs
heart words, look-say, language experience approach (LEA), shared/interactive read-alouds.
Goal: extract what's worth keeping for a free, research-backed English reading course usable
absolute-beginner → advanced, any age, including adult/ESL learners (Pakistan context).

---

## 1. Key Findings Per Method

### 1.1 Analytic phonics (onset-rime, word families)
Analytic phonics teaches children to identify a whole word first, then analyze it into
parts (typically onset + rime, e.g. c-at, fl-ag), rather than blending individual phonemes
left-to-right (synthetic phonics: c/a/t → cat).

**Johnston & Watson, Clackmannanshire Synthetic Phonics Initiative (Scotland, ~300 children,
P1–P7, comparing synthetic phonics vs. two analytic-phonics comparison groups)**
- End of Primary 1: synthetic-phonics group ~7 months ahead of chronological age in word
  reading, ~8–9 months ahead in spelling, vs. the analytic-phonics groups.
- By Primary 7 (7-year follow-up): word reading 3 years 6 months ahead of chronological age;
  spelling 1 year 9 months ahead; reading comprehension only ~3.5 months ahead (comprehension
  gap much smaller than the decoding/spelling gap — worth noting for course design).
- Effect sizes for word identification grew year over year over the 7-year period.
- Programme fidelity note: the analytic-phonics comparison groups were later switched onto
  the synthetic-phonics programme and finished it within their first year — so the "analytic"
  arm was not run for 7 years, complicating the long-tail comparison somewhat.
- Sources: Johnston & Watson (2004, various), summarized at
  https://researchgate.net/publication/249744319 ,
  https://impact.ref.ac.uk/casestudies/CaseStudy.aspx?Id=34480 ,
  https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/726bc06e00e4d969b4b9d7698c1fe70f1b91fdf6

**Verdict:** analytic phonics is not "no phonics" — it is real phonics, just sequenced
differently and less explicit about blending mechanics. The Clackmannanshire evidence favors
synthetic over analytic for word reading/spelling speed, with a much smaller edge for
comprehension. Onset-rime/word-family instruction still has a legitimate place as a
*secondary* pattern-recognition layer once basic blending is automatic (see KEEP, §2).

### 1.2 Whole language (Goodman, Smith)
Kenneth Goodman's 1967 paper "Reading: A Psycholinguistic Guessing Game" argued that skilled
readers sample text and use semantic, syntactic, and graphophonic cues to *predict* words
rather than decode them precisely — the theoretical root of whole language and later
three-cueing/MSV.

- Critique (Hempenstall, others): Goodman's claim rests on treating a metaphor ("guessing
  game") as literal mechanism — reification fallacy — and generalizing it to ALL reading by
  ALL readers at all skill levels, not just early/struggling readers sampling unfamiliar text.
- Empirical follow-up (post-1990s eye-tracking/fMRI research, see §1.4 below) shows skilled
  readers fixate on nearly every letter in a word; they do not skip/predict from partial cues.
  This directly contradicts Goodman's central mechanism.
- Sources: https://www.taylorfrancis.com/chapters/edit/10.4324/9780203366929-10 ,
  https://www.nifdi.org/news/hempenstall-blog/442 ,
  https://www.thereadingape.com/single-post/2019/02/20 (Goodman/Smith/Clay lineage)

**Verdict:** the core decoding theory of whole language is empirically wrong for how skilled
readers process text. But whole language's classroom *culture* — rich authentic literature,
high-volume reading, writing for real purposes, motivation and choice — is separable from its
(wrong) theory of word recognition, and that culture is worth keeping.

### 1.3 Balanced literacy (Fountas & Pinnell, Lucy Calkins Units of Study)
Balanced literacy positioned itself as a "best of both worlds" compromise between phonics and
whole language, but in practice systematic phonics was often minimal/incidental and cueing
strategies (see §1.4) were taught explicitly for word-solving.

- **EdReports (2021) independent review:** Fountas & Pinnell Classroom (2020) and Lucy
  Calkins' Units of Study for Teaching Reading BOTH received "does not meet expectations"
  ratings across grade levels reviewed, primarily for weak/absent alignment to
  foundational-skills standards and text quality/complexity issues.
  Source: https://www.edweek.org/teaching-learning/new-curriculum-review-gives-failing-marks-to-popular-early-reading-programs/2021/11 ;
  https://edreports.org/reports/overview/fountas-pinnell-classroom-2020
- **Student Achievement Partners (2020) review of Units of Study:** prominent reading
  researchers found the program's treatment of foundational skills (phonemic awareness,
  phonics, sound-symbol mapping) did not reflect the last ~40 years of research on how
  reading skill develops.
  Source: https://www.edweek.org/teaching-learning/reading-workshop-unlikely-to-lead-to-literacy-success-researchers-say/2020/01 ;
  https://www.apmreports.org/episode/2020/01/27/lucy-calkins-reading-materials-review
- **"Sold a Story" (APM Reports podcast, Emily Hanford, 2022–2024)** traced the commercial
  and pedagogical influence of Marie Clay's cueing-based theory through Fountas & Pinnell's
  Leveled Literacy Intervention and Calkins' Units of Study into thousands of US districts.
  Publisher (Heinemann) disputed the reporting but simultaneously revised materials.
  Source: https://features.apmreports.org/sold-a-story/
- **Response/revision:** Calkins revised Units of Study in 2022 to add explicit phonics
  ("phonics units" bolted onto the reading workshop). Fountas & Pinnell said they would add
  more phonics practice/new routines. Teachers College closed the Reading and Writing Project
  in October 2023 and replaced it with a new program.
  Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/TCRWP's_Writing_Workshop
- **Writing Workshop component specifically** (distinct from the reading side): a smaller,
  less contested case-study literature finds scaffolded writer's workshop structure (mini
  lesson → independent writing with topic choice → conferring → share) improves student
  writing independence and engagement; choice-of-topic is the most-cited strength, though
  critics say too much unstructured choice under-serves rigor/standards alignment.
  Source: ERIC ED588005; https://www.edweek.org/teaching-learning/remodeling-the-workshop-lucy-calkins-on-writing-instruction-today/2016/06

**Verdict:** balanced literacy's foundational-skills component (how it teaches decoding) is
the well-documented failure point, confirmed by two independent reviewer bodies (EdReports,
Student Achievement Partners) plus investigative journalism. Its *writing workshop* structure
and its *classroom culture of real books/choice/conferring* are different components with
better-supported outcomes and should not be thrown out with the decoding failure.

### 1.4 Three-cueing / MSV (meaning, syntax/structure, visual)
The "searchlight model," MSV, or three-cueing system trains children — explicitly, via
prompts like "does that make sense?", "does that sound right?", "check the picture" — to use
context and pictures to guess an unfamiliar word rather than sound it out letter-by-letter.
Roots trace to Goodman (§1.2) and Marie Clay's Reading Recovery (§1.6).

- fMRI/eye-tracking research (cited across multiple secondary sources, e.g. Kentucky Dept of
  Education fact sheet, ExcelinEd fact sheet) shows skilled readers fixate on nearly every
  letter of nearly every word — they do not skip ahead by guessing from context/pictures.
  This contradicts the theoretical basis of three-cueing.
  Source: https://www.education.ky.gov/curriculum/EarlyLiteracy/Documents/Why_the_Three-Cueing_Method_Hinders_Reading_Proficiency.pdf ;
  https://excelined.org/wp-content/uploads/2022/12/ExcelinEd_FactSheet_ThreeCueingDoesNotTeachChildrenToRead.pdf
- Practical harm cited: cueing-taught children develop a habit of guessing from first letter
  + picture/context and stop self-correcting via phonics once they're "fluent enough" to
  guess correctly most of the time on easy leveled text — a habit that collapses on harder,
  less-predictable text later (a plausible mechanism behind Reading Recovery's negative
  long-term effect, §1.6).
- Policy response: at least 8 US states have statutorily banned three-cueing instruction in
  K–3 as of 2024. Source: https://excelinedinaction.org/2024/01/10/from-policy-to-action-why-8-states-banned-three-cueing-from-k-3-reading-instruction/

**Verdict:** DROP as a *decoding* strategy — this is the most research-discredited practice
in the whole non-synthetic bucket. However, "does that make sense?" as a *comprehension
self-monitoring* check (after decoding, not instead of it) is legitimate and should be kept —
the failure mode is using meaning/context to solve unknown words, not using meaning to check
comprehension of correctly decoded text.

### 1.5 Leveled readers and guided reading
Guided reading groups students by an assessed "instructional level" (Fountas & Pinnell A–Z,
or Lexile) and gives each group books calibrated to that level, on the theory that reading
material at a precisely matched level (not too hard, not too easy) maximizes learning.

- Tim Shanahan (Reading Hall of Fame, former leveled-reading practitioner/trainer) reviewed
  the foundational study behind "instructional level theory" and found it was never actually
  published and contained no evidence that teaching at a matched instructional level produces
  better learning outcomes than teaching with grade-level (harder) text plus scaffolding.
  Source: https://www.shanahanonliteracy.com/blog/rejecting-instructional-level-theory-1 ;
  https://hechingerreport.org/proof-points-shanahan-leveled-reading/
- Grouping-by-level for guided reading, done rigidly, tends to widen the gap between
  higher- and lower-achieving readers (low groups get less-complex language input, over
  time, than high groups) — cited across multiple secondary sources (SLJ, Shanahan).
  Source: https://www.slj.com/story/Where-did-Leveling-Go-Wrong
- Alternative Shanahan recommends: keep students on **grade-level** text and use
  pre-teaching vocabulary, chunking, and re-reading as the scaffolds, rather than routing
  weaker readers permanently to easier text.
- Shanahan's 2025 book "Leveled Readers, Leveled Lives" is the fullest current synthesis
  (unverified beyond secondary coverage — could not access full text).

**Verdict:** DROP rigid leveling-as-permanent-track and cueing-based "just right" book
selection. KEEP the underlying good instinct — every learner needs *some* text they can
succeed on for motivation/volume — but implement it as flexible, short-term scaffolding
(pre-teaching + chunking + re-reading of grade-appropriate text) rather than a permanent
level label that becomes a self-fulfilling ceiling.

### 1.6 Reading Recovery
One-on-one, 30-min/day, ~12–20 week pull-out intervention for the lowest-performing first
graders, developed by Marie Clay in New Zealand, teaching via cueing/MSV strategies on
leveled texts plus some phonics.

- **May et al. (2015), i3 4-year scale-up evaluation (Consortium for Policy Research in
  Education, U Penn + U Delaware CRESP), large multi-site RCT:**
  Year-1 effect size = **+0.69 SD** on ITBS Total Reading vs. the population of struggling
  readers eligible for RR (+0.47 SD vs. all first graders nationally). Effect sizes grew
  across years of the scale-up.
  Source: https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/abs/10.3102/0162373718764828 ;
  https://ies.ed.gov/ncee/WWC/Study/90698
- **Critique of the i3 study (Chapman & Tunmer, 2016, Reading Psychology):** many of the
  very lowest-achieving eligible students were excluded from the RR arm; the control group
  received a heterogeneous mix of other interventions (not a clean no-treatment control);
  RR's own completion/discontinuation rate was modest; no data on sustained gains.
  Source: https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ1106499
- **Sirinides, Gray & May (2022) long-term follow-up (grades 3–4), CRESP/U Delaware:**
  the long-term impact estimates turned **significant and negative** — children who received
  Reading Recovery in Grade 1 scored *below* matched non-RR peers on state reading tests in
  Grades 3–4, despite the strong short-term Grade-1 gains. Three explanatory hypotheses
  offered: (a) RR builds early skills that don't transfer to later, harder reading demands;
  (b) gains fade without continued support in later grades; (c) the initial effect was
  washed out by different subsequent classroom experiences.
  Source: https://www.edweek.org/teaching-learning/surprise-finding-suggests-reading-recovery-hurts-students-in-the-long-run/2022/04 ;
  https://www.apmreports.org/story/2022/04/23/reading-recovery-negative-impact-on-children ;
  https://www.shanahanonliteracy.com/blog/how-can-effective-teaching-do-harm

**Verdict:** this is the single most important CONTESTED/cautionary finding in this whole
research area — large, well-measured short-term gains from an individualized intervention
that *reversed sign* by grade 3–4. Directly implicates cueing-based instruction (RR's core
method) as building a fragile, non-transferable kind of "reading." KEEP the intensity/dosage
model (daily 1:1, short intensive burst for struggling readers) but DROP the cueing-based
instructional content inside it — pair the same delivery model with phonics-based content
instead (this is exactly what current "Reading Recovery reform" proposals in the field argue
for, per Shanahan's commentary).

### 1.7 Sight words: Dolch/Fry rote memorization vs. "heart words"/orthographic mapping
Traditional sight-word instruction (Dolch 1930s list, Fry 1950s list) asks children to
memorize ~200–300 common words by whole-word shape via repeated flashcard drilling,
independent of phonics.

- Ehri's orthographic-mapping research: skilled word recognition is built by mapping
  phonemes to graphemes and storing that phonological-orthographic connection in memory —
  not by whole-word shape memorization. Words with regular spelling should be *decoded*, not
  memorized; only the truly irregular part of an irregular word ("said" → /s/ /ai/(said as
  "eh") /d/, only the middle vowel is irregular) needs rote memorization ("by heart" — hence
  "heart words").
  Source: various secondary summaries at https://mrslearningbee.com/... ,
  https://pridereadingprogram.com/sight-words-vs-heart-words/
- Cited repetition-count claim (secondary source, not a peer-reviewed primary citation —
  **mark as unverified/approximate**): flashcard-based whole-word memorization may take
  5–500 repetitions per word to stick, vs. 1–4 repetitions for a word taught via
  phoneme-grapheme mapping, consistent with Ehri's broader claims about orthographic mapping
  being fast once phonics skills are in place.
- Overlap finding: ~37% (82/220-ish) of Dolch words contain at least one "heart letter"
  (irregular grapheme-phoneme part) — meaning most Dolch/Fry words are actually decodable and
  don't need rote memorization at all; only the truly irregular minority does.

**Verdict:** DROP whole-word rote flashcard drilling as the default method for high-frequency
words. KEEP the *goal* (fast, automatic recognition of the most common words) but reach it
via decoding-first + explicit heart-word marking of only the irregular part, which is both
faster per the research and reinforces the general decoding skill rather than working around
it.

### 1.8 Look-say / whole-word method (historical)
The direct 19th–20th century ancestor of sight-word and whole-language approaches; children
were taught to recognize whole word shapes ("look" then "say") rather than decode letters.
Championed by Horace Mann in the 1840s (rejected within the same decade by Boston
schoolmasters once results came in), revived and dominant via Gray/Gates and the "Dick and
Jane" readers from the 1920s–1950s.

- Samuel Orton (1929) linked the look-say method to reading disability patterns in children.
- Rudolf Flesch's "Why Johnny Can't Read" (1955) was the popular broadside against it.
- Jeanne Chall's "Learning to Read: The Great Debate" (1967) is the first major
  research-synthesis conclusion that (synthetic) phonics outperforms look-say — considered
  the formal end of look-say's academic legitimacy, though it persisted in practice for
  decades after via whole language/balanced literacy's cueing methods.
  Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_learning_to_read ;
  https://www.lexialearning.com/blog/the-science-of-reading-vs-balanced-literacy-the-history-of-the-reading-wars

**Verdict:** DROP outright. No modern salvageable component beyond "whole word shape
recognition still happens automatically once decoding is fluent" (i.e., automaticity is the
end-state goal, not the teaching method to get there).

### 1.9 Language Experience Approach (LEA)
Learner dictates a personally meaningful story/experience aloud; teacher transcribes it
verbatim; the transcript becomes the learner's own reading text, connecting their existing
oral vocabulary to print.

- Explicitly documented as effective and widely used with **adult learners** and adult ESL/
  foreign-language learners, not just young children — this is the one non-synthetic method
  with a real evidence base specifically for the adult-literacy population your course also
  targets.
  Source: ERIC ED350887 ("The Language Experience Approach and Adult Learners," ERIC Digest);
  https://atlasabe.org/resource/language-experience-approach-and-adult-learners/
- A cited study found LEA effective for improving reading fluency in Indonesian EFL
  (English-as-foreign-language) students — directly relevant to a Pakistan-context course.
  Source: https://www.researchgate.net/publication/340784005 (unverified beyond abstract-level
  secondary summary — treat specific fluency-gain numbers as unverified until primary text
  reviewed)
- LEA is described as building listening/speaking/writing alongside reading, not reading in
  isolation.

**Verdict:** KEEP, specifically as an adult/ESL/older-learner tool (see §6) — the guaranteed
match between the learner's own spoken vocabulary and the print text removes the "these
words mean nothing to me" barrier that generic children's readers create for older beginners.
Should NOT be the primary decoding-teaching method (it's not designed to teach systematic
phonics), but is an excellent supplementary motivation/meaning-bridge activity, especially for
absolute-beginner adults who find childish content patronizing.

### 1.10 Shared / interactive read-alouds
Adult reads text aloud to learner(s), pausing to discuss, predict, question, and explain
vocabulary — distinct from guided reading (learner reads) and from three-cueing (which
teaches the *learner* to guess); here, the adult does the decoding and the interaction targets
comprehension/vocabulary/oral language.

- A synthesis of 40+ experimental/quasi-experimental studies of shared and dialogic reading
  found **medium-to-large effect sizes** on both receptive and expressive vocabulary.
  Source: cited in secondary summaries, e.g. https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/EJ888269.pdf
- Key moderator: it's the **quality of adult-child interaction** (turn-and-talk, open
  questions, sustained dialogue) that drives the gain, not mere frequency/exposure — matters
  for course design (a scripted "read this, ask nothing" read-aloud is much weaker).
- Benefits reported as equal across high- and low-SES backgrounds, and caregivers/teachers of
  both backgrounds implement it equally effectively when given the technique — an important
  equity point for a free course aimed at under-resourced learners.
  Source: pubs.asha.org RCT (2020), https://pubs.asha.org/doi/10.1044/2020_JSLHR-19-00288

**Verdict:** KEEP fully — this is the strongest-evidenced practice in this entire non-synthetic
bucket, arguably strong enough to be a first-class pillar of the course, not just a "salvaged
part." Works for all ages (adapted register) and is one of very few practices here that
directly builds vocabulary/comprehension/oral language rather than word-recognition mechanics.

## 2. KEEP — Salvageable Parts (with evidence strength)

| Practice | From method | Evidence strength | Why keep |
|---|---|---|---|
| Interactive/dialogic read-alouds with discussion, not passive listening | Shared reading | **Strong** — meta-analytic, medium-large effect sizes, RCT-replicated, equity-neutral | Best-evidenced comprehension/vocabulary booster in this whole document; complements decoding instruction rather than competing with it |
| Language Experience Approach for absolute-beginner adults/ESL | LEA | **Moderate** — consistent adult-literacy literature, some EFL-specific studies (fluency claims unverified in primary source) | Solves the "childish content" and "no connection to my own vocabulary" problems that plague generic phonics readers for older learners |
| Writer's workshop structure: mini-lesson → independent writing with topic choice → conferring → sharing | Balanced literacy (writing side only) | **Moderate** — case-study/scaffolding literature, less contested than the reading side | Builds motivation, ownership, and real writing practice; keep it decoupled from Units of Study's reading-decoding content |
| Onset-rime/word-family pattern practice as a *secondary* layer after basic blending is automatic | Analytic phonics | **Moderate** — legitimate technique, just second-order in Clackmannanshire comparison | Useful for accelerating recognition of rime families (-at, -ight, -ould) once synthetic blending is secure; not a substitute for it |
| Rich, authentic children's/adult literature as reading material (not exclusively decodable texts) | Whole language | **Weak-to-moderate as decoding method, strong as engagement/motivation factor** | Motivation and volume of reading matter; use real books for enjoyment/read-aloud/independent reading time, use decodable texts specifically for the decoding-practice slot |
| Flexible, temporary scaffolds (pre-teach vocabulary, chunk text, re-read) instead of permanent "reading level" tracks | Guided reading (reformed) | **Moderate** — Shanahan's critique-and-alternative | Keeps struggling readers on grade-level, richer-language text instead of a permanently easier diet |
| "Does that make sense?" as a POST-decoding comprehension self-monitoring check | Three-cueing/MSV | **Weak-to-moderate, narrowly scoped** | Legitimate metacognitive habit if and only if it checks comprehension of a correctly decoded word/sentence, never used to guess an unknown word instead of decoding it |
| Choice and student agency in independent reading/writing time | Whole language + balanced literacy | **Moderate** — motivation literature broadly, not method-specific | Improves engagement and volume, which correlates with reading growth, independent of decoding method used |

## 3. DROP / AVOID — With Why

| Practice | From method | Why drop |
|---|---|---|
| Three-cueing/MSV as a WORD-SOLVING strategy ("check the picture," "what would make sense," guess-from-first-letter) | Whole language / three-cueing / Reading Recovery | fMRI/eye-tracking evidence contradicts the theory (skilled readers fixate nearly every letter); now legally banned in 8+ US states for K-3 instruction; plausible mechanism behind Reading Recovery's negative long-term effect |
| Whole-word/look-say flashcard memorization as the primary method for teaching any regularly-spelled word | Look-say, Dolch/Fry sight-word drilling | Slower (5–500 reps vs 1–4 for phonics-mapped words per secondary sources), bypasses/undermines the general decoding skill, historically discredited since Chall (1967) |
| Rigid ability-grouped guided reading with permanent "reading level" labels as the primary instructional structure | Guided reading / Fountas & Pinnell leveling | Foundational "instructional level theory" study was never published/evidenced (Shanahan); widens achievement gaps by permanently restricting lower groups to less-complex language input |
| Minimal/incidental phonics as the sole foundational-skills approach (i.e., decoding taught only "as it comes up" in leveled texts, not systematically/explicitly/sequentially) | Balanced literacy (Units of Study, Fountas & Pinnell Classroom, as originally published pre-2022 revisions) | Independently confirmed by two reviewer bodies (EdReports 2021, Student Achievement Partners 2020) as not reflecting 40 years of reading-development research; publishers themselves conceded and revised |
| Using Reading Recovery's cueing-based content even where the daily-intensive delivery model is kept | Reading Recovery | 2022 long-term follow-up: significant NEGATIVE effect by grade 3-4 despite strong grade-1 gains — the strongest documented "looks like it works, actually harms later" case in this research area |

## 4. CONTESTED

1. **Reading Recovery's reversal (short-term gain, long-term harm).** This is the most
   important contested/cautionary finding in the whole non-synthetic literature. It should
   inform course design generally: *any* intervention that shows strong short-term gains must
   be checked against longer-horizon follow-up before being trusted, especially if the
   intervention's content leans on cueing/guessing strategies that could create a fragile
   skill. Chapman & Tunmer's critique of the original i3 evaluation (sample exclusion,
   heterogeneous control group) also means the original +0.69 SD headline number should not be
   taken fully at face value either — evidence quality cuts both ways here.

2. **Analytic phonics vs. synthetic phonics for reading comprehension specifically.** The
   Clackmannanshire word-reading/spelling gap (3.5 years) is enormous; the comprehension gap
   (3.5 months) is comparatively tiny. This raises an open question this course should track:
   does analytic phonics eventually "catch up" on comprehension even while lagging badly on
   decoding speed/spelling? Or does the comprehension measure simply saturate/ceiling in this
   particular study? Not enough evidence gathered here to resolve — flag for course
   comprehension-assessment design.

3. **How much "authentic literature and choice" volume/engagement actually drives reading
   growth vs. how much is confounded with home literacy environment/general ability.** The
   whole-language/balanced-literacy motivation case is intuitively compelling and widely
   believed by practitioners but the specific causal evidence gathered in this pass is thinner
   than the decoding-methods evidence — mark as directionally supported, not rigorously proven,
   pending a dedicated motivation-literature research pass (may already be covered by another
   research file in this series).

4. **LEA fluency-gain numbers for EFL learners** (Indonesian EFL study cited above) — only
   accessed via abstract-level secondary summary, not the primary paper. Treat any specific
   numeric fluency-gain claim from that study as unverified until the primary source is read.

## 5. Concrete Design Rules for the Course

1. **Decoding instruction is synthetic-phonics-first, always.** Analytic/onset-rime pattern
   work is allowed only as a secondary layer AFTER basic left-to-right blending is automatic —
   never as the primary teaching method for new words (per §1.1, §2).
2. **Never teach "guess from picture/context" as a strategy for solving an unknown word.**
   Ban three-cueing/MSV word-solving prompts entirely from lesson scripts (per §1.4, §3).
   The ONLY legitimate use of "does this make sense?" is as a comprehension check AFTER a word
   has been correctly decoded — script this distinction explicitly so course authors don't
   accidentally reintroduce cueing under a different name.
3. **High-frequency words are taught via phoneme-grapheme mapping (heart words), never rote
   whole-word flashcard drilling.** Mark only the genuinely irregular grapheme in each word;
   decode the rest normally (per §1.7, §2).
4. **Every lesson unit includes a scripted, discussion-driven read-aloud or shared-reading
   segment, separate from the decoding-practice segment.** This is the single strongest-evidenced
   component in this document (§1.10) and should not be treated as filler/reward time — script
   specific open-ended questions and vocabulary-explanation points, not just "read and ask if
   they understood."
5. **Do not permanently level/track learners.** Use temporary scaffolds (pre-teach key words,
   chunk the passage, allow re-reading) to let learners engage with age/stage-appropriate
   material, rather than routing weaker readers to permanently easier text (§1.5, §2, §3).
6. **If the course ever includes an intensive 1:1/small-group remediation track for struggling
   readers, model its DELIVERY on Reading Recovery (daily, short-burst, individualized,
   12–20 weeks) but its CONTENT strictly on synthetic phonics — never cueing-based strategies
   (§1.6, §2, §3).** Build in a longer-horizon check (not just an immediate post-test) given
   the 2022 reversal finding.
7. **Independent writing time with topic choice, teacher conferring, and sharing** should be a
   parallel strand to the reading strand, not cut for time — it is the one balanced-literacy
   component with genuinely separable, positive evidence (§1.3, §2).
8. **For absolute-beginner OLDER learners (adults, older kids, ESL), open with a
   Language-Experience-Approach-style unit**: have the learner dictate/describe something in
   their own words, transcribe it, and use that as their first connected-text reading material,
   before or alongside generic decodable readers — avoids the "this is for little kids"
   disengagement problem (§1.9, §6).
9. **Track a comprehension metric separately from a decoding-speed/accuracy metric** in course
   assessments, given the Clackmannanshire finding that these two things can diverge sharply
   under different methods (§4.2) — don't assume a decoding win is automatically a
   comprehension win.
10. **Treat "the child enjoys it and reads more" as a real, trackable goal, not a soft add-on** —
    motivation/volume plausibly matters (§4.3) even though the causal evidence gathered here is
    thinner than for decoding method; build in genuine choice and real (not exclusively
    decodable) books once basic decoding is secure enough not to require cueing as a crutch.

## 6. Implications for Older / Adult / ESL Learners

- **LEA is the standout method here for this population** (§1.9) — it is explicitly documented
  as used with adult learners and adult ESL/foreign-language learners, unlike almost every
  other method in this document, which is researched primarily on young children. Use it early
  and often for absolute-beginner adults.
- **Generic children's decodable readers ("The cat sat on the mat") risk feeling patronizing
  to teenage/adult beginners.** The course should pair systematic phonics content (which must
  stay synthetic-phonics-based regardless of age, per §5.1) with age-appropriate *topics* and
  LEA-generated personal texts, rather than assuming decodable-text content must be
  childish.
- **Read-alouds/shared reading (§1.10) generalize well across ages** — the RCT evidence base
  spans child populations but the underlying mechanism (adult explains/models fluent reading,
  discusses meaning, explains vocabulary) has no obvious child-specific dependency; adapt
  register and topic, keep the interactive-discussion structure.
  For adult/ESL learners this can double as a listening-comprehension and pronunciation-model
  activity, which matters more for L2 learners than for L1 children.
- **Do NOT permanently level/track adult learners either** (§5.5) — adult learners are often
  even more sensitive to being placed in a "beginner reading" bucket that feels infantilizing;
  temporary scaffolds on real, adult-relevant material serve this population better than a
  leveled-reader ladder.
- **Writing workshop's choice-of-topic principle (§1.3, §2)** maps naturally onto adult/ESL
  learners writing about their own lives, work, and goals in English — likely higher-motivation
  material than assigned prompts, though this specific age-extension is inferred from the
  general choice/motivation literature rather than a study of adult writers-workshop outcomes
  directly (mark as reasoned extrapolation, not directly evidenced).
