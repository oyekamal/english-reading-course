## 1. Key Findings (citation + URL + numbers)

### Fluency

- **National Reading Panel (2000)** reviewed **77 studies of Guided Repeated Oral Reading (GROR) with feedback** (teacher/peer/parent guided). Found a **weighted average effect size of d = 0.41** (moderate) on reading achievement (word recognition, fluency, comprehension), across grade levels, in regular and special-education classrooms, through at least grade 4 and with struggling readers through high school. NRP explicitly did **not** find evidence supporting silent, independent reading practice (e.g. sustained silent reading / DEAR) as an effective fluency-building method — data were "conflicting or insufficient."
  Source: https://www.nichd.nih.gov/publications/pubs/nrp/findings , https://www.readingrockets.org/topics/curriculum-and-instruction/articles/findings-national-reading-panel

- **Kuhn & Stahl** (review, and Kuhn et al. 2006 "Teaching Children to Become Fluent and Automatic Readers," *Scientific Studies of Reading*): fluency instruction is generally effective; **assisted** reading approaches (choral, echo, partner, audio-supported) are more effective than **unassisted** independent reading; **repeated** reading of the same text does **not** show a clear advantage over **non-repetitive wide reading** when both are scaffolded/assisted — i.e., what matters is assisted practice with connected text, not repetition per se. Effective fluency instruction "moves beyond automatic word recognition to include rhythm and expression" (prosody).
  Source: https://advising.ecu.edu/wp-content/pv-uploads/sites/257/2019/07/kuhn_stahl.pdf , https://doi.org/10.1207/s15548430jlr3804_1

- **Stahl & Heubach / Kuhn et al., Fluency-Oriented Reading Instruction (FORI)** vs. **Wide Fluency-Oriented Reading Instruction (Wide-FORI)**: FORI = one grade-level text repeated/scaffolded across a week; Wide-FORI = 3 different grade-level texts per week, each read once with scaffolding, far less repetition. **Both produced similar year-end gains** in word-reading efficiency and comprehension vs. control; Wide-FORI's fluency gains **emerged earlier** in the year. This is direct evidence that volume of *scaffolded* reading of connected text matters as much as or more than repetition of a single passage.
  Source: https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1207/s15548430jlr3701_2

- **Rasinski (1999, unverified exact citation — cited across multiple secondary sources)**: students doing **Readers Theatre** gained an average of **17 WCPM over the study period** — described as "the expected gain for an entire year" — vs. less than half that gain for a traditional-instruction comparison group. Readers Theatre (repeated, purposeful oral rehearsal of a script for eventual performance, no memorization, no costumes) improves **word-recognition automaticity and prosody** specifically, and separately raises reading motivation/enthusiasm. [Numbers reported consistently across secondary sources; could not verify against the 1999 primary source directly in this session — mark CONFIDENCE: medium.]
  Source: https://scholarworks.wmich.edu/cgi/viewcontent.cgi?article=3073&context=reading_horizons , https://irrc.education.uiowa.edu/blog/2022/12/drama-classroom-readers-theatre-fluency-practice
  Rasinski & Samuels (2011) frame fluency as three components: **automaticity** (effortless word recognition) + **pace** (appropriate rate) + **prosody** (expressive phrasing/intonation) — not "reading fast." Phrase-cued texts (marked phrase boundaries, e.g. with slashes) improve prosody specifically (Rasinski 1990 review; replicated in Rodgers 2025, *Journal of Research in Reading*).

- **Hasbrouck & Tindal (2017), "An Update to Compiled ORF Norms," Technical Report No. 1702**, Behavioral Research and Teaching, U. Oregon: the standard U.S. oral-reading-fluency (WCPM) benchmark table, grades 1–6, at 5 percentile bands (90/75/50/25/10) × 3 seasons (Fall/Winter/Spring). **I could not extract the complete numeric table from the source PDFs in this session** (PDF fetch returned raw/encoded content or 403s) — only partial Spring/50th-percentile figures were recoverable from a secondary summary page:
  Grade 1: 60 · Grade 2: 100 · Grade 3: 112 · Grade 4: 133 · Grade 5: 146 · Grade 6: 146 (Spring, 50th percentile WCPM).

### Automaticity / orthographic mapping

- **Kilpatrick, "Equipped for Reading Success"**: orthographic mapping is the process by which skilled readers bond written spellings to their pronunciations and meanings in memory, producing **instant, effortless word recognition** — this, not "sight word memorization" or visual memory, is what automaticity actually is. The engine of orthographic mapping is **phonemic proficiency at the advanced level**: not just blending/segmenting, but **phoneme manipulation** (deletion, addition, substitution, reversal) done automatically. Kilpatrick's core claim: most reading-intervention failure is because programs stop at basic phonemic awareness (blending/segmenting) instead of pushing to advanced/automatic phoneme manipulation, which is the actual bottleneck for word-level automaticity in struggling readers past the earliest grades.
  Source: https://www.nwea.org/blog/2026/helping-words-stick-the-role-of-orthographic-mapping-in-reading/ , https://www.shanahanonliteracy.com/blog/rip-to-advanced-phonemic-awareness

### Vocabulary

- **Beck, McKeown & Kucan (2002, 2013), *Bringing Words to Life: Robust Vocabulary Instruction***: the **three-tier model**.
  - **Tier 1**: basic, high-frequency words of everyday spoken language ("clock," "happy") — rarely need direct instruction.
  - **Tier 2**: high-utility, cross-domain words of mature written/academic language ("coincidence," "reluctant," "analyze") — these are the **priority instructional target**: high frequency in books/academic contexts but low frequency in casual speech, so they are the words students are least likely to pick up incidentally.
  - **Tier 3**: low-frequency, domain/subject-specific words ("photosynthesis," "isotope") — teach only just-in-time, in content-area context.
  Estimate cited: of roughly ~15,000 root word families in mature language, ~8,000 are Tier 1, ~7,000 Tier 2/3 candidates for direct academic vocabulary instruction (2013 estimate).
  "Robust" instruction = multiple exposures, varied contexts, requiring active/generative use (not just define-and-use-in-a-sentence).
  Source: https://achievethecore.org/peersandpedagogy/robust-vocabulary-instruction , https://www.missdecarbo.com/three-tiers-of-vocabulary-instruction/

- **Stahl & Fairbanks (1986), "The Effects of Vocabulary Instruction: A Model-Based Meta-Analysis," *Review of Educational Research* 56(1):72-110**: mean effect size **d = 0.97** for comprehension of passages *containing the taught words*; mean effect size **d = 0.30** for global/standardized comprehension measures. Most effective methods combined **definitional + contextual information**, involved **deeper processing** (not rote definition copying), and gave **more than 1–2 exposures** per word.
  Source: https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.3102/00346543056001072

- **National Reading Panel (2000), vocabulary chapter**: vocabulary should be taught both directly and indirectly; repetition and multiple exposures matter; rich/varied contexts aid deep learning; active engagement beats passive; computer-based instruction can outperform some traditional methods for some outcomes; vocabulary tasks should be restructured for task/situation (i.e. no single "best method" — direct instruction, incidental exposure through wide reading, and word-learning strategies all need a place). (High-level qualitative; NRP's meta-analytic vocabulary evidence base was thinner/more mixed than for phonics or fluency.)
  Source: https://www.readingrockets.org/topics/curriculum-and-instruction/articles/findings-national-reading-panel

- **Nagy, Herman & Anderson (1985, 1987), "Learning Word Meanings from Context During Normal Reading," *American Educational Research Journal* / *AERJ 24(2)*, 352 students, grades 3/5/7**: incidental learning from context is real but **slow and probabilistic** — the probability of learning a new word's meaning from a **single exposure** in context is roughly **~5% (0.05 probability, sometimes reported as ~10-15% depending on word/text)** [reported figure varies by source in this session's searches — treat the "~1 in 20 single exposures" order of magnitude as the load-bearing number, mark exact percentage UNVERIFIED, re-check primary source before publishing]. Multiple exposures across varied contexts are necessary for durable learning; this is the empirical basis for "wide reading builds vocabulary, but slowly, and needs to be supplemented by direct instruction for Tier 2 words."
  Also: learning one word tends to make **1–3 morphologically/semantically related words** more accessible, depending on the learner's ability to use context + morphology.
  Source: https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.3102/00028312024002237

### Morphology instruction

- **Bowers, Kirby & Deacon (2010), "The Effects of Morphological Instruction on Literacy Skills: A Systematic Review," *Review of Educational Research* 80(2):144-179**, 22 studies, preschool–grade 8. **Small-to-moderate positive effects** on reading, spelling, and reading comprehension vs. untreated/business-as-usual controls; effects **shrink toward zero** vs. alternative-treatment controls (i.e. morphology instruction ≈ as good as, not clearly better than, other well-designed literacy instruction — it's a good use of instructional time, not a silver bullet). Morphological instruction **particularly benefits less-able readers**; is **no less effective for younger students** (contradicts the old assumption that morphology should wait until later grades); **more effective combined with other literacy instruction** than taught in isolation. Effects largest for **sub-lexical** tasks (manipulating morphemes within words), smallest for **supra-lexical** tasks (sentence/paragraph comprehension) — i.e. morphology instruction reliably improves word-level skills; the transfer to comprehension is real but weaker and less direct.
  Source: https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/abs/10.3102/0034654309359353

- **Goodwin & Ahn (2013), "A Meta-Analysis of Morphological Interventions in English," *Scientific Studies of Reading*, 30 studies, 92 effect sizes**. Overall effect **d̄ = 0.32** (moderate). By outcome: morphological knowledge **d̄=0.44**, phonological awareness **d̄=0.48**, vocabulary **d̄=0.34**, decoding **d̄=0.59** (largest), spelling **d̄=0.30** — but **no significant effect on reading comprehension or fluency**. Effects were larger for students with **reading/learning/language disabilities, ELLs, and struggling readers** — morphology instruction as a targeted-intervention tool, not just a general-curriculum add-on. Larger effects in quasi-experimental designs and researcher-made (vs. standardized) outcome measures — apply a discount for real-world/standardized-test transfer.
  Source: https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ1012349

- **White, Sowell & Yanagihara (1989), "Teaching Elementary Students to Use Word-Part Clues," *The Reading Teacher***: analyzed **2,959 prefixed words** in school reading material and found that **20 prefixes account for ~97%** of all prefixed words encountered; the prefix **"un-" alone accounts for ~26%**; the top **4 prefixes (un-, re-, in-/im-"not", dis-)** together cover **~58%**. This is the empirical justification for teaching only a short, high-yield prefix list rather than an exhaustive one. Full ranked list (order = teaching priority, most common first):
  `un-, re-, in-/im-/ir-/il- ("not"), dis-, en-/em-, non-, in-/im- ("in"), over-, mis-, sub-, pre-, inter-, fore-, de-, trans-, super-, semi-, anti-, mid-, under-`
  Source: https://keystoliteracy.com/wp-content/uploads/2017/03/CommonPrefix.pdf , https://keystoliteracy.com/blog/using-morphology-to-teach-vocabulary/

- **Derivational suffixes** (no single canonical "97%" study found in this session comparable to White/Sowell/Yanagihara for suffixes — the commonly cited high-utility set across multiple secondary pedagogical sources, MARK: convergent-but-not-meta-analytically-ranked): `-s/-es, -ed, -ing, -ly, -er/-or, -tion/-sion, -able/-ible, -al, -y, -ness, -ment, -ful, -less, -ive, -ity`. These are the standard "most productive/most frequent" derivational and inflectional suffixes cited across Anita Archer-style multisyllabic-word curricula and morphology-instruction guides.
  Source: https://literacylearn.com/common-suffixes-free-list/ , https://ejournal.upi.edu/index.php/IJAL/article/viewFile/28589/13020

- **Greek & Latin roots**: no single "White-Sowell-Yanagihara-equivalent" ranked/quantified root list was located in this session (claims of "75% of English words derive from Greek/Latin" and "100 most common Latin/Greek stems unlock 5,000+ words" are **repeated across pedagogical blogs without a primary citation** — MARK UNVERIFIED, do not present as a research-backed number in the course; treat as folk-pedagogy consensus, useful directionally but not citable as "research says"). Practical convergent recommendation: start with **number-prefixes** (mono-, uni-, bi-, tri-, quad-, pent-, hex-, oct-, dec-) since they are self-evidently transparent and motivating, then move to the highest-yield roots by classroom frequency (e.g. spect, port, dict, struct, scrib/script, tract, rupt, vis/vid, aud, graph/gram, ject, mit/mis, cred, duc/duct, ver, corp, gen, log/logy, phon, therm). Teach 5 roots/affixes per week alongside ~10 derived words as one commonly-cited pacing.

### Spelling as reading support

- **Graham & Santangelo (2014), "Does Spelling Instruction Make Students Better Spellers, Readers, and Writers? A Meta-Analytic Review," *Reading and Writing* 27(9):1703-1743**, 53 studies, 6,037 students, K-12, 58 effect sizes. Key finding: **formal spelling instruction is significantly better than no/incidental spelling instruction — effect size d = 0.54, p ≤ .001**. Spelling instruction also improved **phonological awareness, reading, and word decoding** — evidence for the encoding↔decoding reciprocal link (spelling practice reinforces the same grapheme-phoneme mappings needed for decoding, in the opposite direction). [Exact effect sizes for the reading-specific and phonological-awareness-specific outcomes were not fully retrievable in this session's searches — the d=0.54 figure is confirmed for "formal instruction vs. no/unrelated instruction" overall; get exact per-outcome numbers from the full text before final publication.]
  Source: https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11145-014-9517-0 , https://texasldcenter.org/education-research-matters/november-2014/

### Multisyllabic word strategies

- **Archer, Gleason & Vachon (2008), "Syllable Types: A Strategy for Reading Multisyllabic Words," *TEACHING Exceptional Children*, 40(5)**: explicit instruction in the **6 syllable types** (closed, open, silent-e/VCe, vowel team, r-controlled, consonant-le) plus a flexible word-attack strategy (find vowels → mark prefixes/suffixes → divide the base into syllable-type chunks → blend) is the standard, evidence-based approach for grades 3–12 and remedial/older-student contexts. The claim that this generalizes well to older/adult readers is supported qualitatively by fMRI/eye-tracking evidence that **even skilled adult readers parse morphological and syllabic units rather than whole-word memorize** — validating explicit chunking instruction as appropriate at any age, not just "babyish" for older learners.
  Source: https://academics.franciscan.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/18/2022/03/6-syllables-sight-recog-extra-4-wr.pdf , https://www.nwea.org/blog/2026/6-strategies-for-teaching-multisyllabic-word-reading/

### High-frequency words / "heart words"

- The **Dolch list** (Edward Dolch, 1930s-40s): 220 "service words" + 95 nouns, compiled from children's-book frequency analysis, split into Pre-Primer/Primer/1st/2nd/3rd grade tiers. The **Fry list** (Edward Fry, 1950s, updated 1980) extended this with a larger, more contemporary corpus-frequency-based list.
  Source: https://www.readsters.com/wp-content/uploads/2013/03/ComparingDolchAndFryLists.pdf
- Key empirical/practical finding repeated across multiple sources: **roughly 63% of the Dolch list is fully phonetically decodable** (e.g. *and, big, but, made, pick, this, wish, well*) using ordinary phonics rules — meaning most "sight words" should be taught **through phonics, not rote memorization**. Only the remaining **~27% (~60 words)** contain a genuinely irregular part that must be explicitly flagged and memorized. The **"Heart Word" method**: segment the word into sounds, show which parts are regular/decodable, and explicitly mark **only the irregular part** as the piece to "know by heart" (hence "heart word," not "whole word memorization"). This reframes high-frequency-word instruction as an extension of phonics instruction rather than a separate rote-memorization track.
  Source: https://luckylittlelearners.com/how-to-teach-heart-words/ , https://www.readingrockets.org/topics/phonics-and-decoding/articles/new-model-teaching-high-frequency-words

## 2. KEEP (strong evidence)

- Assisted, scaffolded oral reading of connected text (echo/choral/partner/audio-supported) — NRP d=0.41, replicated across FORI/Wide-FORI studies.
- Explicit syllable-type / word-part chunking strategy for multisyllabic words (Archer et al.) — works across ages including adult/ESL per neuroscience evidence.
- Heart-word method for high-frequency words: decode the regular part, flag only the irregular part — evidence-based reframe, not folk wisdom.
- Formal, systematic spelling instruction (Graham & Santangelo, d=0.54) as a reading-reinforcing activity, not a separate/optional subject.
- Robust (Tier 2-focused, multi-exposure, generative-use) vocabulary instruction — Stahl & Fairbanks d=0.97 on taught-word comprehension.
- Morphology instruction generally, ESPECIALLY for struggling readers/ELLs/students with LD (Goodwin & Ahn: larger effects for these groups) and ESPECIALLY for decoding/spelling outcomes (d=0.59, d=0.30) rather than as a comprehension-fix tool.
- High-yield, frequency-ranked prefix teaching order (White/Sowell/Yanagihara's 20-prefix, 97%-coverage list) — one of the few morphology findings with an actual quantified coverage number; use it as-is for sequencing.
- Reader's Theatre / repeated purposeful oral performance practice for prosody + motivation, particularly for older/struggling readers who resist "baby" fluency drills.
- Phrase-cued texts (marked phrase boundaries) to build prosody specifically.

## 3. DROP / AVOID

- Sustained Silent Reading (SSR) / "just read more silently and independently" as the primary fluency-building method for struggling readers — NRP found insufficient/conflicting evidence. Silent independent reading has a place for volume/motivation but should not replace assisted oral practice for anyone below fluent-automatic level.
- Presenting repeated reading of a single text as inherently superior to varied/wide reading — Kuhn & Stahl found no clear advantage for repetition once assisted/scaffolded reading is controlled for. Design implication: rotate texts (Wide-FORI style) rather than drilling the same passage to exhaustion — better motivation, equal or better outcomes.
- Whole-word/rote "sight word" memorization presented as separate from phonics — evidence (63% decodability of Dolch list) argues this should be folded into phonics instruction, with heart-word marking only for the truly irregular ~27%.
- Definition-only or "look it up and use in a sentence" vocabulary instruction — Stahl & Fairbanks explicitly found this weaker than approaches combining definitional + contextual info + deeper processing + multiple exposures.
- Presenting morphology instruction as a comprehension intervention — Goodwin & Ahn found no significant effect on comprehension or fluency; position it honestly as a decoding/spelling/vocabulary tool with indirect, not guaranteed, comprehension payoff.
- Unsourced "75% of English words are Greek/Latin-derived" and "100 roots unlock 5,000 words" claims — repeated in pedagogy blogs without a locatable primary study. Do not cite these as "research shows" in course marketing/instructional copy; use softer framing ("many English academic words share a small set of roots").
- An unverified full Hasbrouck-Tindal numeric table — do not publish a fabricated or reconstructed-from-memory version of the complete grade×percentile×season matrix. Pull it directly from the primary PDF before shipping (see §5 action item).

## 4. CONTESTED

- **Repeated reading vs. wide/varied reading** for fluency: NRP's original 77-study synthesis emphasized repeated reading; later Kuhn/Stahl work (and the FORI vs Wide-FORI head-to-head) suggests wide reading is at least as effective and produces earlier fluency gains — the field has moved from "repetition is the active ingredient" toward "assisted/scaffolded practice volume is the active ingredient, repetition optional."
- **Morphology instruction's contribution beyond phonics/spelling**: Bowers, Kirby & Deacon found effects shrink to near-zero versus *alternative* (rather than no-treatment) literacy instruction — i.e., morphology instruction is a good way to spend instructional time, but not proven to be better than other well-designed literacy time. Treat "structured word inquiry" advocacy claims of transformative comprehension gains as **overstated relative to the meta-analytic evidence**, which shows the reliable effects are at the word level (decoding, spelling), not comprehension/fluency.
- **Exact incidental-word-learning-from-context rate**: sources in this session gave inconsistent percentages (roughly 5–15% per single exposure depending on the summary). The direction (single exposures are unreliable, multiple varied exposures are needed) is solid; the precise number needs verification against Nagy/Herman/Anderson (1985/1987) primary text before quoting a specific percentage to users.
- **Reader's Theatre effect size (17 WCPM gain)**: consistently repeated across secondary/blog sources citing "Rasinski 1999" but not independently verified against a primary source in this session — treat as plausible/likely accurate given how consistently it's cited, but downgrade confidence until the primary citation is checked.

### 5b. Fluency instructional sequence (all ages)
1. **Model** fluent reading of the target text first (teacher/audio) — establish the prosody target before the learner attempts it.
2. **Assisted practice**, not solo drilling: echo reading → choral reading → partner reading → then independent.
3. Prefer **rotating texts across a week** (Wide-FORI pattern) over drilling one passage to death — same or better gains, higher engagement, works better for adult/self-directed learners who won't tolerate rote repetition.
4. For **older kids/teens/adults who find fluency drills babyish**: use Readers Theatre (script-based, performance-oriented, no memorization) as the vehicle — same skill, adult-compatible framing.
5. Use **phrase-cued texts** (marked with `/` at natural phrase boundaries) explicitly during the early fluency stage to teach prosody, then fade the markup.
6. Track WCPM against the benchmark table (§5a) but **always pair the rate metric with a prosody/expression check** — Rasinski's warning that fluency ≠ speed applies directly; don't let learners or the app over-optimize for raw WCPM.

### 5c. Automaticity / orthographic mapping
- Before pushing multisyllabic/vocabulary work, verify the learner has **advanced phonemic manipulation** (not just blend/segment): can they delete, add, substitute, and reverse phonemes in spoken words fluently? If not, this — not more phonics content — is Kilpatrick's diagnosed bottleneck. Build a short phoneme-manipulation diagnostic/drill set into the course's placement/remediation path, not just an initial phonics unit.

### 5d. Multisyllabic word strategy (teach explicitly, all ages including adult/ESL)
Teach the flexible word-attack strategy directly, in this order:
1. Find and mark the vowels in the word.
2. Peel off any prefix/suffix you recognize.
3. Divide the remaining base into syllable-type chunks (6 types: closed, open, silent-e/VCe, vowel team, r-controlled, consonant-le).
4. Blend the chunks, adjusting for natural stress/schwa.
This is appropriate for adult/ESL learners as-is — it is not a "kid-only" strategy; frame it as "how skilled readers actually process long words" (supported by eye-tracking/fMRI evidence of morphological/syllabic parsing even in fluent adult readers), which removes any stigma for older learners.

### 5e. Vocabulary
- Prioritize **Tier 2 words** (Beck/McKeown/Kucan) for direct instruction — cross-domain academic/written-register words learners won't reliably pick up from casual speech or one-off reading exposure.
- Every direct-vocabulary lesson must include: a **student-friendly definition**, **contextual example(s)**, and a **generative/active-use task** (not just "use it in a sentence" passively) — apply Stahl & Fairbanks' finding directly.
- Give each target word **3+ varied exposures** across different contexts/sessions, not a single encounter.
- Treat wide reading as a **supplementary, not primary**, vocabulary-growth mechanism — it works, but slowly (single-exposure learning is unreliable) — pair every extended-reading activity with a short explicit-vocabulary follow-up for the Tier 2 words it contained.

### 5f. Morphology teaching order (prefixes — verified quantified list; roots/suffixes — convergent pedagogical list, mark as such in course metadata)

**Prefixes, in priority teaching order** (White, Sowell & Yanagihara 1989 — 20 prefixes = ~97% coverage of prefixed words; un- alone = ~26%; top 4 = ~58%):
`un-, re-, in-/im-/ir-/il- (not), dis-, en-/em-, non-, in-/im- (in/into), over-, mis-, sub-, pre-, inter-, fore-, de-, trans-, super-, semi-, anti-, mid-, under-`

**Suffixes, commonly-cited high-utility teaching set** (not independently quantified/ranked in this session — teach after basic inflectional suffixes are automatic):
Inflectional first: `-s/-es, -ed, -ing`.
Then derivational, roughly in order of classroom frequency: `-ly, -er/-or, -tion/-sion, -able/-ible, -al, -y, -ness, -ment, -ful, -less, -ive, -ity`.

**Roots — start with number-prefixes (self-evidently transparent, good early win)**: `mono-, uni-, bi-, tri-, quad-, pent-, hex-, oct-, dec-`.
Then move to high-frequency Greek/Latin roots by convergent recommendation (unverified against a primary frequency study — present in-course as "commonly useful," not "research-ranked"): `spect (see), port (carry), dict (say), struct (build), scrib/script (write), tract (pull/drag), rupt (break), vis/vid (see), aud (hear), graph/gram (write/draw), ject (throw), mit/mis (send), cred (believe), duc/duct (lead), ver (truth), corp (body), gen (birth/produce), log/logy (study/word), phon (sound), therm (heat)`.
Pace: ~5 roots/affixes per week alongside ~8-10 derived example words — a commonly cited, reasonable pacing given no contrary evidence found.

Design constraint from the evidence: **combine morphology instruction with decoding/spelling practice, not as a comprehension shortcut** — teach students to use morphemes to (a) decode/read the word, (b) spell it, (c) infer a plausible meaning to check against context — but don't oversell it as a comprehension strategy on its own (Goodwin & Ahn: no significant comprehension effect).

### 5g. Spelling
- Include **formal, systematic spelling instruction** as a required strand, not an optional add-on — it has a documented, non-trivial effect on reading/decoding/phonological awareness (Graham & Santangelo, d=0.54 vs. no/incidental instruction), independent of a dedicated "spelling module" being a common cut candidate in reading-focused course designs.
- Sequence spelling patterns to match the phonics/morphology scope-and-sequence already taught for decoding — same grapheme-phoneme correspondences, same affix list — so spelling reinforces rather than introduces new content.

### 5h. High-frequency / heart words
- Never introduce a high-frequency word as "just memorize this whole word." For every word on the Dolch/Fry list: **segment it into sounds first**; identify which parts are decodable via phonics already taught; **only** explicitly flag and drill the genuinely irregular part (the "heart" of the heart word).
- Use Dolch/Fry as a **checklist of exposure/coverage**, not as a standalone rote-memorization curriculum — fold every entry into the ongoing phonics scope-and-sequence at the point where its phonics pattern is being taught.

## 6. Implications for Older / Adult / ESL Learners

- **Nothing in this evidence base is age-restricted in principle** — syllable-type strategy instruction, morphology instruction, and heart-word decoding all have direct rationale (including neuro-cognitive evidence of adult skilled readers parsing morphemes/syllables, not whole-word memorizing) for use with older kids, adults, and ESL learners. Reframe delivery, not content: avoid childish visuals/texts; use Readers Theatre / adult-appropriate scripts and passages for fluency practice instead of picture-book-style repeated-reading texts.
- **ESL/multilingual learners get outsized benefit from morphology instruction** (Goodwin & Ahn found larger effects for ELLs specifically) — prioritize the morphology strand for adult/ESL tracks, not just as an "advanced extra."
- **Vocabulary for adult/ESL learners**: Tier 2 academic/cross-domain vocabulary is exactly the register adult learners and ESL learners most need for workplace/academic contexts and are least likely to acquire from casual spoken exposure — the Beck/McKeown/Kucan Tier framework maps cleanly onto adult-learner needs without modification.
- **Fluency for adults**: the underlying automaticity/prosody targets are identical; the delivery format (Readers Theatre scripts, adult nonfiction/news-style passages, phrase-cued adult text) should be adult-appropriate. Assisted reading (audio-supported/partner) remains the evidence-based method regardless of age — don't assume adults only need silent reading practice.
- **Caution**: none of the fluency benchmark norms (Hasbrouck & Tindal) were normed on adult or L2 learners — they are K-6 U.S. school norms. Use them only as a rough automaticity reference for adult/ESL learners, explicitly caveat in the course that the numeric benchmarks are a children's-classroom instrument being borrowed for a rough estimate, not a validated adult-fluency norm.
