# 06 — Comprehension & Advanced/Critical Reading

Scope: upper strands of Scarborough's rope — comprehension strategy instruction, background
knowledge, text structure, reciprocal teaching, questioning, summarisation, inference, syntax,
text complexity/close reading, writing-to-read, disciplinary literacy, critical/lateral reading,

real finish line. All numbers below are as reported in the cited secondary sources found via

## 1. Key Findings

### 1.1 Comprehension strategy instruction — does explicit strategy teaching work?

- **National Reading Panel (NICHD, 2000), Chapter 4-II** — reviewed comprehension strategy
  research; identified strategies with a research base as effective when taught individually
  or in combination: comprehension monitoring, cooperative learning, graphic/semantic
  organizers, story structure, question answering, **question generation**, and summarization
  (the commonly cited "7 strategies"). Strongest single-strategy evidence: **question
  generation**, 27 studies grades 3–9, effect sizes **0.95 (multiple choice, n=6)**,
  **0.85 (short-answer, n=14)**, **0.85 (summary measures, n=3)**. Panel's overall
  recommendation: **teaching multiple strategies in combination is more effective than any
  single strategy alone.**
  Source: https://www.nichd.nih.gov/sites/default/files/publications/pubs/nrp/Documents/ch4-II.pdf ;
  https://www.readingrockets.org/topics/curriculum-and-instruction/articles/findings-national-reading-panel

- **Willingham (2006), "The Usefulness of Brief Instruction in Reading Comprehension
  Strategies"; Willingham & Lovette (2014), "Can Reading Comprehension Be Taught?"** —
  central claim: comprehension strategies are **"a bag of tricks"** that indirectly improve
  comprehension by making the reader's existing (usually tacit) processes explicit and
  effortful; they are **easy to learn and need minimal practice** — extended/repeated
  practice of strategy instruction shows **no added benefit over brief, targeted instruction**
  once the strategy is learned. Precondition: the strategies only help if the student can
  already decode fluently — strategies are not a fix for a decoding problem, and they cannot
  substitute for background knowledge or vocabulary.
  Source: http://www.danielwillingham.com/uploads/5/0/0/7/5007325/willingham&lovette_2014_can_reading_comprehension_be_taught_.pdf ;
  https://education.ufl.edu/patterson/files/2020/10/Willingham-ComprehensionStrategies.pdf

- **Elleman & Compton (2017)** meta-analytic/synthesis work on reading comprehension
  interventions, incl. inference-focused instruction. Specific pooled effect size **not
  confirmed in this pass** — searches surfaced only citations to it inside newer
  meta-analyses (Sohn et al. 2023, Filderman et al. 2022), not the primary number.
  **[UNVERIFIED — could not source directly; re-fetch Elleman & Compton (2017) "Inferring
  from text: issues and complexities" or the relevant Elleman meta-analysis before quoting
  a number in the course.]**

- **Okkinga, van Steensel, van Gelderen & Sleegers (2018), "Effectiveness of
  Reading-Strategy Interventions in Whole Classrooms: A Meta-Analysis," Educational
  Psychology Review** — whole-classroom (not pull-out) strategy instruction, standardized
  tests: **very small effect, d = 0.186**; researcher-developed comprehension tests:
  **small effect, d = 0.431**; strategic ability (metacognitive knowledge of strategies):
  **medium effect, d = 0.786**. Moderators: effects larger when "setting reading goals" was
  part of the package, when the **researcher** (not the classroom teacher) delivered the
  intervention, and in **grades 6–8**. Modelling was the strongest didactic principle.
  Source: https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10648-018-9445-7
  **Reading between Willingham and Okkinga: strategy instruction has a real but small and
  fragile effect once ecologically valid (teacher-delivered, standardized-test) conditions
  apply — consistent with "teach briefly, don't drill."**

### 1.2 Background knowledge

- **Recht & Leslie (1988), "Effect of Prior Knowledge on Good and Poor Readers' Memory of
  Text," Journal of Educational Psychology** — the "baseball study." 64 junior-high
  students, crossed on reading ability × baseball knowledge; read a baseball-game passage,
  reenacted it on a model field. **Baseball knowledge outweighed generic reading-skill
  rating**: poor readers with high baseball knowledge outperformed good readers with low
  baseball knowledge on comprehension/recall measures. Poor readers with high topic
  knowledge were also **better at identifying important ideas and summarizing** than their
  knowledge level alone would predict.
  Source: https://www.coreknowledge.org/baseball-experiment-two-wisconsin-researchers-discovered-comprehension-gap-knowledge-gap/ ;
  https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Baseball_Study
  **CONTESTED** — Reynolds (2024, *Reading Teacher*; 2025, *RRQ*) has since re-examined the
  study, raising questions about gendered knowledge domains and over-generalization from a
  single-topic, small, non-representative sample. Use the finding as directional evidence
  for "knowledge matters, sometimes more than 'skill'" — not as proof that skill instruction
  is dispensable.

- **Smith, Snow, Serry & Hammond (2021), "The Role of Background Knowledge in Reading
  Comprehension: A Critical Review," Reading Psychology** — background knowledge is real
  and substantial but **partially, not fully, compensatory**: it cannot fully substitute for
  weak decoding or weak strategic reading; different knowledge types matter differently for
  different comprehension tasks (single-text recall vs. multiple-text synthesis).
  Source: https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/pdf/10.1080/02702711.2021.1888348

- **Tyner & Kabourek (2020), "Social Studies Instruction and Reading Comprehension:
  Evidence from the Early Childhood Longitudinal Study," Thomas B. Fordham Institute** —
  used ECLS-K:2011 (thousands of US students, K→grade 5). Elementary students got **~2
  hrs/day ELA, 82 min/day math, ~30 min/day each for science and social studies.** More
  time on social studies (not more ELA time) predicted better grade-5 reading:
  **+30 min/day of social studies → +15% of one SD on the grade-5 reading test.** No
  comparable effect found for additional ELA time itself.
  Source: https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/ED609934.pdf ; https://fordhaminstitute.org/national/resources/social-studies-instruction-and-reading-comprehension

- **Grissmer & Buddin et al. (2023), Core Knowledge charter-school lottery study (UVA,
  David Grissmer, lead)** — six-year, lottery-based (as-good-as-randomized) study, 2,310
  Colorado charter-school applicants; children who attended Core Knowledge-curriculum
  charter schools from kindergarten had **stronger reading scores in grades 3–6** than
  lottery-losers who attended other schools, and the knowledge-building approach **"nearly
  eliminated income-based achievement gaps"** in the study's framing. **Not yet
  peer-reviewed** at time of the cited press coverage — treat as strong but pre-publication
  evidence.
  Source: https://fordhaminstitute.org/national/commentary/long-last-ed-hirsch-jr-gets-his-due-new-research-shows-big-benefits-core ;
  https://knowledgemattterscampaign.org (see knowledgematterscampaign.org)

### 1.3 Text structure instruction

- **Hebert, Bohaty, Nelson & Brown (2016), "The Effects of Text Structure Instruction on
  Expository Reading Comprehension: A Meta-Analysis," Journal of Educational Psychology,
  108(5), 609–629** — 45 studies, grades 2–12. **Overall effect: Hedges' g = 0.56.**
  For students with/at-risk-for disabilities: **large effect, g = 0.96.** Effects shrank
  when compared against stronger (active) control groups rather than business-as-usual.
  Source: https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/ED648909.pdf ; https://www.shanahanonliteracy.com/blog/does-text-structure-instruction-improve-reading-comprehension

### 1.4 Reciprocal teaching / questioning

- **Palincsar & Brown (1984)** — originated reciprocal teaching: predicting, questioning,
  clarifying, summarizing, done as a scaffolded teacher–student dialogue over text, gradually
  released to student-led.
- **Rosenshine & Meister (1994), "Reciprocal Teaching: A Review of the Research," Review of
  Educational Research, 64(4), 479–530** — 16 quantitative studies. **Median effect on
  standardized comprehension tests: d = 0.32.** **Median effect on experimenter-developed
  tests: d = 0.88.** Conclusion: reciprocal teaching works, generalizes less well to
  standardized measures, and implementation guidance in the original studies was thin.
  Source: https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.3102/00346543064004479

- **Elaborative interrogation** ("why" questioning, tied to prior knowledge) — Dunlosky et
  al. (2013) rated it **"moderate utility"** in the original 10-technique review; a later
  quantitative meta-analysis of the same 10 techniques found **d = 0.56** for elaborative
  interrogation specifically. Works best when elaborations are **precise, self-generated,
  and the learner already has some prior knowledge** — weaker for learners with low prior
  knowledge, and better suited to surface/fact-level than deep conceptual understanding.
  Source: https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/education/articles/10.3389/feduc.2021.581216/full ;
  https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26173288/

- **QAR (Question–Answer Relationships, Raphael)** — a taxonomy (Right There / Think and
  Search / Author and You / On My Own) for teaching students to locate where an answer
  comes from. Widely used; **no independent large-scale effect-size figure was located in
  this search pass — [UNVERIFIED, needs dedicated follow-up search on "Raphael QAR
  effect size"]**.

### 1.5 Syntax / sentence-level comprehension

- **Park, Elleman, Magne, Pingitore & MacDonell (2026 — journal-listed, likely early-view
  from 2024/2025 work), "Effects of Syntactic Skill Interventions on Passage- and
  Sentence-Level Reading Comprehension in Pre-K–12 Students: A Meta-Analytic Review"** — 40
  studies/33 articles, 174 effect sizes, 15,784 students. **g = 0.17 passage-level
  comprehension, g = 0.15 sentence-level comprehension, g = 0.47 syntactic knowledge
  itself.** Interpretation: syntax instruction reliably builds syntactic knowledge but has
  only a **small** direct transfer to comprehension outcomes.
  Source: https://doi.org/10.3102/00346543261466253
- **Older sentence-combining meta-analysis** (Reading Research and Instruction, 31(1)) — 24
  qualifying studies; sentence-combining had a **greater effect at elementary level than
  high-school/college level**; in a broader ranking of 11 writing-instruction approaches,
  sentence-combining ranked **5th of 11** by weighted effect size.
  Source: https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/abs/10.1080/19388079109558072

### 1.6 Close reading of complex text vs. leveled/instructional-level text

- **Shanahan (ongoing blog series, "Shanahan on Literacy")** — argues against using
  "instructional level" leveled texts for **core, whole-group** instruction; students
  learn more, over time, from **grade-level complex text with heavy teacher scaffolding**
  than from texts pre-matched to their independent/instructional level, even though
  performance (fluency, immediate comprehension) on the complex text is initially worse.
  Explicit exception: **true beginning readers**, and for **struggling readers/students with
  disabilities** the recommendation is still to work mostly **above** the traditional
  instructional level, not at it — with mandatory scaffolding (read-aloud support,
  vocabulary pre-teaching, chunking, guided rereading).
  Source: https://www.shanahanonliteracy.com/blog/the-instructional-level-concept-revisited-teaching-with-complex-text ;
  https://www.educationnext.org/right-reading-level-or-right-reading-challenge/
  **CONTESTED** — this is Shanahan's strong personal reading of a mixed evidence base
  (ties to the "just right" vs. "productive struggle" debate); IES's own text-complexity
  guidance has been characterized by Shanahan himself as partly wrong (see "Who's Right
  About Text Complexity" post) — flag to the reader as a design *stance*, not settled law.

### 1.7 Writing-to-read

- **Graham & Hebert (2010/2011), "Writing to Read: A Meta-Analysis of the Impact of Writing
  and Writing Instruction on Reading," Carnegie Corp. report / Harvard Educational Review
  81(4), 710–744** — three converging findings: (1) **writing about text read** improves
  comprehension of that text; (2) **teaching writing** (e.g., sentence construction,
  paragraph/text-structure writing, the writing process) improves reading comprehension,
  fluency, and word reading; (3) **increasing how much students write** improves reading
  comprehension. Reported effect sizes: **writing about material read, ES ≈ 0.40; responding
  to text, ES ≈ 0.77; writing summaries, ES ≈ 0.52.**
  Source: https://media.carnegie.org/filer_public/9d/e2/9de20604-a055-42da-bc00-77da949b29d7/ccny_report_2010_writing.pdf ;
  https://www.harvardeducationalreview.org/content/81/4/710

### 1.8 Disciplinary literacy

- **Shanahan & Shanahan (2008), "Teaching Disciplinary Literacy to Adolescents: Rethinking
  Content-Area Literacy," Harvard Educational Review, 78(1), 40–59** — content experts
  (mathematicians, chemists, historians) read and evaluate texts in their field in
  **discipline-specific ways** that differ sharply from each other and from generic
  "content-area reading" strategies (e.g., historians corroborate/source documents;
  scientists read for reproducibility and interrogate methods; mathematicians read
  symbol-by-symbol, very slowly, often multiple passes). Implication: **generic strategies
  plateau around middle school** — advanced comprehension of subject matter requires
  discipline-specific reading moves, taught inside the discipline, not as a stand-alone
  "reading class."
  Source: https://www.nesacenter.org/uploaded/conferences/FLC/2015/Handouts/Shanahan_HER_2008.pdf

### 1.9 Critical reading / lateral reading / online civic reasoning

- **Stanford History Education Group (SHEG), 2016 pilot + Wineburg et al. (2019/2021)
  "Students' Civic Online Reasoning: A National Portrait"** — 2016: young people broadly
  lack basic digital-source evaluation skills. 2019 national assessment, **3,446 students**,
  demographically matched to the US HS population, 6 tasks. Findings: **52% judged a
  grainy, out-of-context video "strong evidence"** of US election fraud (it was filmed in
  Russia, unrelated); **~two-thirds could not distinguish a news story from labeled
  "Sponsored Content"**; **96% did not factor in a site's bias when judging credibility**.
  **Lateral reading** (leaving the page and opening new tabs to check who's behind a
  source, rather than staying on-page reading "vertically") is SHEG's trained
  countermeasure and transfers to college students in follow-up intervention studies
  (Harvard Kennedy School Misinformation Review).
  Source: https://stacks.stanford.edu/file/druid:cz440cm8408/Students'%20Civic%20Online%20Reasoning_2021.pdf ;
  https://news.stanford.edu/stories/2020/10/judging-fact-fiction-online ;
  https://misinforeview.hks.harvard.edu/article/lateral-reading-college-students-learn-to-critically-evaluate-internet-sources-in-an-online-course/

### 1.10 Reading volume, stamina, motivation

- **Allington (summarized across his work, e.g. 2014)** — reading volume ("time
  actually spent reading connected text") is one of the most consistently cited
  correlates of reading achievement and vocabulary growth; stamina (sustaining
  comprehension effort without scaffolding over longer stretches) is presented as a
  trainable, separate proficiency, not just a byproduct of skill.
  Source: https://textproject.org/wp-content/uploads/2022/07/Hiebert-2015.pdf ;
  https://education.scholastic.com/content/dam/education/resources/why-sustained-reading-matters_march-2026.pdf
  **Note: exact quantitative effect sizes for "volume → comprehension" causally (vs.
  correlationally) were not found in this pass — the causal direction (better readers read
  more vs. reading more makes better readers) is a known unresolved debate; treat volume
  claims as strongly correlational, weakly causal, unless later cross-checked.**

- **Guthrie, CORI (Concept-Oriented Reading Instruction)** — integrates reading/language
  arts with hands-on science inquiry + motivation supports (autonomy, collaboration,
  real-world interaction, strategy instruction). Early Guthrie et al. study: CORI students'
  **curiosity** motivation ES **≈ 1.94** vs. traditional classrooms (very large). Later
  11-study/75-effect-size meta-analytic synthesis: **reading comprehension ES ≈ 0.26** for
  CORI vs. traditional instruction — motivation gains are much larger than direct
  comprehension gains, and it's the **strategy-instruction component inside CORI**, not
  motivation alone, that drives the comprehension effect.
  Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Concept-Oriented_Reading_Instruction ;
  https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4264840/ ;
  https://ies.ed.gov/ncee/wwc/Docs/InterventionReports/wwc_cori_081010.pdf

### 1.11 Defining "advanced reader" — CEFR and PIAAC

- **CEFR (Council of Europe) reading descriptors:**
  - **C1** — "Can understand in detail lengthy, complex texts, whether or not they relate to
    his/her own area of speciality, provided he/she can reread difficult sections."
  - **C2** — "Can understand and interpret critically virtually all forms of written
    language including abstract, structurally complex, or highly colloquial texts... can
    understand a wide range of long and complex texts, appreciating subtle distinctions of
    style and implicit as well as explicit meaning."
  Source: Council of Europe self-assessment grid, https://europass.europa.eu/system/files/2020-05/CEFR%20self-assessment%20grid%20EN.pdf

- **PIAAC (OECD) literacy levels, Below 1 through 5:**
  - **Level 5 (376–500 score band)**: search/integrate information across **multiple,
    dense texts**; construct syntheses of similar/contrasting viewpoints; evaluate
    evidence-based arguments.
  - **Level 2**: "nearing proficiency," still struggling with text-based tasks.
  - US adult distribution (2017): **19% of US adults at Level 1 or below**; 79% at Level 2+.
  - Ireland 2023 distribution (fuller spread example): Level 5: 1%, Level 4: 8%,
    Level 3: 32%, Level 2: 38%, Level 1: 16%, Below Level 1: 5%.
  Source: https://nces.ed.gov/surveys/piaac/measure.asp ; https://nces.ed.gov/pubs2019/2019179/index.asp ; https://www.cso.ie/en/releasesandpublications/ep/p-piaac/programmefortheinternationalassessmentofadultcompetenciespiaac2023

## 2. KEEP (strong-to-moderate evidence, build the course on these)

1. **Teach a small set of comprehension strategies explicitly but briefly** — question
   generation, summarization, graphic organizers, monitoring — then move to **applied
   practice on real content**, not endless strategy drilling (NRP 2000; Willingham
   2006/2014; Okkinga 2018 shows real but small gains from whole-class strategy teaching).
2. **Build background/domain knowledge deliberately and cumulatively**, ideally through a
   coherent, spiraling knowledge sequence (Recht & Leslie 1988; Smith et al. 2021; Grissmer
   2023 Core Knowledge RCT; Tyner & Kabourek 2020 — reallocate some ELA-adjacent time to
   content subjects like social studies/science/history read as *text*, not lecture).
3. **Explicit text-structure instruction for expository/informational text** — g≈0.56
   overall, g≈0.96 for struggling readers (Hebert et al. 2016) — signal words, compare/
   contrast, cause/effect, problem/solution, sequence, description.
4. **Reciprocal-teaching-style dialogic reading routines** (predict/question/clarify/
   summarize) as a scaffolded small-group or whole-class practice, gradually released to
   independent use (Palincsar & Brown; Rosenshine & Meister 1994).
5. **Elaborative interrogation ("why" self-questioning)** as a lightweight technique once
   the learner has *some* prior knowledge of the topic — good for fact-dense
   nonfiction/history/science content (Dunlosky 2013; d≈0.56 meta-analysis).
6. **Write about what you read** — summaries, short responses, analytic paragraphs — as a
   comprehension-building activity in its own right, not just an assessment
   (Graham & Hebert 2010, ES 0.40–0.77 depending on task).
7. **Push learners into genuinely challenging (above-instructional-level) text with heavy
   scaffolding**, once decoding is solid — don't cap students at "just right" leveled texts
   forever (Shanahan's instructional-level argument) — but treat this as a directional
   design stance, not an uncontested finding.
8. **Explicit lateral-reading / source-evaluation instruction** for any learner who will
   read on the open internet — SHEG's national data show this is not intuitive and must be
   directly taught (Wineburg et al. 2019/2021).
9. **Discipline-specific reading moves for advanced/adult learners** — once general strategy
   instruction plateaus (roughly middle-school+), move to teaching how historians, scientists,
   and other domains actually read their own texts (Shanahan & Shanahan 2008).
10. **Protect real reading volume/time** — stamina-building through sustained independent
    reading of self-selected, appropriately-challenging texts, escalating duration over the
    course (Allington-line-of-research), while being honest that causality here is not
    fully nailed down.

## 3. DROP / AVOID

- **Do not treat comprehension "strategies" as a skill hierarchy to be drilled for months.**
  Willingham's argument, and the modest Okkinga effect sizes even under ideal
  whole-classroom conditions, argue against a strategies-heavy scope-and-sequence that
  consumes large amounts of course time. Teach each strategy briefly (a lesson or two),
  model it, then get out of the way and put reps into real content.
- **Do not rely on "content-area reading strategies" (generic SQ3R/skim-scan type
  approaches) as the ceiling for advanced readers.** Shanahan & Shanahan's disciplinary
  literacy work shows these generic strategies stop differentiating good from struggling
  advanced readers; the course needs discipline-specific reading modules at the top end,
  not a bigger pile of generic strategy worksheets.
- **Do not use leveled/instructional-level texts as the sole diet for core, whole-class
  instruction**, per Shanahan — but this is a stance to apply carefully (see CONTESTED
  below), and never for true beginning readers.
- **Do not oversell the baseball study (Recht & Leslie 1988) as "knowledge fully replaces
  skill."** It's a small, single-topic, dated study now under critical re-examination
  (Reynolds 2024/2025) — use it as an illustrative anecdote, not a load-bearing citation.
- **Do not assume reading-strategy or motivation gains (e.g., CORI's very large curiosity
  ES of ~1.94) translate one-to-one into comprehension gains** — CORI's comprehension ES
  is a much smaller ~0.26; motivation is necessary but not sufficient.

## 4. CONTESTED

- **Instructional level vs. complex-text-with-scaffolding (Shanahan) vs. mainstream
  "just right book" guidance.** Real, ongoing debate in the field; Shanahan is a strong,
  well-cited voice but not the only one, and IES's own past guidance has shifted. The
  course should state this as a *design choice with a rationale*, and be explicit to
  parents/teachers that it's contested, especially for the youngest and most struggling
  readers.
- **Reading volume/stamina as *causal* driver of comprehension gains** vs. better
  comprehenders simply choosing to read more (reverse causation / Matthew effects). Treat
  volume-building as good practice regardless, but don't claim a settled causal effect size.
- **Recht & Leslie's baseball study generalizability** — see above; treated by some
  (Reynolds 2024/2025) as a flawed or over-extended flagship citation for the
  "knowledge > skill" claim.
- **Whether whole-class strategy instruction (Okkinga's small standardized-test effect,
  d=0.186) is "worth it" at scale** vs. spending that time on knowledge-building content —
  the literature doesn't give a clean verdict on the optimal time split; the course takes
  a position (brief strategy teaching + heavy knowledge-building) but should flag this as
  a judgment call, not settled science.
- **Elleman & Compton (2017) specific numbers** — flagged UNVERIFIED above; don't publish a
  specific effect size from this citation until the primary source is directly confirmed.

## 5. Concrete Design Rules for the Course (with level exits mapped)

**General architecture:** comprehension/critical-reading strand runs in parallel with
decoding/fluency/vocabulary strands (see other numbered research files) from early
intermediate onward, and becomes the *dominant* strand from upper-intermediate through
advanced.

1. **Strategy dosage rule:** each of the ~6 core strategies (question generation,
   summarizing, monitoring, graphic organizers, predicting, clarifying/inference) gets a
   short explicit-teaching block (a handful of lessons, not units), modeled with think-alouds,
   then folded into ongoing content reading. Never more than ~10% of comprehension-strand
   time on strategy instruction per level once introduced.
2. **Knowledge-building spine:** every level after early-beginner includes a
   content-knowledge track (history/science/civics/arts in age-appropriate form) that is
   read as text, sequenced to build on itself across levels (Core-Knowledge-style spiraling),
   because knowledge is the biggest lever for the ceiling levels.
3. **Text-structure unit, mandatory, once per structure type** (sequence, cause/effect,
   compare/contrast, problem/solution, description) with explicit signal-word instruction —
   placed at the upper-elementary-equivalent level and refreshed once at the
   lower-secondary-equivalent level for nonfiction-heavy tracks.
4. **Reciprocal-teaching routine** as the default "how we read together" protocol for
   any shared/close-reading text from upper-beginner level onward, gradually shifting
   facilitation from course/teacher-modeled to learner-led.
5. **Writing-to-read requirement**: every extended reading passage from intermediate level
   up ends in a short written response/summary — non-negotiable, per Graham & Hebert's
   effect sizes.
6. **Text complexity ramp**: place learners in text slightly above comfortable
   independent level with scaffolding (glossing, pre-teaching, chunking, guided rereading)
   rather than perpetually "just right" text — with an explicit carve-out for true
   beginning readers and heavier scaffolding requirement for struggling readers (never
   *below* what Shanahan calls instructional level for beginners).
7. **Disciplinary-literacy module at advanced level**: once a learner exits
   upper-intermediate, add discipline-specific reading modules (how to read a history
   source, a science paper/abstract, an argumentative essay, a legal or policy document,
   basic data/statistics in text) rather than more generic strategy content.
8. **Critical/lateral reading module, mandatory at intermediate level and above**:
   explicit instruction in checking who's behind a source, opening new tabs to verify
   (lateral reading), and recognizing sponsored/persuasive content — modeled directly on
   SHEG's assessment tasks and findings.
9. **Reading volume target, escalating by level**: build in protected independent reading
   time with self-selected, level-appropriate texts, with duration/stamina targets rising
   level over level (e.g., 10 min → 40+ min sustained silent reading by advanced level).
10. **Motivation design**: borrow CORI's structural levers — real-world/inquiry framing,
    learner autonomy in text choice within a topic, collaborative discussion — rather than
    relying on gamification alone; expect the payoff mainly in engagement/volume, with
    comprehension gains coming through the strategy + knowledge components CORI bundles in.

### Level exits — a real finish line

Map course levels to CEFR reading descriptors AND PIAAC-adjacent tasks so both
school-age/ESL learners (CEFR frame) and adult/functional-literacy learners (PIAAC frame)
have a recognizable exit target:

| Course level | CEFR reading anchor | PIAAC-style task anchor |
|---|---|---|
| Beginner | A1–A2: short, simple, concrete texts | Below Level 1 – Level 1: locate a single piece of explicit information in short text |
| Intermediate | B1–B2: main points of clear text on familiar/some unfamiliar topics; some argument-following | Level 2: compare/contrast explicit information, low-level inference |
| Upper-intermediate | B2–C1: detailed understanding of longer, more complex texts, some rereading needed | Level 3: integrate info across paragraphs, some competing information |
| Advanced | C1–C2: critically interpret complex, abstract, structurally dense texts incl. implicit meaning; discipline-specific reading | Level 4–5: search/integrate/evaluate across multiple dense texts, synthesize contrasting viewpoints, evaluate evidence-based arguments |

Advanced/"finished" reader = reliably performing at **CEFR C1 (stretch: C2 for
academic/professional tracks)** and **PIAAC Level 4–5** tasks: can independently read
long, structurally complex, discipline-specific or persuasive text; can evaluate an
argument's evidence; can synthesize across multiple sources; can lateral-read to check a
source's credibility without being told to.

## 6. Implications for Older/Adult/ESL Learners

- **Background knowledge is not blank-slate for adults/ESL learners** — many adult and
  ESL learners bring rich real-world/L1-transferable knowledge that should be leveraged
  explicitly (the Recht & Leslie finding — high topic knowledge compensating for lower
  "reading skill" — is arguably *more* directly applicable to adult ESL learners with
  strong domain expertise in their L1/profession than to children). Course should let
  adult learners choose content-knowledge tracks matched to their existing expertise
  (e.g., an adult ESL learner who's a nurse reads health-domain content first).
- **Strategy instruction should be even briefer for adults** — adult learners typically
  already have functioning comprehension strategies in L1; the task is often transfer
  and vocabulary/syntax in L2, not teaching what a "strategy" is from scratch. Lean
  harder on explicit syntax/sentence-level instruction (see §1.5) for ESL learners, since
  the small-but-real syntactic-knowledge gains (g=0.47) matter more when the learner is
  also acquiring L2 grammar.
- **PIAAC framing matters more than CEFR for adult native-speaker learners** (e.g., adult
  literacy learners who are L1 speakers but read at PIAAC Level 1) — for this population,
  frame the course's exit goals in PIAAC functional-task language (job applications,
  medication labels, workplace documents, news evaluation) rather than CEFR's L2-acquisition
  frame.
- **Disciplinary literacy is highly relevant for working adults** — adapt the "how
  historians/scientists read" model into "how to read in your field" modules (a lawyer, a
  nurse, an engineer, a tradesperson each read differently) — this is a natural adult-track
  extension of Shanahan & Shanahan's core finding.
- **Critical/lateral reading is arguably MORE urgent for adult learners** than for the
  students SHEG studied — adults vote, make health and financial decisions, and are
  targeted by scams/misinformation; put the lateral-reading module earlier in the adult
  track than in the youth track.
- **Reading volume/stamina building must respect adult time constraints** — frame as
  short, protected daily sessions (10–20 min) rather than assuming free classroom time;
  self-selected content (news, workplace materials, hobbies) matters more for adult
  motivation than for children, where CORI's inquiry-science framing was used.
- **Writing-to-read still applies** — even a two-sentence written reaction to what an
  adult learner read strengthens retention/comprehension per Graham & Hebert; keep this
  requirement lightweight for time-constrained adult learners rather than dropping it.
