# 04 — Adult, Adolescent & ESL/EFL Reading Instruction Research

Scope: teaching reading to adolescents, adults, and English learners (ESL/EFL), with
specific attention to Urdu-L1/South Asian learners, for a single course usable by
absolute-beginner-to-advanced learners of any age.

---

## 1. Key Findings (citation + URL + numbers)

**Adult literacy — scale of the problem**
- NRC (National Research Council/National Academies), *Improving Adult Literacy
  Instruction: Options for Practice and Research* (2012): cites survey data that
  **more than 90 million adults in the US** lack literacy skills for fully
  productive/secure lives; identifies 4 systemic issues — variability in adult
  learner profiles, variability of instructor prep, fragmented program types, and
  weak supports for persistence outside class. Research base for *adult-specific*
  reading instruction is thin — the report's central recommendation is more
  systematic research, not a single method.
  https://nap.nationalacademies.org/read/13242/chapter/11
  https://www.nationalacademies.org/projects/CFEX-Q-08-03-A/publication/13242

**Kruidenier (2002), *Research-Based Principles for Adult Basic Education Reading
Instruction* (NCSALL/RMC Research)**
- Organizes adult reading instruction around the same four components as child
  reading research: alphabetics (phonemic awareness + phonics/word ID), fluency,
  vocabulary, comprehension — but stresses assessing each adult's individual
  *profile* across these four rather than assuming grade-level norms apply.
  https://lincs.ed.gov/publications/pdf/adult_ed_02.pdf

**STAR — Student Achievement in Reading (LINCS/OCTAE, since 2001)**
- National PD initiative built directly on Kruidenier's four components
  (alphabetics, fluency, vocabulary, comprehension). Targeted at **intermediate
  adult learners (grade-level equivalent 4.0–8.9)**. Explicit finding used to
  design instruction: **emerging adult readers need proportionally more
  alphabetics + fluency work; advanced adult readers need proportionally more
  vocabulary + comprehension work** — same four components, different weighting
  by level, not different content.
  https://lincs.ed.gov/state-resources/federal-initiatives/student-achievement-reading

**IES Practice Guide (2022), *Providing Reading Interventions for Students in
Grades 4–9*** (What Works Clearinghouse #29)
- 4 recommendations: (1) build decoding of complex multisyllabic words; (2)
  purposeful fluency-building; (3) a routine set of comprehension practices
  (build world/word knowledge, ask-and-answer questions, "gist" routine for
  short text sections, comprehension monitoring); (4) practice with "stretch
  text" — challenging text above independent level, scaffolded.
  https://ies.ed.gov/ncee/wwc/practiceguide/29

**IES Practice Guide (2008), *Improving Adolescent Literacy: Effective Classroom
and Intervention Practices*** (WWC #8)
- 5 recommendations: explicit vocabulary instruction; direct/explicit
  comprehension-strategy instruction; extended discussion of text meaning;
  increase motivation/engagement; intensive individualized intervention for
  struggling readers from trained specialists.
  https://ies.ed.gov/ncee/wwc/docs/practiceguide/adlit_pg_082608.pdf

**IES Practice Guide (2014), *Teaching Academic Content and Literacy to English
Learners in Elementary and Middle School*** (WWC #19, NCEE 2014-4012)
- 4 recommendations: (1) teach a set of academic vocabulary words intensively
  across several days with varied activities; (2) integrate oral+written
  English instruction into content teaching (not a separate bolt-on); (3)
  regular structured opportunities for writing practice; (4) small-group
  intervention for struggling ELs.
  https://ies.ed.gov/ncee/wwc/practiceguide/19

**August & Shanahan (2006), National Literacy Panel on Language-Minority
Children and Youth, *Developing Literacy in Second-Language Learners***
- Synthesis of 200+ studies. Five themes: L2 literacy development, **cross-linguistic
  relationships**, sociocultural context, instruction/PD, assessment. Core
  finding used across the field: **phonological awareness is not strictly
  language-specific — it transfers across L1/L2 and predicts word recognition
  cross-linguistically** ("linguistic interdependence" — Cummins-style transfer
  holds up empirically here).
  https://www.cal.org/projects/national-literacy-panel/

**Nation's vocabulary coverage thresholds (Nation 2001/2006; corpus studies since)**
- **95% text coverage** = threshold for *minimally acceptable* comprehension;
  reachable with **~4,000–5,000 word families** (78% from the first 1,000 words +
  8% next 1,000 + rest + proper nouns).
- **98% coverage** = threshold where *most* learners get adequate unassisted
  comprehension; requires **~8,000 word families**.
- Design implication: a graded/leveled-reader pipeline should be built and
  labeled by word-family count against these two thresholds, not vague
  "beginner/intermediate/advanced" labels.
  https://www.researchgate.net/figure/The-Vocabulary-Size-Needed-to-Reach-95-and-98-Coverage-Plus-1-6-Proper-Nouns-of_tbl1_348932705

**Extensive reading / graded readers (Nation & Waring; Liu & Zhang 2018 meta-analysis)**
- Meta-analysis (21 studies, Liu & Zhang 2018): extensive-reading learners
  outperform pre-test and control groups on vocabulary measures.
- Effects are **larger for shorter programs** (optimal ≈ one semester, <3 months),
  **when graded readers specifically are used** (vs. authentic unleveled text),
  and **when learners answer comprehension questions** about what they read.
- Single-book exposure gives minimal retention — vocabulary gains require
  **repeated encounters across a collection of graded texts at intervals**
  (Nation & Waring, 2020) — this is the strongest single argument for a large
  leveled-reader library over a handful of "flagship" readers.
  https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ1179114
  http://www2.hawaii.edu/~readfl/rfl/October2003/waring/waring.pdf

**Jolly Phonics in Africa/Asia (Kenya being the best-documented case)**
- Kilifi County, Kenya: pilots 2015→2017→2018 (Pwani University partnership,
  30+ schools, 800+ pre-primary pupils) → pupils using Jolly Phonics scored
  **significantly higher across all three assessments** than controls → county
  adopted it system-wide in 2019 across 680+ public pre-primary schools.
  Another small study: 13/15 pupils in an experimental group gained 4–8 months
  of reading age vs. no significant control-group gain (small-n, treat as
  suggestive not definitive).
  https://www.jollyfutures.org/project/kenya
  No India-specific RCT was found in this search — mark **UNVERIFIED for India**
  specifically; Jolly Phonics is widely adopted in Indian private/low-cost
  schools but the search returned no RCT evidence, only descriptive/practitioner
  pieces.

**Pratham "Teaching at the Right Level" (TaRL)**
- J-PAL: **6 randomized evaluations across 7 Indian states**, test-score gains
  **0.07–0.70 SD** — among the largest effect sizes in the education RCT
  literature. Reading-specific: Uttar Pradesh learning camps **doubled** the
  number of children who could read a paragraph/story; language gains of
  **0.15 SD (Haryana)** to **0.70 SD (Uttar Pradesh)**.
  Mechanism directly relevant to this course: **group/assess by demonstrated
  skill level, not age or grade** — the core TaRL method — scaled to 76M+
  students via government partnership.
  https://www.povertyactionlab.org/evidence-effect/teaching-at-the-right-level
  https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w22746/w22746.pdf

**ASER Pakistan 2023 (Annual Status of Education Report)**
- 210,000 children assessed (151 rural + 123 urban districts, ~90,000 households).
- **50%** of surveyed children could read a story in Urdu (grade-2-level text).
- **54%** could read a sentence in English.
- Grade-5 students on average show literacy/numeracy at Grade-3 level.
- Girls consistently score below boys, and the gap has been *widening*, not
  closing, across survey rounds cited (e.g., Sindhi-story reading: 22%/25%
  boys→girls in 2019 vs 19%/15% in 2023 in the specific comparison surfaced).
  https://aserpakistan.org/ (home) — provisional 2023 national report via
  https://www.scribd.com/document/726358201/aser-national-2023
  **Note:** treat exact percentages as provisional/report-specific; verify
  against the final ASER-Pakistan 2023 PDF before quoting in course marketing.

**Urdu-L1 → English-L2 script/orthography research**
- Urdu = Nastaliq script, right-to-left, **abjad** (consonant-based); with
  diacritics present it is a *shallow/transparent* orthography (near 1:1
  letter–sound); diacritics are frequently omitted in everyday print, which
  makes fluent Urdu reading partly context/vocabulary-dependent rather than
  purely decoding-dependent — a genuinely different skill from English's
  deep orthography.
- Study of 76 Pakistan-based + 50 Canada-based Urdu–English bilinguals: L1 and
  L2 phonological awareness correlate — supports Cummins' linguistic
  interdependence hypothesis (transfers, doesn't have to be re-taught from
  zero) but transfer of *specific* skills should be checked "case-by-case" per
  language pair, not assumed wholesale.
  https://doi.org/10.3390/languages8010086
  https://scholars.wlu.ca/etd/1660/

**Pakistani-English phonological interference (specific to this course's target
learner)**
- Documented substitutions: **/θ/ and /ð/** (English "th") realized as
  aspirated **/tʰ/** and **/d/** by Urdu-L1 speakers (interdentals don't exist
  in Urdu).
  https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/EJ1066577.pdf
- **/v/ vs /w/** confusion: Urdu's و (waw) covers both English /v/ and /w/
  territory, so minimal-pair confusion (e.g., "vet"/"wet", "vine"/"wine") is a
  documented, specific, teachable confusion — not vague "accent," a precise
  phoneme-mapping gap.
  https://www.academia.edu/122618011/
- Vowel quality: L1 vowel-system interference documented broadly (Pashto study
  is the most detailed available, generalizes directionally to other
  Pakistani L1s): learners fail to control acoustic vowel quality
  native-like because their L1 vowel inventory doesn't map 1:1 onto English's
  (English has more short-vowel contrasts than most Pakistani L1s).
  https://www.academia.edu/35868021/

**Adult motivation, shame, persistence**
- NPR (2018) + NRC 2012 + dispositional-factors research agree: **stigma is a
  primary barrier to adults even enrolling**, and many adults invest
  significant effort *hiding* low literacy rather than seeking help.
- Persistence in ABE/ASE programs is the single hardest design problem — skill
  gains require "many thousands of hours," and situational/programmatic
  barriers (childcare, lost wages, shame about returning after a gap) dominate
  over motivation deficits.
- Design implication directly usable: **build a course that has zero penalty
  for pausing and re-entering**, and that never puts an adult in visibly
  "kid-labeled" material.
  https://www.npr.org/sections/ed/2018/04/26/602797769/casting-aside-shame-and-stigma-adults-tackle-struggles-with-literacy
  https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3668796/

## 2. KEEP (strong evidence, reusable as course design rules)

- **Four-component model (alphabetics/decoding, fluency, vocabulary,
  comprehension) as the skeleton for every level, adult or child** — Kruidenier
  2002, STAR, both IES guides all converge on this. Strongest, most-replicated
  organizing principle in the whole space.
- **Nation's 95%/98% coverage thresholds** to grade texts and set vocabulary
  targets — quantitative, replicated, directly usable as an engineering spec
  for the leveled-reader pipeline.
- **Extensive reading via a large pool of graded readers with comprehension
  checks**, short cycles (~1 semester), not single "big books" — meta-analytic
  support (Liu & Zhang 2018), plus Nation & Waring's repeated-encounter logic.
- **Group/placement by demonstrated skill level, not age or grade (TaRL)** —
  the single best-evidenced mechanism (0.07–0.70 SD across 6 RCTs) for making
  one system work across ages; this is the direct answer to "how does the same
  material work for a 6-year-old and a 40-year-old."
- **Explicit, direct, multi-day vocabulary instruction with varied activities**
  (IES EL guide) — not incidental-only exposure for beginners.
- **Comprehension monitoring + gist routines + stretch-text practice** (2022
  IES guide) for grades 4–9 equivalent readers — directly transferable to
  adult intermediate learners per STAR's own leveling.
- **Cross-linguistic phonological transfer is real** (August & Shanahan; Urdu
  studies) — a course can explicitly *leverage* Urdu-L1 phonological awareness
  rather than treating ESL learners as blank slates.
- **No stigma / no visible age-labeling; flexible re-entry** — directly
  evidenced adult-persistence design rule.

## 3. DROP / AVOID

- **Assuming adult beginners are like child beginners minus vocabulary.** NRC
  2012 explicitly flags that adult profiles vary far more than child cohorts
  (life experience, partial literacy in another language, undiagnosed learning
  differences, irregular attendance) — a single "adult track" mirroring a kids'
  track 1:1 will misfire.
- **Single-exposure / one-graded-reader vocabulary strategies.** Evidence shows
  minimal retention from one book; don't ship "read this one story and you've
  learned the words."
- **Treating Jolly-Phonics-style synthetic phonics as sufficient on its own for
  older beginners.** The strongest evidence for it is in young/pre-primary
  cohorts (Kenya); for adolescents/adults it should be one component inside the
  four-component model, not the whole method, per STAR's explicit rebalancing
  by level.
- **Kid-coded visual/tonal register for adult-track materials** — direct
  violation of the persistence/stigma evidence.
- **Assuming Urdu-L1 abjad literacy = zero transfer to English.** The
  phonological-awareness transfer finding (August & Shanahan; Urdu bilingual
  studies) says the opposite — don't build the Urdu-L1 track as if starting
  from nothing.

## 4. CONTESTED / needs more evidence before relying on it

- **India-specific Jolly-Phonics RCT evidence** — not found in this search
  despite wide adoption; the Kenya evidence is solid but generalizing it to
  South Asian classrooms/L1s is currently an assumption, not a citation. Mark
  unverified until a dedicated South Asia search is done.
- **Exact ASER-Pakistan 2023 percentages** — sourced through a provisional/
  Scribd mirror in this pass, not the primary PDF; treat the 50%/54% figures
  as directionally right but re-verify against aserpakistan.org's final PDF
  before quoting externally.
- **How much of TaRL's effect is the level-grouping mechanism vs. the specific
  pedagogy/materials Pratham built alongside it** — J-PAL's writeups don't
  fully decompose this; for course design we're borrowing the *grouping*
  principle, which is the least contested part, and being more cautious about
  copying Pratham's specific activity types wholesale.
- **Whether phonological-awareness transfer (Urdu↔English) is strong enough to
  let a course skip a dedicated foundational-sounds unit for Urdu-L1 adults**,
  vs. still needing explicit contrastive phonics for known trouble sounds
  (/θ/,/ð/,/v/-/w/, short vowels). Current evidence supports "transfer exists"
  but not "transfer is sufficient" — treat the contrastive-phonics unit as
  still required, transfer as a speed multiplier, not a replacement.

## 5. Concrete design rules for one course, all ages, ESL included

1. **Placement, not age-gating.** Entry test measures the same four components
   (decoding, fluency, vocabulary size in word-families, comprehension) used
   throughout the course. Route by score into a level, never by birthdate —
   direct application of TaRL's evidenced mechanism.
2. **Track split at the register layer, not the content layer.** Same
   decoding/vocabulary sequence for a 6-year-old and a 40-year-old; only the
   *carrier text and illustration style* differ ("adult register" vs.
   "child register" reader sets covering identical word lists/skill points).
   This directly answers the brief's "same material, two registers" requirement
   and is consistent with STAR's "same four components, different weighting."
3. **Grade every text against Nation's 95%/98% thresholds**, tagging each
   level's target word-family count (roughly: L1 ≈ 500–1,000 families, L2 ≈
   2,000–2,500, L3 ≈ 4,000–5,000 [95% threshold / "functional reading"], L4 ≈
   6,000–8,000 [98% threshold / "fluent independent reading"]).
4. **Build a large leveled-reader library per level (extensive-reading pool),
   not a handful of flagship stories** — target enough titles per level for
   multiple repeated encounters with the target word list, each with 3–5
   comprehension-check questions (both evidenced boosters from the ER
   meta-analysis).
5. **No visible child-coded material in the adult track.** Same skill
   sequence, adult-life-context sentences/topics (money, work, family, health,
   news) instead of animals/toys — direct application of the shame/stigma
   evidence.
6. **Zero-penalty pause/resume.** Progress must persist indefinitely with no
   "restart from unit 1" penalty and no visible streak-shaming — direct
   application of the persistence-barriers evidence (irregular attendance is
   normal for adults, not a failure state).
7. **Pacing: self-paced, not time-boxed**, because adult vs. child time
   budgets differ by an order of magnitude (STAR's target population attends
   inconsistently); "skip if you already know this" must be available at the
   sub-skill level, not just at the unit level, so an educated adult with a
   partial-literacy gap doesn't have to sit through content they already know.
8. **Explicit skip-ahead via diagnostic checkpoints every level**, not just at
   entry — re-running the placement instrument periodically catches adults
   who accelerate past their initial placement (again: TaRL logic, applied
   continuously not just once).
9. **Vocabulary teaching = explicit + multi-day + varied activities** for new
   target words (IES EL guide recommendation 1), reserving pure incidental
   exposure for extensive-reading time, not core instruction.
10. **Comprehension layer**: teach the explicit routines from the 2022 IES
    grades 4–9 guide (gist-of-short-section, ask/answer questions, monitor
    understanding) starting as soon as a learner is decoding at ~L2, and use
    "stretch text" (slightly above independent level, scaffolded) rather than
    only within-level text, once fluency is established.

## 6. Specific notes for Urdu-L1 / South Asian learners

- **Leverage, don't ignore, L1 phonological awareness.** Cross-linguistic
  transfer is empirically supported (August & Shanahan 2006; Urdu-English
  bilingual studies) — course can explicitly bridge ("you already know how to
  break words into sounds in Urdu; here's how English does it differently")
  rather than starting phonics instruction as if from zero.
- **Direction and script are a genuine, separate hurdle** — Urdu is RTL abjad
  (Nastaliq), English is LTR alphabetic with vowels always written. Do not
  conflate "already literate in Urdu" with "ready for English decoding" — a
  short explicit unit on direction-of-reading and "English always writes its
  vowels, unlike everyday Urdu print" is warranted before phonics begins.
- **Build a contrastive-phonics unit targeting the four documented trouble
  spots**, not generic phonics:
  - **/θ/ /ð/ ("th")** — Urdu-L1 speakers default to /tʰ/ and /d/; needs
    explicit tongue-position drilling, minimal pairs (think/tink, this/dis).
  - **/v/ vs /w/** — Urdu و covers both; minimal-pair drills (vet/wet,
    vine/wine, van/wan) needed specifically, not assumed self-correcting.
  - **Short vowel contrasts** — English has more short-vowel distinctions than
    most Pakistani L1 vowel inventories (documented in detail for Pashto,
    directionally true for Urdu/Punjabi/Sindhi speakers too); minimal-pair
    drills (bit/bet/bat, full/fool) before relying on spelling patterns alone.
  - Retroflex/aspirated consonants Urdu has that English lacks are *not* the
    problem direction — the risk is the reverse (missing English-only sounds),
    so the trouble list above is the actual priority, not a generic "accent
    reduction" unit.
- **Diacritics-optional print habit**: because everyday Urdu is usually
  printed *without* short-vowel diacritics, Urdu-L1 readers are used to
  inferring vowels from context/vocabulary rather than decoding them letter
  by letter. English requires the opposite habit (vowels are always written
  and must be decoded) — flag this explicitly as a mindset shift, not just a
  phonics gap.
- **ASER Pakistan 2023 numbers (50% reading Urdu story, 54% reading English
  sentence, Grade-5 performing at Grade-3 level, girls behind boys and gap
  widening)** — implies the course's Pakistan-facing entry point must assume
  **weak L1 (Urdu) literacy is common, not an edge case**; a course that
  requires strong Urdu literacy as a prerequisite for the English bridge unit
  will exclude a large share of the real target population. Consider an
  Urdu-literacy floor-check at intake, with a remedial Urdu-literacy path
  before or alongside the English bridge unit for learners below it.
- **Gender gap**: ASER 2023 shows girls consistently behind boys and the gap
  widening — course marketing/access design (timing, mahram/chaperone norms,
  device access) should be treated as a distribution problem as much as a
  pedagogy problem for this audience; out of scope for the reading-pedagogy
  research itself but flagged for the delivery-design workstream.
