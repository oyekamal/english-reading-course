# Orton-Gillingham & Structured Literacy — Research Brief

Scope: OG principles, the Stevens et al. (2021) meta-analysis controversy, IDA Structured
Literacy definition, multisensory (VAK) evidence, commercial OG-derived programs, syllable
types & division rules, spelling rules, morphology, intervention intensity/dosage, signs of
dyslexia, and implications for a free self-paced course for absolute-beginner → advanced
readers of any age, including adult/ESL learners in Pakistan.

---

## 1. Key Findings (citation + numbers)

1. **IDA Structured Literacy definition** — Structured Literacy is IDA's umbrella term for
   evidence-based programs aligned to the Knowledge and Practice Standards. It names six
   content elements: phonology, sound-symbol association, syllables, morphology, syntax,
   semantics — taught with three instructional principles: systematic & cumulative, explicit,
   diagnostic (continuous assessment driving individualized instruction). Source: IDA,
   "Structured Literacy: Effective Instruction for Students with Dyslexia and Related Reading
   Difficulties," https://dyslexiaida.org/structured-literacy-effective-instruction-for-students-with-dyslexia-and-related-reading-difficulties/

2. **Stevens, Austin, Moore, Scammacca, Boucher & Vaughn (2021)** meta-analysis, *Exceptional
   Children* — pooled OG-based interventions for students with/at risk of word-level reading
   disability. Foundational-skill outcomes (phonological awareness, phonics, fluency, spelling):
   mean effect size **g = 0.22, p = .40 (not statistically significant)**. Vocabulary/
   comprehension outcomes: **g = 0.14, p = .59 (not significant)**. Effect sizes were positive
   in direction but did not reach significance, and the authors call for more, larger, more
   rigorous RCTs. Sources: https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/0014402921993406 ;
   https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/EJ1301083.pdf

3. **What this does/doesn't mean** — "not significant" ≠ "proven ineffective." The
   meta-analysis had few qualifying studies (small samples inflate/deflate estimated effects),
   did not separate "branded" vs. "generic OG" implementations, and could not isolate which
   component (multisensory vs. explicit/systematic/cumulative structure) drove any effect.
   Commentary by Solari, Petscher & Hall (2021), "What Does Science Say About
   Orton-Gillingham Interventions? An Explanation and Commentary on the Stevens et al. (2021)
   Meta-Analysis," makes exactly this caveat. Sources:
   https://www.researchgate.net/publication/350476776 ;
   https://reacheveryreader.gse.harvard.edu/what-does-science-say-about-orton-gillingham-interventions/ ;
   https://www.thereadingleague.org/wp-content/uploads/2021/05/Article-for-May-2021-TRLJ.pdf

4. **Multisensory (VAK/VAKT) component — no isolated evidence of added value.** No published
   study has isolated the multisensory element of OG from its explicit/systematic/cumulative
   scaffold to test it alone. A 2022 meta-analysis on multisensory instruction for
   children with/at-risk-for dyslexia found no consistent added benefit over explicit,
   systematic instruction when time-on-task is held constant; tactile/kinesthetic elements
   may raise attention/motivation but don't reliably outperform explicit phonics alone.
   Sources: https://repository.library.northeastern.edu/files/neu:rx915f81f/fulltext.pdf ;
   https://www.reallygreatreading.com/sites/default/files/2025-01/rgr_research_on_multisensory_instruction.pdf ;
   https://www.collaborativeclassroom.org/blog/multisensory-instruction-what-is-it-and-should-i-bother/

5. **Wanzek et al. (2013), extensive reading interventions for students with reading
   difficulties after grade 3** — synthesized intervention research for older
   (grade 4–12) struggling readers. Mean effect sizes across comprehension, word reading,
   word-reading fluency, reading fluency and spelling outcomes ranged **g = 0.10–0.16** —
   markedly smaller than effects typically found for K–3 early intervention. **Group size was
   a significant moderator of comprehension outcomes** — smallest groups (1:1 or very small
   group) had the largest mean effects; duration alone (more calendar weeks) was not
   sufficient without matched intensity/small group size. Source:
   https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.3102/0034654313477212 ; companion piece: Wanzek et al.
   (2018) "Current Evidence on the Effects of Intensive Early Reading Interventions,"
   https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/abs/10.1177/0022219418775110

6. **Commercial OG-derived program scopes:**
   - **Wilson Reading System (WRS)** — 12 Steps (not grade-linked), 52 substeps, 10-part
     lesson plan (phonemic awareness → phonics/word study → encoding → sight words →
     fluency → vocabulary → comprehension), explicitly organized around the six syllable
     types, moves from phonological to orthographic focus. Source:
     https://www.wilsonlanguage.com/knowledge-library/programs/wilson-reading-system/wilson-reading-system-curriculum/
   - **Barton Reading & Spelling System** — 10 levels, 10–15 lessons each: (1) phonemic
     awareness, (2) consonants & short vowels, (3) closed syllables & units, (4) syllable
     division & vowel teams, (5) prefixes/suffixes, (6) six reasons for silent-e, (7)
     vowel-r syllables, (8) advanced vowel teams, (9) foreign-language influences/spelling,
     (10) Greek/Latin roots. Completing all 10 levels is claimed to reach ~9th-grade
     (adult-level) reading; at 2x/week, 1hr sessions, each level (from L3 on) takes ~3–5
     months. Source: https://homeschoolacademics.org/the-10-levels-of-the-barton-reading-and-spelling-system/ ; https://bartonreading.com/levels/
   - **Lindamood-Bell LiPS** — targets phonemic awareness/oral-motor feedback
     (articulatory awareness of how sounds are produced) as the base skill beneath
     decoding/spelling/speech; PreK–12, ~80–120 lessons, 30–45 min (Tier 2) to 60–120 min
     (Tier 3) sessions. Source: https://cdn.lindamoodbell.com/wp-content/uploads/2024/09/LB4S-0824-FL-LiPS-Fact-Sheet-digital.pdf
   - **SPIRE** — packaged OG-based intervention bundling lesson scripts, decodable readers,
     manipulatives, and assessments; positioned as approachable for both new and experienced
     teachers. Source: https://www.illuminateedtherapy.com/resources/reading-intervention-programs-compared
   - **Sonday System** — OG-aligned; introduces most-common sounds first, spirals concepts,
     3 tiers of small-group intervention plus whole-class structured-literacy lesson plans.
     Source: https://www.imaginelearning.com/wp-content/uploads/2024/12/SON-Reading-Research-and-the-Sonday-System-Foundations-Paper.pdf
   - **Logic of English / Spalding (Writing Road to Reading)** — not deeply sourced in this
     pass (search returned no program-structure detail for Logic of English specifically) —
     **UNVERIFIED, flag for follow-up**; both are widely cited as spelling-rule-driven,
     phonogram-based OG-descendants (70 phonograms in Spalding tradition) but I did not pull
     a primary source confirming exact structure in this session.

7. **Six/seven syllable types** — closed, open, vowel-consonant-e (VCe), vowel team,
   r-controlled, consonant-le (the "6 types"); some programs (e.g., WRS) add a 7th
   ("odd/schwa" or "unaccented final schwa" syllable) as a variant. Definitions confirmed
   across multiple OG-aligned sources (Voyager Sopris, Reading Rockets, Reading Ranch).
   Source: https://www.readingrockets.org/topics/spelling-and-word-study/articles/six-syllable-types

8. **Syllable-division rules — evidence is mixed/limited.** Syllabication is a widely used,
   "research-informed" strategy for decoding multisyllabic words (VCCV → divide between the
   two consonants; VCV → divide before or after the single consonant, tested both ways).
   However, **Devin Kearns' corpus analysis found the VCV rule predicts syllable boundary/
   vowel sound correctly only ~50% of the time**, worse when schwa is involved — i.e., rules
   are a useful decoding *heuristic*, not a reliable phonological fact of English, and should
   be taught as "try both, flex to what makes a real word" rather than an inviolable rule.
   Sources: https://readinguniverse.org/skill-explainer/multisyllable-words/multisyllabic-words-skill-explainer/explicitly-teaching-multisyllabic-words ;
   https://ortongillinghammama.com/decoding-multisyllabic-words/

9. **Structured Literacy for ELLs/adult ESL** — Toronto/Brock University research found **no
   difference in reading-intervention outcomes between English language learners and native
   English speakers** when given systematic, explicit instruction — i.e., decoding responds to
   structured literacy regardless of L1. ELLs showed rapid growth in phoneme awareness and
   decoding after **10 weeks of tiered, explicit instruction** (NIH-cited study). Caveat:
   structured literacy must be paired with deliberate oral-language/vocabulary/comprehension
   support for ELLs, or it collapses into decoding-only drilling that ignores meaning-building.
   Sources: https://ignite-reading.com/insights/structured-literacy-instruction-multilingual-learners/ ;
   https://thecorecollaborative.com/rethinking-structured-literacy-for-english-language-learners/

10. **Adult dyslexia signs** — early difficulty learning to read relative to peers/general
    intelligence, underperformance vs. ability, persistent difficulty with foreign-language
    learning, slow/effortful reading, spelling errors, reading avoidance, word/name
    mispronunciation. IDA and Additude both publish adult screeners; the **Adult Reading
    History Questionnaire (ARHQ)** is a validated self-report screener (not diagnostic).
    Sources: https://dyslexiaida.org/screening-for-dyslexia/dyslexia-screener-for-adults/ ;
    https://neurolearning.com/n_blog/signs-that-an-adult-should-be-screened-for-dyslexia/ ;
    https://www.additudemag.com/dyslexia-symptoms-test-adults/

## 2. KEEP (evidence strength: solid → moderate)

- **Explicit, systematic, cumulative sequencing of phonics content** — strongest evidence
  base of anything in OG; this is the actual load-bearing ingredient, confirmed both by the
  Stevens meta-analysis's directionally-positive (if non-significant) effects and by the
  broader structured-literacy/systematic-phonics literature outside OG specifically.
- **Diagnostic/continuous assessment driving what's taught next** — core to IDA's definition,
  uncontested, essential for a self-paced course (can't cumulative-sequence without knowing
  where a learner actually is).
- **Six syllable types as a teaching frame** — near-universal across every commercial program
  surveyed (Wilson, Barton, Sonday), used as the backbone for decoding progression. Good
  organizing scaffold even where the underlying division "rules" are imperfect.
- **Small group size / high intensity for older struggling readers** — Wanzek et al. found
  this is the biggest moderator of outcomes for grade 4–12 strugglers. For a self-paced
  digital/1:1 course this is actually a natural strength (effectively "group size = 1").
  Directly informs pacing design (see §5).
- **Morphology instruction (prefixes, roots, suffixes) layered on top of syllable/phonics
  work** — part of IDA's six elements, and every advanced-level program (Barton L9–10, WRS
  later steps) shifts weight from phonics to morphology as words get longer/more Latinate —
  strong practical justification even where isolated RCT evidence is thinner.
- **Explicit instruction works for ELLs at the decoding level with no adjustment needed** —
  supported by the Toronto/Brock finding of no outcome gap by L1; safe to keep the phonics
  scope-and-sequence uniform across native/non-native learners.

## 3. DROP / AVOID

- **Treating "multisensory" (VAKT: sand trays, carpet-letter-tracing, air-writing, color-coded
  manipulatives) as a load-bearing pedagogical mechanism.** No isolated evidence it adds
  reading gains beyond what explicit/systematic instruction already delivers; it may help
  engagement/attention for young or kinesthetic learners but should be framed in the course as
  optional engagement flavor, not "the reason OG works." Over-claiming this is the single most
  common overreach in OG marketing.
- **Rigid "always divide VCV before the consonant" (or "after") as an inviolable rule.**
  ~50% real-word accuracy per Kearns' analysis — teach it as a flexible first-guess heuristic
  ("try open, then try closed, check which makes a real/recognizable word") not a law.
- **Citing the Stevens et al. (2021) "not significant" result as proof OG "doesn't work"** —
  equally wrong as citing OG as "gold-standard proven." Present both sides (§4).
- **Assuming any single commercial program's exact lesson count/timeline generalizes** — Barton's
  "3–5 months per level, ninth-grade by level 10" is program-marketing-adjacent and specific to
  their pacing (2x/week, 1hr); don't import as a hard promise for this course.

## 4. CONTESTED

- **Does OG/multisensory instruction actually outperform other structured-literacy programs
  without the multisensory add-ons?** Unresolved — Stevens et al. found no significant
  effect either way at the aggregate level; Solari et al.'s commentary argues the
  null result reflects weak/heterogeneous evidence base, not proof of no effect. Present
  as genuinely open, not settled science in either direction.
- **Syllable-division rules: teach explicitly vs. de-emphasize in favor of larger
  orthographic-pattern recognition / morphology.** Kearns' critique suggests the rules
  are weak predictors; some structured-literacy voices (Reading Universe et al.) still teach
  them as a first-decoding-attempt strategy while explicitly flagging their unreliability.
  This course should do the latter (teach + flag limits), not silently drop syllable division.
- **Exact dosage/hours needed for an older non-reader** — Wanzek et al.'s g=0.10–0.16 for
  older students came from studies with widely varying total hours; no single
  "X hours to grade-level" number is defensible from the sources pulled this session. Treat
  any specific hour-count claim elsewhere in the industry (e.g., "300 hours to close the gap")
  as UNVERIFIED unless a primary source is found.
- **Logic of English and Spalding program structure** — not verified this session; needs a
  follow-up pass with direct primary-source fetches (their own scope-and-sequence documents)
  before citing specifics.

## 5. Concrete Design Rules for the Course

**Sequencing backbone (syllable types, in teaching order used by Wilson/Barton):**
1. Phonemic awareness (oral, no print) — segmenting/blending phonemes, rhyme, first sound
2. Closed syllables (CVC, short vowels) + basic digraphs/blends
3. Vowel-consonant-e (VCe / "silent e" / "magic e")
4. Open syllables (long vowel, ends in vowel)
5. Vowel teams (ai, ea, oa, ee, etc. — taught as a family, most-common-spelling first)
6. R-controlled syllables (ar, er, ir, or, ur)
7. Consonant-le (final stable syllable: -ble, -tle, -dle...)
8. Syllable division (VCCV, VCV, VCCCV) taught explicitly as a flexible first-attempt
   strategy, immediately paired with "does this make a real/known word?" check — never
   presented as a hard rule
9. Prefixes & suffixes (inflectional first, then derivational)
10. Advanced/Latin-Greek morphology (roots, more vowel-team variants, less-common patterns)

**Spelling rules to teach explicitly, in this order (align with when the phonics pattern is introduced):**
- FLOSS rule (f/l/s/z doubled after short vowel: off, well, pass, buzz) — teach alongside
  closed syllables
- 1-1-1 doubling rule (double final consonant before a vowel suffix when 1 syllable, 1 vowel,
  1 final consonant: run→running) — teach alongside suffixing unit
- Drop-e rule (drop silent e before a vowel suffix: hope→hoping) — teach alongside VCe unit
- Change-y-to-i rule (change y to i before a suffix unless suffix starts with i: happy→happier,
  but crying) — teach alongside suffixing unit
- Six reasons for silent-e (Barton's framing: marks long vowel, makes c/g soft, "no English
  word ends in v/u/j/i", prevents plural confusion (s vs se), signals "the" sound in a
  consonant-le word, distinguishes homophones) — teach as an advanced/consolidation unit once
  VCe is solid, since several of the six reasons only make sense after other patterns exist

**Diagnostic/pacing rules:**
- Never advance a unit purely on calendar time — require a mastery check (mixed real + nonsense
  word decoding at ~90%+ accuracy) before moving forward, per IDA's "diagnostic" principle.
- Since this is self-paced/effectively 1:1, lean into Wanzek et al.'s intensity finding —
  design for short, frequent (near-daily) sessions rather than long infrequent ones; small
  "group size" (here, 1) is the biggest lever the evidence supports for older/struggling
  learners.
- Keep multisensory elements (tracing, saying-while-writing, tapping out sounds) as *optional,
  labeled* engagement features — never claim they are required for the method to work.

**Screening/flagging:**
- Build a lightweight, explicitly-non-diagnostic screener into onboarding (borrowing item
  types from ARHQ / IDA adult screener style: reading-history questions, foreign-language
  learning difficulty, family history, slow/effortful reading, spelling difficulty) — flag
  "consider a formal evaluation" rather than ever labeling a user as dyslexic.

## 6. Implications for Older/Adult/ESL Learners

- Decoding-level structured literacy instruction transfers to ELLs/adult ESL with **no
  adjustment needed to the phonics scope-and-sequence itself** — same six syllable types,
  same spelling rules apply regardless of L1 (Toronto/Brock finding).
- **Do** add explicit vocabulary/oral-language/comprehension scaffolding alongside decoding
  for ESL learners — the risk flagged in sources is that structured literacy narrows to
  "phonics drilling" and starves meaning-building, which matters far more for an adult/ESL
  learner who already has real-world knowledge/vocabulary in L1 that needs bridging, not
  building from zero.
- **Do** expect much smaller effect sizes for older non-readers than are seen in K–3 early
  intervention (g≈0.10–0.16 in Wanzek et al. vs. larger effects typical for younger children)
  — set realistic expectations in course marketing/UX copy: an older/adult non-reader should
  expect steady incremental gains, not a fast "cracks the code" moment, and dosage (frequency,
  small-group/1:1 equivalent) matters more than any single program's brand.
- Adapt the adult-facing tone/UI so an adult or older child never gets child-coded content
  (avoid babyish illustrations/manipulatives framing) while keeping the same underlying
  sequence — several programs (Barton, Wilson) explicitly market their later levels as
  reaching "adult-level" (9th grade) reading, i.e., the sequence itself scales to adult text
  complexity by design.
- Persistent difficulty learning a foreign language is a a commonly cited adult
  dyslexia-risk signal — relevant to a Pakistan-based ESL audience where "struggling with
  English" could be either (a) normal L2-acquisition difficulty or (b) an underlying
  reading disability; the course's screener should ask about L1 literacy history to help
  distinguish the two, not assume all English struggle is purely an L2 issue.
