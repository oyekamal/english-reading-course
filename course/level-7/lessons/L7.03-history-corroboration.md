# L7.03 — Reading Like a Historian II: Corroboration

**Goal:** Learn the historian's third move — corroboration: reading further independent accounts
of the same event, applying the SAME sourcing template to each one (not just the first document
you meet), and seeing where accounts agree, disagree, and where the disagreement itself is the
most important finding.
**Time:** 45 min · **Materials:** notebook, Session 2 notes (the engraving and Preston's account).
**Research base:** SHEG corroboration framework; Shanahan & Shanahan 2008 (research/06 §1.8–1.9).

## 1. Retrieval warm-up (3 min)

From memory: state the sourcing template (who/when/why/predict). What specific fact did Source 1
(the engraving) and Source 2 (Preston) directly disagree about?

## 2. Word work (5 min)

- **Corroborate** (review, now applied) — Session 1 defined it; today you'll actually do it.
- **Discrepancy** — a difference or disagreement between two accounts of the same thing. *"There
  was a discrepancy between the two witnesses' description of the getaway car's color."* Your
  turn: a discrepancy doesn't automatically mean one person is lying — name another reason two
  honest witnesses might describe the same event differently (different vantage point, stress,
  memory, what they were focused on).
- **Involved witness** — a witness who was not just watching but did something during the event
  themselves (intervened, was struck, struck someone) — as distinct from a purely passive
  bystander. An involved witness is not automatically less honest, but their own actions can
  shape, understandably, how they remember and describe what happened next.

## 3. Prime the topic (3 min)

One fact before you read further: the real Boston Massacre trial record includes testimony from
dozens of witnesses, and historians studying it today still cannot fully reconcile every detail —
not because the record is bad, but because five minutes of a violent, dark, snow-covered street
scene genuinely looks different from every different position in the crowd. **Orienting
question:** if a fight broke out in a crowded room you were standing in, would you expect your own
account afterward to be fully accurate about who moved first — or would adrenaline and your own
angle make some details genuinely unreliable, through no dishonesty on your part?

## 4. Meet the move: corroboration (4 min)

**Corroboration** means finding accounts from different, independent people or sources — people
with different stakes and different vantage points — and comparing them point by point.
Historians treat agreement across independent sources as much stronger evidence than either source
alone, and they treat disagreement not as a dead end but as a clue: *why* do these two honest-
seeming accounts differ, and does the difference matter to the actual question being asked?
Critically: **corroboration does not skip the sourcing step for the new document** — every new
source gets the same who/when/why/predict questions Session 2 applied to the engraving and to
Preston, before its content is trusted or compared.

## 5. Close reading — a third source, sourced first (16 min)

**Context:** Richard Palmes was a Boston merchant who, by his own account, walked toward the
soldiers when the crowd gathered to see what was happening — not a passenger drifting past, and
not one of the crowd's ringleaders either. His account was collected as a sworn deposition and
later given as testimony at the soldiers' trial. **A note on this source, ahead of reading it:**
Palmes' own actions in the final seconds are a genuinely disputed detail across secondary
accounts of this event — this session quotes his own sworn testimony directly, rather than
narrating a single settled sequence, precisely because the disagreement between his testimony and
other accounts is itself the teaching point (see Question 3 below).

> **Source 3 — Richard Palmes, Boston merchant. From his sworn testimony at the soldiers' trial,
> as transcribed in the trial record (Boston, 1770). Quoted directly, in the original phrasing
> and spelling, from the primary-source transcription at bostonmassacre.net/trial/d-palmes.htm —
> see the precise source note below.**
>
> "I went to see what was the matter, and found Captain Preston at the head of six or eight
> soldiers... I asked him if the soldiers' guns were loaded. He answered they were. I asked him
> if they were going to fire upon the inhabitants. He answered by no means... I had not been
> there more than two minutes before I heard the word 'fire'... I heard the report of a gun...
> After that gun went off I heard the word fire, but who said it I know not... After that I
> turned and saw the Grenadier who fired first, attempting to prick me by the side of the Captain
> with his bayonet. I had a large stick in my hand. I struck over-hand and hit him on his left
> arm, and knocked his hand from his gun. Upon that I turned, thinking the other would do the
> same, and struck at anybody at first, and hit the Captain."
>
> *(Palmes also testified the whole encounter, from first shout to last shot, lasted "not more
> than three quarters of a minute in the whole.")*

**Sourcing questions for Source 3 — apply the same template as Sessions 2, before comparing
anything:**

1. **Who** is Palmes, **when** did he give this account (relative to the trial), and **for what
   purpose** was it recorded (sworn trial testimony, not a private diary)?
2. In his own words, when did Palmes strike the soldier — before, or after, that soldier had
   already fired? What was Palmes' stated reason for striking him (read the sentence just before
   "I struck over-hand" again)?
3. **This is the teaching point:** other, independent secondary accounts of this same night
   describe a *second* detail Palmes' own quoted testimony above doesn't spell out in this much
   length — that his blow aimed at Captain Preston (the one right after he struck the soldier)
   narrowly missed Preston's head and landed on his arm instead, by some accounts because Palmes
   slipped on the icy ground. A witness who was himself swinging a weapon in the final seconds
   before and after the first shot is describing his own actions, under oath, in a chaotic scene,
   about facts that bear directly on his own potential legal exposure. **What would you check,
   knowing that, before taking his account of "who fired first" and "why" at face value?** (There
   is no single settled answer here — historians and popular accounts genuinely disagree on some
   of the finer sequencing; naming that disagreement honestly is the correct move, not picking a
   version and stating it as fact.)
4. Does the fact that Palmes physically struck a soldier make him an **involved witness** rather
   than a neutral one? Does that automatically make his account less trustworthy than a pure
   bystander's would be — or does it mean his stake and vantage point are different from
   Preston's, and need to be weighed on their own terms, the same as any other source?

**Questions:**

5. Where do Sources 2 and 3 **agree**, despite Source 3 coming from a witness with his own
   involvement in the chaos? Why does that agreement matter more than if only Preston himself made
   the same claim?
6. Where does Source 1 (the engraving) most sharply **contradict** both Source 2 and Source 3?
   Given what you know about who made Source 1 and why (Session 2), how much weight should that
   contradiction carry?
7. **Think question:** a historian reading all three sources might conclude: "It is not possible
   to know with certainty who shouted the order to fire — and that uncertainty is itself a fact
   worth stating, not a gap to paper over with a guess, or with whichever source is most dramatic."
   Do you agree that admitting uncertainty can be the historically honest answer, even though it
   feels unsatisfying? Give a reason.

## 7. Close reading — a fourth source: how the case was actually decided (8 min)

> **Source 4 — from John Adams' closing argument for the defense, given at the soldiers' trial
> (December 1770), as recorded in the published trial transcript. Quoted directly, checked
> against the Founders Online / Adams Papers transcription — see the precise source note below.**
>
> "Facts are stubborn things; and whatever may be our wishes, our inclinations, or the dictates of
> our passions, they cannot alter the state of facts and evidence."

**Sourcing questions for Source 4:**

8. **Who** spoke this, **when** (relative to the trial's outcome), and **for what purpose**? Adams
   was a Patriot himself, arguing to acquit British soldiers — why might a Patriot lawyer choose
   this specific line as his argument's foundation?
9. Given everything you now know from Sources 1–3 (a propaganda image, an accused officer's
   defense, and an involved witness's own testimony about his own actions), what do you think
   Adams meant by "facts... cannot alter the state of facts and evidence" — as a warning against
   which of the three sources' temptations specifically?

## 8. Discussion — reciprocal roles (6 min)

**Predict:** if a fifth independent account existed — say, from a soldier standing in the firing
line — would you expect it to side more with Source 2/3's uncertainty or Source 1's "clear order"
version? **Question:** what one question would resolve the discrepancy if you could ask Palmes,
Preston, and the engraver Revere directly? **Clarify:** explain why "Palmes struck a soldier, so
ignore his account" would be exactly the wrong move for a historian to make — and why "Palmes'
blow caused the shooting" would ALSO be wrong, since his own sworn testimony places his blow after
the first shot, not before it. **Summarize:** in two sentences, what does the corroboration of all
four sources actually establish, and what remains open?

## 9. Write to read (6 min)

Write a short paragraph (6–8 sentences): using the corroboration table, explain what you can now
say with confidence about the night of March 5, 1770, what remains uncertain, and why reading
Palmes' own testimony directly (rather than a single narrated summary) changed your reading. Use
**corroborate**, **discrepancy**, and **involved witness** correctly at least once each.

## Check

Model answer elements: confident claim — the crowd was hostile and provoked the soldiers before
any shots were fired (Sources 2 and 3 agree; Source 1 omits this entirely, which is itself
informative given Source 1's known purpose); uncertain claim — whether Preston personally ordered
the fire (Sources 2 and 3 both say no clear order was heard, while Source 1 depicts a clear
signal — and Source 1 is the source with the strongest documented motive to dramatize); Palmes'
own testimony places his blow on the soldier *after* that soldier had already fired, in response
to being threatened with a bayonet — a deliberate, defensive counter-strike, not an accidental
cause of the shooting — which makes him an involved participant in the chaos that followed the
first shot, not a neutral bystander, and not (contrary to how popular retellings sometimes
compress the story) someone whose own swing precipitated it. His account is still valuable
specifically because it's a first-person description of his own actions under oath — exactly the
kind of testimony that most needs checking against other sources, and that this session checks
directly rather than smoothing over.

Self-check: fill in the corroboration table correctly (4 rows × 3 sources), correctly state, using
Palmes' own quoted words, when his blow landed relative to the first shot and why he says he threw
it, and state the real historical trial outcome (Preston and six soldiers acquitted of murder; two
soldiers convicted of manslaughter) as an example of how courts, like historians, sometimes have to
decide despite real uncertainty.

**Tutor notes:** The point of this session is not "who really shouted fire" (unresolvable) but the
habit of sourcing AND corroborating every document, including the ones that feel neutral at first
glance — and reading a participant's own account of their own actions with the same scrutiny
applied to everyone else's. Do not let a learner conclude "Palmes hit someone, so he's unreliable,"
and do not let a learner conclude "Palmes' blow caused the shooting" either — his own sworn
testimony places the blow after the first shot. The correct teaching point is: every witness in
this event had some stake or involvement, including ones who seem passive at first, and a witness
describing their own actions in a fast-moving, legally consequential event is exactly the case
where a historian checks hardest, not the case where they relax. Secondary accounts of this event
genuinely differ on some finer sequencing details even among historians — flag that honestly to
learners rather than presenting one smoothed-over version as settled. For younger learners, the
idea that "an honest person's own actions can still shape how they remember and tell a story" is
worth pausing on explicitly.
