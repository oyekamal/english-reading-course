# L7.13 — Literary Reading II: Unreliable Narrator & Poetry

**Goal:** Recognize when a first-person narrator's account of events can't be fully trusted, and
read a short poem for figurative meaning, sound, and compression (saying a lot in very few words).
**Time:** 40 min · **Materials:** notebook.

## 1. Retrieval warm-up (3 min)

From memory: define *figurative language*, *tone*, and *situational irony* (Session 12).

## 2. Word work (5 min)

- **Unreliable narrator** — a first-person narrator whose account the reader has reason to
  doubt — because they're lying, mistaken, mentally unwell, a child who doesn't understand what
  they're describing, or simply too invested in their own version of events to see it clearly.
  The reader has to read *around* the narrator's claims to reconstruct what's probably really true.
- **Connotation** — the feelings and associations a word carries beyond its literal dictionary
  meaning. *"Slender" and "skinny" can describe the same body — one carries admiration, the other
  something closer to pity or judgment.* Poets choose words as much for connotation as for
  literal meaning.

## 3. Prime the topic (3 min)

Two facts before you read: **(1)** first-person narration is never a neutral camera — every
narrator selects, frames, and sometimes distorts what they report, whether or not they mean to.
**(2)** poetry compresses meaning into very few words on purpose; a single concrete image is often
doing the work a whole paragraph of abstract explanation would do in prose. **Orienting question:**
recall a time a friend told you their side of an argument they'd had with someone else — did
anything in how they told it make you suspect there was more to the other person's side than they
were letting on?

## 4. Meet the move: spotting an unreliable narrator (4 min)

Ask: does the narrator have an obvious reason to want you to believe a particular version of
events? Does the narrator's own language undercut their claims (insisting too hard that they're
calm, or reasonable, or definitely not the one at fault)? Do other characters' brief reactions,
reported in passing, seem to contradict the narrator's version of themselves?

## 5. Close reading — an original short story with an unreliable narrator (14 min)

> **A Perfectly Reasonable Explanation**
>
> I want to be clear from the start that I am an extremely calm person, and that everything I did
> that Thursday was completely reasonable given the circumstances, and I would like whoever reads
> this to keep that firmly in mind, because I know how it's going to sound.
>
> My neighbor, Mr. Feldt, had for several weeks been leaving his garbage bins out on the curb a
> full day early, which is, if you consult the homeowners' association bylaws — and I have, twice —
> a clear violation, and which I mentioned to him on four separate occasions, each time perfectly
> calmly, using a completely normal tone of voice that in no way could be described as anything
> but civil.
>
> On the Thursday in question, I noticed the bins out again, a full eighteen hours early, and I
> want to stress that what I did next was not, as my wife later suggested, "unhinged" — that word
> is simply not accurate — but was, in fact, the only reasonable response left to a reasonable
> person who had already tried every reasonable channel. I moved the bins. That is all I did. I
> moved them, calmly, back onto his own driveway, which took, I will admit, several trips, since
> apparently four days' worth of garbage bins are heavier than one might expect, and I may have
> raised my voice slightly at one point when he came out onto his porch, though "screaming," which
> is the word his porch camera's audio apparently picked up according to the police officer who
> came by later — purely as a courtesy visit, nothing more — is, again, simply not an accurate
> description of a person who is completely calm.
>
> The officer asked whether I thought this was, perhaps, an overreaction to a bylaw about garbage
> bins. I explained, calmly, that he clearly did not understand the full eighteen-day history of
> the situation, at which point he asked whether "eighteen days" sounded to me like something a
> calm person would have been counting. I did not think that question was especially relevant, and
> said so, calmly.

**Questions:**

1. List three specific phrases the narrator uses to insist they are "calm" or "reasonable." Why
   does a narrator who is genuinely calm rarely need to say so this many times?
2. Name one detail reported almost in passing — something another character says or does — that
   directly contradicts the narrator's version of themselves.
3. Reconstruct, in your own words, what probably actually happened that Thursday, reading around
   the narrator's own framing rather than trusting it.
4. **Think question:** the narrator never once lies about a fact you can point to (the bins were
   moved; the officer did come by) — the unreliability is entirely in the *interpretation* laid
   on top of true events, not in the events themselves. Why can a narrator be unreliable while
   still technically telling the truth about every individual fact?

## 6. Close reading — an original poem (8 min)

> **What the Water Keeps**
>
> The river does not remember
> the bridge that used to stand here,
> only the shape a bridge leaves
> in how a current has to bend.
>
> My grandmother's kitchen is gone,
> the house sold, the table sold,
> but I set my own table crooked
> in the exact way hers always was —
>
> not from clumsiness. From memory
> keeping a shape it never named,
> the way water still turns left
> at a bridge that isn't there.

**Questions:**

5. Identify the poem's central metaphor. What is being compared to what?
6. The poem never says "I miss my grandmother" or "grief" directly. What specific image carries
   that meaning instead, and what does the compression (saying it this way, in so few words) add
   that a direct statement wouldn't?
7. Look at the word "crooked" in stanza 2. What connotation does it carry here — clumsy/wrong, or
   something closer to inherited/loving? How does stanza 3 change your reading of "crooked"?

## 7. Discussion — reciprocal roles (5 min)

**Predict:** if "A Perfectly Reasonable Explanation" continued for one more paragraph, what would
the narrator most likely still be denying? **Question:** what's one question you'd ask the
narrator directly that they'd almost certainly answer unreliably? **Clarify:** explain "unreliable
narrator" to someone using the specific "eighteen days" detail as your proof. **Summarize:** state
the poem's central idea in one sentence without using the word "memory."

## 8. Write to read (6 min)

Choose one: (a) rewrite the garbage-bin Thursday as a neutral, third-person, reliable news-brief
version of events (3–4 sentences) — strip out every unreliable framing word; or (b) write your own
3–4 line poem using one concrete, physical image (not an abstract feeling-word) to carry an
emotion, the way "crooked" carries inheritance and love in "What the Water Keeps." Use
**unreliable narrator** or **connotation** correctly at least once in a short explanation of your
choice.

## Check

Model reliable rewrite: *"A resident and his neighbor had a dispute over early curbside garbage
placement. After several verbal warnings, the resident removed the bins from the curb and returned
them to the neighbor's driveway, raising his voice during the confrontation. Police responded to a
noise complaint; no charges were filed."* Notice everything "calm" and "reasonable" is gone, and
the story reads completely differently with the same facts.

Self-check: in your rewrite, did you remove every self-defense word ("calm," "reasonable") while
keeping every actual fact? That's the core skill this session teaches.

**Tutor notes:** Both texts are original compositions written for this course. For real,
classic public-domain companion texts using these exact techniques for further reading:
**Edgar Allan Poe's "The Tell-Tale Heart"** (1843, U.S. public domain, via Project Gutenberg) is
the canonical unreliable-narrator short story (a narrator insisting on his own sanity while
describing a murder); **Robert Frost's "The Road Not Taken"** (1916, U.S. public domain) is a
short, widely anthologized poem built on compressed metaphor and a famously mis-read final
stanza — a genuinely good discussion pairing with today's session once the class wants to try a
real historical text against the moves just learned.
