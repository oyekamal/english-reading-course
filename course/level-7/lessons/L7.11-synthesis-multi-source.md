# L7.11 — Synthesis II: 3–4 Sources, One Research Question

**Goal:** The capstone disciplinary-reading task — read four mixed-genre sources (a data summary,
a historical document excerpt, a science-style study summary, and an op-ed) on one real question,
apply the right discipline-specific move to each, and synthesize all four into one answer. This
is PIAAC Level 4–5 territory: "search/integrate/evaluate across multiple dense texts... construct
syntheses of similar/contrasting viewpoints" (research/06 §1.11).
**Time:** 45–50 min · **Materials:** notebook.

## 1. Retrieval warm-up (3 min)

From memory: what four steps does synthesizing two sources require (Session 10)?

## 2. Word work (4 min)

- **Triangulate** — to check a claim against three (or more) different kinds of evidence, from
  independent angles, the way a surveyor fixes a location using three reference points. Today
  you'll triangulate across four sources of very different kinds.
- **Weight of evidence** — not every source deserves equal trust; a reader has to judge how much
  each source should count based on what it actually is (see Session 4's evidence-strength
  ranking) and factor that into the final synthesis.

## 3. Prime the topic (3 min)

Two facts before you read: **(1)** questions like "should companies require full-time office
work" rarely have one clean, fully-settled answer — the honest task is weighing real, imperfect
evidence of different kinds, not finding the one source that "wins." **(2)** the strongest
professional writing on hard questions (a policy memo, a board recommendation) routinely
synthesizes exactly this mix of data, historical pattern, controlled research, and opinion.
**Orienting question:** think of a real decision in your own life (a job, a purchase, a move) where
you had to weigh a personal survey-of-friends kind of evidence against something more rigorous
(a review, a data sheet) — which one did you actually trust more, and why?

## Research question

**Should most office-based companies require employees back in the office full-time, five days a
week?**

## 4. Read all four sources, applying the discipline-specific move you learned for each (20 min)

> **Source 1 — Data summary (apply Session 5's data-in-text move)**
>
> A 2024 survey of 3,200 employees across 40 mid-size U.S. companies that had shifted to hybrid
> (2–3 office days/week) found: 71% reported preferring hybrid to full-time office work; 58% said
> they would consider leaving their job if required back full-time; managers at the same
> companies reported mixed views on productivity — 34% said team productivity had improved under
> hybrid, 29% said it had declined, and 37% reported no clear change. The survey did not measure
> actual output data (sales, project completion rates) directly — all productivity figures are
> manager *perception*, not measured performance.

> **Source 2 — Historical context excerpt (apply Session 2–3's sourcing/contextualizing move)**
>
> From a 1962 internal memo, a mid-size insurance company, on why the firm was moving from a
> six-day to a five-day office week (adapted, illustrative period document): "Several of our
> competitors have adopted the five-day week this year, citing improved morale and no measurable
> loss in output. We have reviewed our own production figures for the departments that piloted
> the change last quarter and found output essentially unchanged, with a modest improvement in
> absenteeism. The Board has therefore approved the five-day week for all departments effective
> January."
>
> *Context note:* the standard five-day office week was itself a contested, gradually adopted
> change in the mid-20th century, resisted at first by many employers on exactly the same
> "productivity will suffer" grounds now used against hybrid/remote work — worth noting as
> historical pattern, not proof about today's specific question.

> **Source 3 — Science-style study summary (apply Session 4's claim/method/sample move)**
>
> A frequently cited 2015 workplace study (Bloom, Liang, Ouyang & Ying, published research on a
> Chinese travel-booking company) had customer-service employees volunteer to work from home for
> nine months, randomly assigning half to work from home and half to stay in the office —a real
> controlled experiment, not just an observed pattern. Home workers showed a 13% performance
> increase (more calls handled per shift), attributed mainly to a quieter environment and fewer
> sick days; however, home workers were also promoted at roughly half the rate of office workers
> over the same period, and after the experiment, many home workers voluntarily chose to return
> to the office, citing loneliness. This is a real, controlled, published, widely-cited study —
> but it studied one company, one job type (customer service), in one country, in 2015; whether
> its findings generalize to other kinds of work, or to today's post-pandemic environment, is a
> real, open question.

> **Source 4 — Op-ed (apply Session 6–7's claim/reasons/evidence/fallacy move)**
>
> "The office isn't just a place to do tasks — it's where junior employees learn by watching how
> senior colleagues handle a hard client call, where a hallway conversation solves a problem
> that would have taken a week of scheduled video calls, and where a company's culture actually
> gets transmitted, not just described in a handbook. Every company that has fully abandoned the
> office is going to regret it within five years, when they realize they've raised a generation of
> employees who never learned the informal skills that only come from being physically present.
> Some hybrid advocates point to survey data showing employees prefer flexibility — but employee
> preference for an easier arrangement isn't the same as that arrangement being good for their
> long-term careers, any more than students preferring no homework means no homework is good for
> their education."

## 5. Synthesis questions (14 min)

1. **Weight the evidence:** rank the four sources by evidence strength for the specific question
   asked, using Session 4's ranking (controlled experiment > survey/observational > single
   opinion, etc.). Which source is the strongest evidence, and why? Which is the weakest?
2. **Find the real agreement:** what does Source 1's data and Source 3's study actually agree on
   (hint: look at what happened to absences/sick days and performance metrics in both)?
3. **Find the real disagreement/gap:** Source 3 found a performance *increase* for home workers,
   but also a promotion *penalty* and eventual voluntary return. Does Source 1's survey measure
   either of those two things? What does that gap tell you about how much Source 1 alone can
   settle the question?
4. **Fallacy check (Session 7):** Source 4 contains a comparison in its last sentence ("any more
   than students preferring no homework means no homework is good for their education"). Is this
   a fair analogy or a weak one? Does it commit a fallacy, and if so which one?
5. **Contextualize (Session 2–3):** what does Source 2 add that none of the other three sources
   can — and what should you NOT conclude from it (i.e., don't assume 1962's five-day-week
   pattern proves today's hybrid question will resolve the same way)?
6. **Write the synthesis (write this as your actual answer, 8–12 sentences):** Using all four
   sources, state: what's actually well-supported (cite the strongest source); what's genuinely
   contested or unmeasured (cite the gap you found in Q3); what Source 4's argument gets right and
   where it overreaches; and your own reasoned judgment on the research question, explicitly
   naming which piece of evidence moved you most and why.

## 6. Discussion — reciprocal roles (6 min)

**Predict:** what additional source (a fifth one) would most improve this synthesis — what's the
single biggest gap in the evidence as it stands? **Question:** what would you ask a company that
claims hybrid work hurt productivity, based on Source 1's own limitation? **Clarify:** explain to
someone why Source 3, despite being the strongest single piece of evidence, still can't fully
answer the research question alone. **Summarize:** state the synthesis in three sentences.

## Check

Model synthesis core: Source 3 (controlled experiment) is the strongest evidence but narrow in
scope (one company, one job type, 2015); Source 1 (large survey) is broader but measures
perception, not real output, and doesn't test promotion/loneliness effects the way Source 3 did —
so the two sources' apparent "agreement" (fewer sick days, positive productivity signal) is real
but doesn't fully corroborate each other on the exact same measures. Source 4's homework analogy
is a real but imperfect analogy (weak, not a formal named fallacy, but worth flagging as an
assumed premise — "less homework preference ⇒ worse for education" — that isn't itself proven
either) — a strong learner answer notes the comparison assumes its own conclusion. Source 2 adds
useful historical pattern-recognition (resistance to schedule change is not new) but cannot
predict today's outcome. A defensible overall judgment: the evidence supports flexible/hybrid
arrangements having a real, if job-type-dependent, productivity case, alongside real, unresolved
costs to career-development access — both should appear in a fair synthesis.

Self-check: does your Q6 synthesis name a specific piece of evidence for each claim it makes,
rather than a general impression? Did you correctly flag Source 4's analogy as an assumption
rather than proof?

**Tutor notes:** Source 3 is closely based on real, well-documented research (Nicholas Bloom and
coauthors' widely cited randomized work-from-home study of a Chinese travel company, first
published 2013–2015) — the description above is accurate to the widely reported findings
(performance increase, lower promotion rate, high voluntary return-to-office rate) though exact
percentages are illustrative/rounded; a learner or tutor who wants to verify should search "Bloom
Liang Ouyang Ying work from home experiment" as good lateral-reading practice. Sources 1, 2, and 4
are constructed for this lesson. This is the heaviest single session in the level — allow the full
45–50 minutes and split across two sittings if needed rather than rushing Q6.
