# L7.10 — Synthesis I: Two Sources, Contrasting Viewpoints

**Goal:** Combine two opposing op-eds into one written answer that represents both sides fairly
and states, specifically, where they actually disagree — not just "some people think X, others
think Y."
**Time:** 40 min · **Materials:** notebook, Session 6 notes (the phone-ban essay).

## 1. Retrieval warm-up (3 min)

From memory: state the four obligations/checks a careful contract reader applies (Session 9).

## 2. Word work (5 min)

- **Synthesis** — combining information or viewpoints from more than one source into a new,
  coherent understanding — not just summarizing each one separately, side by side, and stopping.
- **Represent fairly** — describing an opposing view the way its strongest supporter would
  recognize it, even if you disagree with it — the opposite of a straw man (Session 7).
- **Point of disagreement** — the specific claim, fact, or value judgment where two sources
  actually part ways — as distinct from things they agree on, or issues neither addresses.

## 3. Prime the topic (3 min)

Two facts before you reread: **(1)** two well-argued essays on the same question often agree on
more than they disagree on — the disagreement is usually narrower and more specific than "they
disagree about phones" suggests. **(2)** representing an opposing view fairly is a skill separate
from agreeing with it; a strong thinker can state the other side's best point better than a weak
thinker can state their own side's. **Orienting question:** recall a real disagreement (with a
parent, friend, or in the news) where, once you stated the other person's actual point instead of
your assumption of it, the disagreement turned out to be narrower than you'd thought.

## 4. Meet the move: synthesizing two sources (4 min)

Reading two sources on the same question and writing one answer requires four steps: **(1)**
identify what both sources agree on (often more than expected); **(2)** identify the specific
point(s) where they genuinely disagree; **(3)** for each disagreement, note what kind of
disagreement it is — a factual dispute (checkable), a values dispute (not resolvable by more
facts alone), or a dispute about priorities (both facts might be true, but which matters more?);
**(4)** write an answer that states both, fairly, before giving your own judgment.

## 5. Close reading — two op-eds on the same question (16 min)

Reread Session 6's essay ("Schools Should Ban Phones During the School Day — All Day") if needed.
Now read the opposing view.

> **A Full-Day Phone Ban Goes Too Far**
>
> Nobody disputes that phones distract students during class — the attention research on that
> point is genuinely strong, and no serious opponent of a full-day ban argues students should be
> texting during a lesson. The real disagreement is narrower than full-day-ban advocates suggest:
> should phones also be banned during lunch and passing periods, when class isn't happening at
> all?
>
> A full-day ban treats a fifteen-year-old checking a message from a working parent, or
> coordinating a ride home, the same as a student ignoring a teacher mid-lesson — as if all phone
> use, everywhere in the building, is equally harmful. It isn't. The in-class case is about
> protecting instructional time from a specific, measurable distraction. The lunch-period case is
> about something much softer and less measurable: a hoped-for increase in face-to-face
> conversation, based on informal staff impressions in a handful of districts, not the kind of
> large, controlled research that backs the in-class case.
>
> There's also a real equity dimension full-day-ban advocates rarely address directly: students
> who work part-time jobs, who care for younger siblings after school, or whose parents' work
> schedules are unpredictable, use lunchtime phone access to coordinate real logistics — a ride, a
> shift change, a sibling pickup — not just social messaging. A full ban's "office stays reachable"
> answer assumes every urgent need is an emergency the office would even know to relay; a text
> about a changed pickup time is not an emergency, but it's still something a student needs to
> handle, and an office switchboard is a poor substitute for a two-line text a student can send in
> ten seconds between classes.
>
> None of this means "no phone policy at all." A ban on phone use during instructional time,
> strictly enforced, addresses the actual, well-evidenced problem. Extending that ban into every
> unstructured minute of the day trades a strong, specific case for a much weaker, more speculative
> one — and does it by treating every student's non-class phone use as equally disposable, which
> for some students, it simply isn't.

**Questions:**

1. What do both essays agree on? Quote the sentence(s) that show the agreement most clearly.
2. State the specific point of disagreement — not "phones," but the precise scope question both
   essays are actually arguing about.
3. Is the disagreement mainly factual, values-based, or about priorities? Defend your answer using
   both texts' actual content.
4. This essay raises an argument the original essay never addressed at all (not just answered
   weakly — never mentioned). What is it?
5. **Think question:** both essays agree the in-class attention research is strong. Given that,
   what's the actual, narrower question a school board would need to decide — and what
   additional evidence (not yet in either essay) would help settle it?

## 6. Discussion — reciprocal roles (6 min)

**Predict:** which essay's evidence, overall, is stronger on the specific disagreement point
(lunch/passing periods), and why? **Question:** what data would you want a district to collect
before deciding? **Clarify:** explain "represent fairly" using this second essay's fair treatment
of the first essay's strongest point as the example. **Summarize:** two sentences: agreement,
then the actual disagreement.

## 7. Write to read (7 min)

Write a synthesis paragraph (7–10 sentences) that: states what both essays agree on; states the
specific, narrow point of disagreement; names what kind of disagreement it is; represents each
side's strongest point fairly (in your own words, not a straw man); and ends with your own
reasoned judgment — which you should be able to defend by naming the specific evidence that moved
you, not just a preference. Use **synthesis** and **point of disagreement** correctly at least
once each.

## Check

Model synthesis core: both essays agree in-class phone bans, backed by strong attention research,
are justified. They disagree narrowly on whether the ban should extend to lunch/passing periods —
a dispute that is partly factual (how strong is the "social skills" evidence, really — weak per
Session 6's own analysis) and partly about priorities (protecting a hoped-for social benefit vs.
protecting some students' real logistical needs). The second essay raises the equity/logistics
point the first essay never addressed at all. A fair synthesis names both points clearly before
giving a personal judgment, rather than picking a side and ignoring the essay it disagrees with.

Self-check: does your Write to read answer state the *specific* disagreement (scope: in-class only
vs. all-day) rather than a vague "some people disagree about phones"? Did you name the unaddressed
equity point?

**Tutor notes:** The goal here is not to make the learner pick a "correct" side — a genuinely
well-supported synthesis can favor either position, provided it's built on the actual points of
agreement/disagreement and represents both essays fairly. Watch for a learner defaulting to "both
sides have a point" without naming anything specific — push for the precise scope question and at
least one piece of evidence that would resolve it.
