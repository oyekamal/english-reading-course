# L7.01 — Orientation & Baseline: What "Advanced Reader" Means

**Goal:** Understand the Level 7 finish line in concrete terms; take a baseline stamina/speed
reading; meet the academic-vocabulary routine that runs through the whole level.
**Time:** 40 min · **Materials:** timer, notebook/paper, this lesson.
**Self-study note:** every "Check" step has a model answer — score yourself honestly; the level
only works if you're honest about what you can't yet do.

## 1. Retrieval warm-up (4 min)

From Level 6: name the 5 expository text structures (sequence, cause/effect, compare/contrast,
problem/solution, description) and the 4 reciprocal-teaching roles (predict, question, clarify,
summarize). Write them from memory, then check against this list. If you can't recall 3+ of
either, review your Level 6 notes before continuing — this level assumes both are automatic.

## 2. Word work — the academic-vocabulary routine (6 min)

Every session in Level 7 introduces 2–3 **academic words** (mostly Tier 2 — words used across
subjects, not tied to one topic) the same way: friendly definition → 2 examples → your turn.
Today's words, because you'll meet them constantly this level:

- **Infer** — to work out something not directly stated, using clues plus what you already know.
  *"The story never says she was angry, but I infer it from her slammed door."* *"Detectives infer
  who committed a crime from evidence, not confessions."* Your turn: infer something about a
  neighbor from one clue (a locked gate, a light on at 3am) without being told it directly.
- **Corroborate** — to confirm something using an independent, separate source. *"One witness
  says the car was blue; a security camera corroborates it."* *"A single study rarely proves a
  drug works — later studies have to corroborate it."* Your turn: name one thing you believe that
  is corroborated by more than one source (a news event, a fact from school), and one thing you
  believe that you've never checked against a second source.
- **Bias** — a leaning toward one side, often invisible to the person who has it, that shapes what
  they notice, report, or leave out. *"A company's own website has a bias toward praising its own
  product."* *"A sports fan's bias makes them see every close call against their team as unfair."*
  Your turn: name a source (a person, a website, a channel) whose bias you already account for
  when you read or listen to it, and say what the bias is.

## 3. Prime the topic (3 min)

Two facts before the main passage: **(1)** this level's five "rooms" (history, science,
argument, contracts, data) map onto how Shanahan & Shanahan (2008) found real historians,
scientists, and other specialists actually read — not a generic invented list (research/06
§1.8). **(2)** SHEG's 2019 national study found 96% of students did not check a website's bias
before trusting it — the gap this whole level is built to close (research/06 §1.9). **Orienting
question:** which of the five "rooms" do you think you already do some version of, without
anyone teaching you — and which feels like a completely new skill?

## 4. Close reading — the finish line, in a text (14 min)

Read this passage once at a normal pace, timing yourself (write your start/end time).

> **What Does It Mean to Read Well?**
>
> Ask a six-year-old what a good reader can do, and the answer is simple: read a page without
> stopping. Ask a hiring manager, a nurse reading a drug interaction chart, or a citizen deciding
> how to vote, and the answer gets larger and much harder to fake. Real advanced reading is not
> a single skill; it is a bundle of moves that specialists in different fields learned to make
> automatic inside their own subject before anyone called it "critical reading" at all.
>
> A historian who opens a soldier's diary from 1770 does not read it the way she reads a modern
> newspaper. She asks who wrote it, when, and why, before she asks what it says — a soldier
> defending himself in court writes a different account than a bystander with nothing to lose.
> She then goes looking for a second, independent account of the same event, because a single
> voice, however sincere, is a claim, not a fact, until something else corroborates it.
>
> A scientist reading a study asks a different set of questions: what, exactly, was measured; how
> many people or things were tested; and — the trap that catches almost everyone outside the
> field — whether two things moving together (say, ice cream sales and drowning deaths, both
> rising every summer) actually means one causes the other, or whether a hidden third factor (hot
> weather) is driving both. A journalist reading a company's press release asks who benefits if
> the reader believes the headline. A tenant reading a lease asks which sentence, buried in
> paragraph nine, quietly changes what a friendlier sentence in paragraph two seemed to promise.
>
> None of these moves are exotic. They are teachable, and they are largely the same handful of
> questions — who is speaking, what is their evidence, does something independent confirm it,
> what would change my mind — asked inside four or five different rooms. Two large research
> traditions converge on this point from different directions. One (Shanahan & Shanahan, 2008)
> studied working historians, scientists, and mathematicians and found each discipline reads its
> own texts in genuinely different, learnable ways, and that generic "read the textbook"
> strategies stop working for readers once they pass roughly a middle-school level of skill. The
> other (the Stanford History Education Group's 2019 national study of nearly 3,500 students)
> found that most people, including capable ones, do not do this automatically online: over half
> judged a piece of unverified video "strong evidence," two-thirds could not tell a paid
> advertisement from a news story, and almost nobody checked who was behind a website before
> trusting it. The gap between those two findings is exactly what an advanced reader closes: not
> more reading, and not a longer checklist, but the specific, disciplined habit of asking a
> source what it is and what would confirm or break it, every single time, before believing what
> it says.
>
> That is the finish line this level is built around: not reading faster, though speed matters,
> and not reading harder books for their own sake, though the books here are genuinely hard —
> but reading like the specialist whose room you happen to be standing in, and never fully
> trusting a single voice until something independent has had a chance to argue with it.

**Questions (write full-sentence answers):**

1. According to the passage, what is the actual difference between how a historian reads a
   soldier's diary and how she reads a modern newspaper?
2. Name the "trap" the passage says catches almost everyone when they read a scientific study,
   and explain it in your own words using a different example than ice cream/drowning.
3. **Think question:** The passage claims specialists' reading habits reduce to "largely the same
   handful of questions... asked inside four or five different rooms." List those questions in
   your own words. Do you think the same four questions really would work for reading a poem
   (Session 13)? Why or why not — answer before you've done that session; you'll revisit this.

## 5. Discussion — reciprocal roles (6 min)

With a partner or aloud to yourself: **Predict** which of the five "rooms" (history, science,
argument, contracts, data) you expect to find hardest, and why. **Question:** ask one question
this passage raised that it doesn't answer. **Clarify:** find the one sentence in paragraph 5
that states the course's actual thesis, and explain it as if to someone who skipped the passage.
**Summarize** the whole passage in exactly two sentences.

## 6. Write to read (6 min)

In 4–6 sentences: pick ONE of the four "rooms" you already do some version of in real life (you
compare prices before buying something = data reading; you decide whether to trust a stranger's
online review = source credibility) and describe, honestly, one specific time your instinct
failed you — a time you believed something you shouldn't have. This is your personal baseline;
you'll return to it in Session 14.

## 7. Stamina/speed baseline (4 min)

Using your start/end time from Section 4, calculate your reading rate for that passage
(≈520 words) in words per minute. Record it. Target by Session 14: read a similar C1 passage at
a comfortable, comprehending pace **and** be able to explain, afterward, when you deliberately
slowed down and why (Level 7 is not about reading everything fast — it's about choosing your
speed on purpose, taught explicitly in Session 14).

## Check (4 min)

Self-score against this list — you should be able to say, right now, without rereading:

1. Give a correct one-sentence definition of *infer*, *corroborate*, and *bias* — 3/3 ✓.
2. State, without looking, the four questions a specialist reader asks of any source (who's
   speaking / what's their evidence / does something independent confirm it / what would change
   my mind) — approximate wording is fine.
3. State your baseline WPM and one honest example from Section 6.

If you can do all three, you're ready for Session 2. If not, reread Section 4 once more before
moving on — this session is the spine the other 13 hang off.

**Tutor notes:** Young advanced readers (a strong 11–13-year-old) handle this passage fine but
may need "press release" and "lease" glossed with one real-life example each — use a phone
contract or a school permission slip as the concrete referent. Adults with a technical/trade
background often already do the "science" or "contract" room professionally without naming it;
let them name their own real-world version in Section 6 rather than forcing the four listed
examples.

**Decodability/vocabulary audit:** New academic vocabulary this session: infer, corroborate,
bias. All other vocabulary assumed known from Level 6 exit or glossed inline above (disciplinary,
independent, unverified, thesis).
