# L7.14 — Reading Stamina & Speed; Course Capstone Bridge

**Goal:** Practice choosing your reading speed on purpose — skim, scan, or deep-read — and
sustain a long stretch of deep reading with comprehension; review the level before the mastery
check.
**Time:** 45 min · **Materials:** notebook, timer.
**Research base:** Allington-line research on reading volume/stamina, treated as good practice
with acknowledged uncertain causal strength (research/06 §1.10).

## 1. Retrieval warm-up (3 min)

From memory: define *unreliable narrator* and *connotation* (Session 13).

## 2. Word work (4 min)

- **Skim** — moving quickly over a whole text to get the general shape/gist (what's this about,
  roughly, and is it worth reading closely?) — not aiming to catch every detail.
- **Scan** — moving quickly through a text looking for one specific piece of information (a date,
  a name, a number) — not reading for overall meaning at all.
- **Deep reading** — slow, careful, often reread reading, aimed at full comprehension of a dense
  or important passage — the mode this whole level has mostly used.

## 3. Prime the topic (3 min)

Two facts before you read: **(1)** skilled readers don't read everything at the same speed — they
switch gears constantly, often without noticing they're doing it, based on what they actually need
from a given text. **(2)** sustained reading stamina (staying focused through 30+ minutes of dense
text) is a trainable capacity that tends to correlate with reading achievement, even though the
exact causal direction is still debated (research/06 §1.10). **Orienting question:** think of the
last time you had to read something long and dense (a manual, a syllabus, an assignment) —
did you read the whole thing at one speed, or did your pace change depending on the section?

## 4. Meet the move: choosing your speed on purpose (5 min)

An advanced reader doesn't read everything at one speed. Before reading anything substantial, ask:
*what do I actually need from this text?* A general sense (skim), one fact (scan), or full
understanding (deep read)? Then match your pace to the actual goal — reading a whole document
slowly to find one phone number wastes time; skimming a contract you're about to sign is a real
risk (Session 9).

## 5. Practice — matching speed to purpose (10 min)

Below are three short tasks using the same source text. Time yourself on each and note how your
approach changed.

> **Source — a notice**
>
> COMMUNITY POOL — SUMMER HOURS NOTICE. Effective June 15 through Labor Day, the community pool
> will be open Monday–Friday 6:00 AM–8:00 PM and weekends 8:00 AM–7:00 PM, with the exception of
> the last Wednesday of each month, when the pool closes at 2:00 PM for scheduled maintenance.
> Lifeguard-supervised swim lessons run Tuesday and Thursday mornings, 9:00–11:00 AM, ages 5 and
> up, registration required two weeks in advance at the front desk; drop-in lap swim is available
> during all lifeguarded hours without registration. The pool will be fully closed July 4th for
> the community holiday event. Guests are limited to two per member household per visit during
> peak hours (weekends and weekdays after 4:00 PM); a valid membership card is required for entry
> at all times, and lost cards may be replaced at the front desk for a $5 fee. Pool rules,
> including no diving in the shallow end and no glass containers on deck, are posted at the
> entrance and apply to all visitors regardless of membership status.

- **Task A (scan, 15 seconds):** What time does the pool close on the last Wednesday of the month?
- **Task B (scan, 15 seconds):** What is the fee to replace a lost membership card?
- **Task C (skim, 20 seconds):** In one sentence, what is this notice generally about — not every
  detail, just the gist?
- **Task D (deep read, untimed):** A member wants to bring three friends' kids swimming on a
  Saturday afternoon for a lesson without registering ahead of time. List every rule from the
  notice that affects whether this plan works as described.

## 6. Sustained deep reading (18–20 min)

Read the following passage once, slowly, without stopping to answer questions yet — this is the
level's stamina target: 30–40 minutes of sustained focus on dense text (a shorter version here so
the session fits 45 minutes; extend the time and add a second passage if building toward a longer
target).

> **The Weight of a Decision You Didn't Notice Making**
>
> Most people, asked to name the biggest decision of their life, reach for the obvious candidates:
> a marriage, a career change, a move to a new city. Fewer people notice the decisions that were
> never really decided at all — the ones that accumulated, one unremarkable choice at a time,
> until a shape had formed that nobody had deliberately drawn.
>
> Consider a habit as ordinary as checking a phone first thing in the morning. No one sits down
> and decides, formally, "I will now spend the first ninety seconds of every day absorbing
> whatever notification arrived overnight, before I have had a single unprompted thought of my
> own." And yet that is, in practice, the decision that gets made, daily, by the simple failure to
> make any decision at all — the phone is there, reaching for it costs nothing in the moment, and
> the accumulated cost only becomes visible zoomed out, across a year, in a way no single morning
> ever reveals.
>
> Behavioral researchers have a name for the gap this exploits: **present bias** — the well-
> documented tendency to weigh a cost or benefit happening right now far more heavily than an
> identical cost or benefit happening later, even when, calculated honestly, the two should carry
> equal weight. Present bias is not a character flaw unique to distracted people; it is close to
> a universal feature of how human decision-making works, which is exactly why it is so
> effectively exploited, deliberately, by the design of anything competing for attention — a
> notification is engineered to make "later" (finishing the thought you were having, sleeping
> fully) lose to "now" (checking) every single time the two compete, because the app's success is
> measured by exactly how often "now" wins.
>
> None of this means the fix is willpower, applied harder, in the moment of temptation — present
> bias, by definition, is precisely the bias that makes in-the-moment willpower an unreliable
> defense, since the moment itself is engineered to feel low-stakes. The more durable fix,
> according to the same research tradition, is structural: decisions made in advance, at a calm
> moment, that remove the in-the-moment choice altogether — a phone charging outside the bedroom,
> not a phone charging on the nightstand with a promise not to check it; a specific, scheduled
> time for messages, not an open-ended "I'll just glance quickly" available at every idle moment.
> The insight generalizes well past phones: nearly any habit someone describes as a failure of
> willpower is, on closer inspection, better described as a structural decision that was never
> made — an environment left to default toward whatever is easiest in the moment, which is rarely
> the same as whatever the person, asked calmly in advance, would actually choose.
>
> This is, in its own way, exactly the disciplinary-reading lesson this level has spent thirteen
> sessions building toward, aimed inward instead of outward: the same habit of asking "what's
> actually driving this outcome — the obvious surface reason, or something structural and less
> visible underneath it" that a historian applies to a deposition, a scientist applies to a
> correlation, and a critical reader applies to a headline, applies with equal force to a
> person's own daily habits, if they're willing to read their own life with the same disciplined
> skepticism they've just spent this level learning to apply to everyone else's claims.

**Questions (answer without rereading, to test what stuck from one careful pass):**

1. State the passage's central claim in one sentence.
2. Define **present bias** in your own words, using the passage's phone example.
3. Why does the passage say in-the-moment willpower is an unreliable fix for present bias
   specifically?
4. What structural fix does the passage recommend, and how is it different from "just try
   harder"?
5. **Think question:** the final paragraph explicitly connects this passage back to the whole
   level's disciplinary-reading habit. Restate that connection in your own words — what single
   question, learned across Sessions 2–13, does the passage say applies just as well to reading
   your own life?

## 7. Discussion — reciprocal roles (5 min)

**Predict:** name one of your own habits that might be a "structural decision that was never
made," in the passage's terms. **Question:** what's one structural change (not a willpower change)
you could actually make this week? **Clarify:** explain "present bias" to someone who's never
heard the term, using an example other than phones. **Summarize:** the whole passage in two
sentences.

## 8. Write to read (5 min)

Write a paragraph (5–7 sentences) naming one real structural decision in your own life you'd
change, using **present bias** correctly, and stating specifically what environmental change (not
a willpower resolution) would remove the in-the-moment choice.

## 9. Level review — bridge to the mastery check (5 min)

Before the mastery check, confirm you can, from memory, name: the historian's three moves
(sourcing, contextualizing, corroborating); the scientist's three questions (claim, method,
sample size) plus correlation-vs-causation; the four parts of an argument map plus at least 3
fallacies; the three lateral-reading questions; the four things a contract reader checks for; the
four synthesis steps; figurative language/tone/irony; unreliable narrator; and skim/scan/deep-read.
If any of these is shaky, revisit that session's Word work and Meet-the-move sections before
attempting the mastery check — do not attempt it cold.

## Check

Model answers, Section 6: (1) many important life patterns form from accumulated small
non-decisions, not one deliberate choice; (2) present bias — weighing an immediate cost/benefit
more heavily than an equal later one; (3) willpower fails because the in-the-moment choice is
engineered to feel low-stakes, exactly the condition present bias exploits; (4) remove the choice
in advance at a calm moment (charge phone outside the bedroom) rather than resist it in the
moment; (5) the same question — what's really driving this, the surface reason or something
structural underneath — that Sessions 2–13 applied to sources, studies, arguments, and contracts.

Self-check on stamina: did you complete Section 6's passage in one sustained pass without
stopping, and answer Q1–5 with reasonable accuracy from that one read? If you needed to reread
most of it, that's useful diagnostic information, not failure — note it, and build toward more
sustained daily reading time before attempting longer texts.

**Tutor notes:** For a genuinely escalating stamina program (rather than this session's one
compressed passage), have the learner add 5–10 minutes of self-selected, appropriately challenging
independent reading daily between now and the mastery check, tracking minutes in a simple log —
per research/06 §1.10, frame this as good practice worth doing regardless of the exact causal
strength of the volume-comprehension link, not as a guaranteed-effect intervention.
