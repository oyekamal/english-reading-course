# L7.08 — Lateral Reading & Source Credibility

**Goal:** Practice the single most important habit found missing in the Stanford History
Education Group's national study of student online reasoning: actually leaving the page to check
who's behind it, on a real organization, before trusting what it says — not just reasoning about
it in the abstract.
**Time:** 40 min · **Materials:** a phone, tablet, or computer with internet access — this is
required by default this session (see Section 5); the no-device version is a fallback only, in
the tutor notes.
**Research base:** SHEG/Wineburg et al. 2019/2021 national study of civic online reasoning
(research/06 §1.9); the Civic Online Reasoning (COR) curriculum's own practice format, which
trains the skill on real websites rather than constructed examples.

## 1. Retrieval warm-up (3 min)

From memory: name three of the six fallacies from Session 7.

## 2. Word work (5 min)

- **Lateral reading** — instead of staying on a webpage and judging it only by how it looks
  ("vertical" reading, top to bottom), you open new tabs to check who runs the site, what other
  sources say about it, and whether it's known for accuracy — **before** deciding whether to trust
  what's on the page.
- **Credible** — worthy of belief because of a track record of accuracy, transparency about
  sources, and accountability for errors. Credibility is earned by an organization or person over
  time, not claimed by a confident tone.
- **Conflict of interest** — a hidden or undisclosed financial or personal stake that could bias
  an organization's or person's conclusions, even if every individual fact they state is technically
  accurate.

## 3. Prime the topic (3 min)

One fact before you start: in SHEG's 2019 national study of nearly 3,500 U.S. students, **96% did
not factor a website's bias into whether they trusted it**, and most never left the page at all to
check (research/06 §1.9). Today you will physically do the thing that study found almost no one
does by default. **Orienting question:** think of the last time you searched for health, product,
or nutrition information online — did you check who ran the site you landed on, or just read what
it said?

## 4. Meet the moves — 3 lateral reading questions (3 min)

Before believing anything a website claims about itself or its topic, ask: **(1) Who is behind
this** — what organization, and who funds it? **(2) What do independent sources say about this
organization's track record** — search the organization's name plus "funding" or "bias" or
"fact-check" in a new tab, don't just read their own "About" page. **(3) Is this specific piece
functioning as advertising or advocacy**, even if it doesn't say so in large letters?

## 5. The real exercise — lateral-read two real organizations (18 min)

**Do this for real, right now, with a device.** You are going to check two real, currently active
organizations that regularly publish claims about health, food, and chemical safety. Do not read
about them below first — search each one cold, the way you would if you'd landed on their content
by accident.

**Target A — search: "American Council on Science and Health" funding**

Open a new tab. Search that exact phrase. Skim the first several results (not just the
organization's own site) and note: who founded it, what topics it covers, and what independent
sources (news investigations, watchdog sites, media-literacy organizations) say about its funding.

**Target B — search: "FactCheck.org" about funding**

Open a new tab. Search that. Note: what organization runs it, how it's funded, and what
independent media-bias/media-literacy raters say about its reliability.

**Now answer, using what you actually found:**

1. For Target A: name the organization that founded it and at least one type of industry
   (food/beverage, chemical, pharmaceutical, tobacco) that independent reporting says has funded
   it. Does the organization's own website disclose this as prominently as the independent
   reporting does?
2. For Target B: name the university/institution it's a project of, and how independent
   media-bias raters generally describe its reliability and funding transparency compared to
   Target A.
3. Both organizations use "science" or "fact" in ways that sound authoritative. Based only on what
   you found by actually searching (not the name alone), which one would you trust more on a
   contested food-safety or chemical-safety claim, and why — cite the specific independent source
   you found, not just an impression.
4. **Think question:** Target A's own homepage does not read as obviously biased — no dramatic
   claims, no "buy our supplement" link. Why is "the page itself looks neutral" not enough,
   on its own, to establish credibility — and what did leaving the page actually add that staying
   on it never would have?

## 6. Expected findings (compare after you've searched — don't read this before Section 5)

**Target A (American Council on Science and Health):** independent reporting — most notably a
2013 *Mother Jones* investigation based on leaked internal documents, plus watchdog summaries at
SourceWatch and DeSmog, and Wikipedia's own sourced funding section — documents that ACSH has
received substantial funding over the years from food, beverage, chemical, and pharmaceutical
companies (reporting names Coca-Cola, PepsiCo, and chemical/oil firms among past donors), while
publicly presenting itself as an independent science-advocacy nonprofit and, per reporting, having
stopped disclosing its donor list. Media Bias/Fact Check and similar raters flag it for a strong
pro-industry lean on food and chemical safety topics specifically.

**Target B (FactCheck.org):** it is a project of the Annenberg Public Policy Center at the
University of Pennsylvania, funded by an endowment, and its own stated policy is that it does not
accept funding from corporations, unions, political parties, or advocacy groups. Independent
media-bias raters (e.g., AllSides) generally rate it as nonpartisan/high on factual reporting.

Neither organization is being singled out arbitrarily — both are real, current, and this exact
funding information is publicly documented by independent journalism and watchdog sources, not by
either organization's opponents alone (see the pinned source note below).

## 7. Discussion — reciprocal roles (5 min)

**Predict:** if you searched a claim from Target A on a specific food-safety topic, what would you
now expect an independent scientific source (e.g., a university extension site or a government
health agency) to say, based on what you now know about Target A's funding pattern? **Question:**
what one piece of information, if Target A's homepage included it, would most change how you read
its content? **Clarify:** explain lateral reading to someone who has never heard the term, using
what you just did with Targets A and B as the example. **Summarize:** state which of the two you'd
trust more on a contested claim and the single strongest reason why, citing your actual search.

## 8. Write to read (5 min)

Write a paragraph (5–7 sentences): describe what you found searching Target A and Target B, and
what changed between your first impression (reading each site's own "About" section) and your
lateral-read impression (reading independent sources about them). Use **lateral reading** and
**conflict of interest** correctly at least once each.

## Check

Model answer core points: Target A presents as neutral science advocacy but independent,
well-sourced reporting documents a real, undisclosed pattern of industry funding directly relevant
to the topics it covers — a textbook conflict of interest. Target B discloses its funding
structure and independent raters corroborate its nonpartisan reputation. The key shift is: reading
only each organization's own page gives a false impression of rough equivalence ("both call
themselves credible science/fact sources"); leaving the page and checking independent sources is
what actually separates them.

Self-check: could you name all three lateral-reading questions from memory, and state, using your
own actual search results (not a guess), one specific independent source for each organization's
funding/reliability reputation?
