# L7.05 — Reading a Science Article II: Correlation vs. Causation & Data in Text

**Goal:** Tell the difference between "two things happen together" and "one causes the other";
read a percentage, a described graph, or a statistic used inside an argument and judge whether it
actually supports the claim built on top of it.
**Time:** 40 min · **Materials:** notebook.

## 1. Retrieval warm-up (3 min)

From memory: what three questions does a scientific reader ask of a study (Session 4)? What went
wrong with the GlowGuard press release specifically?

## 2. Word work (5 min)

- **Correlation** — when two things tend to change together (as one goes up, the other tends to
  go up or down too). *"Correlation" only means they move together — it says nothing yet about
  why.*
- **Causation** — when one thing actually makes the other happen. Establishing causation usually
  needs a controlled experiment, not just an observed pattern.
- **Confounding variable** (also called a "third factor" or "lurking variable") — a hidden factor
  that causes both things in a correlation to move together, making them look connected to each
  other when they're really both connected to it. Your turn: if "houses near fire stations burn
  down more often" turned out to be true, what confounding variable might actually explain it
  (fire stations are placed in denser, older neighborhoods with more fire risk to begin with)?

## 3. Prime the topic (3 min)

Two facts before you read: **(1)** almost any two things that both rise and fall with the
seasons, the economy, or a population's growth will show some correlation, whether or not either
one causes the other. **(2)** journalists and advertisers both have an incentive to state a
correlation as if it were a proven cause, because "X causes Y" is a much more compelling sentence
than "X and Y happen to move together." **Orienting question:** recall a headline you've seen
that claimed one thing "causes" another — would "is linked to" have been the more honest word?

## 4. Meet the move: the correlation/causation check (4 min)

Whenever a text claims "A causes B," ask three questions: **(1)** Is this actually a controlled
experiment (one thing was deliberately changed, everything else held constant), or just two
numbers that happened to move together over time? **(2)** Could B actually cause A instead (the
arrow reversed)? **(3)** Is there a **confounding variable** driving both A and B independently?

## 5. Close reading — a real, classic example (14 min)

> **Ice Cream and Drowning**
>
> Every summer, two numbers rise together in most countries with cold winters: ice cream sales
> and the number of people who drown. Track this across any year and the two lines climb and fall
> in almost the same shape, month by month. If you only looked at the correlation, you could
> write a headline: "Ice Cream Sales Linked to Drowning Deaths" — and it would be, strictly, true:
> the two numbers do correlate.
>
> Nobody seriously believes ice cream causes drowning, or that drowning makes people crave ice
> cream. The real explanation is a confounding variable: hot weather. Hot weather makes people buy
> more ice cream, and hot weather also makes far more people go swimming — which is when almost
> all drownings happen. Ice cream and drowning are correlated only because they share a common
> cause; neither one causes the other at all.
>
> This example is famous precisely because it's obvious once explained — which is exactly why it's
> useful. The same structure hides inside far less obvious, far more consequential claims: a study
> might find that people who take a particular vitamin have fewer heart attacks, and a headline
> might imply the vitamin protects the heart — when the real explanation could be that people who
> already exercise, don't smoke, and see a doctor regularly are more likely to take vitamins in
> the first place, and it's the exercise and non-smoking doing the protective work, not the pill.

**Questions:**

1. Explain, in your own words, why ice cream sales and drowning deaths correlate without either
   one causing the other.
2. In the vitamin/heart-attack example, name the possible confounding variable(s).
3. **Think question:** the passage says this "hides inside far less obvious... claims." Why is an
   obvious example like ice cream/drowning actually more useful for learning the *pattern* than a
   realistic-but-murky one would be on its own?

## 6. Close reading — data in text (8 min)

Now read a short passage that puts a statistic inside an argument, and judge it.

> A local news segment reports: "Since the new speed cameras went up on Elm Street last year,
> accidents are down 40%! Clearly the cameras are working." What the segment does not mention:
> Elm Street also had a new stop sign installed the same month, a nearby school closed (cutting
> morning traffic by roughly a third), and the city-wide accident rate — across streets with no
> cameras at all — also fell about 25% that year, likely due to a mild winter with less ice.

**Questions:**

4. What is the segment's causal claim, and what's the correlation it's actually built on?
5. Name at least two confounding variables the passage itself supplies.
6. Given the city-wide 25% drop with no cameras anywhere, roughly how much of Elm Street's 40%
   drop might the cameras actually explain — all of it, none of it, or something in between?
   Defend your estimate using the numbers given.

## 7. Discussion — reciprocal roles (5 min)

**Predict:** name one confounding variable you'd want ruled out before believing a claim like
"kids who play chess get better grades." **Question:** what's one real headline you've seen that
you now suspect confuses correlation with causation? **Clarify:** explain the difference between
"correlation" and "causation" to someone who's never heard either word. **Summarize:** two
sentences on the speed camera example.

## 8. Write to read (5 min)

Write a paragraph (5–7 sentences): pick either the ice-cream/drowning or speed-camera example and
explain what would need to be true for the causal claim to actually be well-supported (e.g., a
controlled trial: cameras installed on some streets and not others, similar in every other way,
compared over the same period). Use **correlation**, **causation**, and **confounding variable**
correctly at least once each.

## Check

Model answer for the speed-camera estimate: since city-wide accidents fell ~25% with no cameras
anywhere, at most roughly 15 percentage points of Elm Street's 40% drop (40 − 25) can plausibly be
attributed to the cameras specifically — and even that residual could still be explained by the
stop sign or the school closure, so the honest answer is "the cameras may have helped some, but
this data cannot show how much, or whether they helped at all."

Self-check: can you correctly identify the confounding variable in both examples without
rereading, and explain in one sentence why "correlation" alone never proves "causation"?

**Tutor notes:** The ice-cream/drowning pairing is real, widely documented seasonal data (both
genuinely track with summer heat) and is used here purely as the teaching example, not as a
cited research finding requiring its own citation. Watch for learners who overcorrect into "no
correlation ever means anything" — the correct lesson is that correlation is a starting clue
that demands more evidence, not proof and not noise.
