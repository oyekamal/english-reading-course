# Level 7 — Advanced & Critical Reading

**Anchor:** CEFR C1 (stretch C2) · PIAAC Literacy Level 4–5
**Sessions:** 14 · **Time:** 35–50 min each · **Format:** tutor-led, parent-led, or self-study (self-study notes in every session)
**Research base:** `research/06-comprehension-advanced.md` (disciplinary literacy §1.8; lateral reading §1.9; syntax §1.5; writing-to-read §1.7; CEFR/PIAAC finish line §1.11/§5)

## Entry requirement

Finished Level 6 (Reading to Learn): comfortably reads B1–B2 text, can name the 5 expository text
structures (sequence, cause/effect, compare/contrast, problem/solution, description), runs
reciprocal-teaching roles (predict/question/clarify/summarize) with light facilitation, writes a
paragraph summary after an extended text, and has met a first, brief lateral-reading routine.

If a learner has **not** done Level 6 but reads fluently and answers literal + simple inferential
questions on B1 text with ≥85% accuracy, they can start here — assign the Level 6 lateral-reading
mini-unit as a catch-up first (ask a tutor/see `course/level-6/` once built).

## Exit — this is the course finish line

A learner who completes Level 7 and passes the mastery check can, independently and without being
told which move to use:

1. **Read a primary historical source** and state where it came from, what the author's stake in
   the event was, and what other sources would need to say to corroborate or contradict it
   (sourcing, contextualizing, corroborating — SHEG "Reading Like a Historian" moves).
2. **Read a science article or abstract** and identify the actual claim, the method used to test
   it, the sample size, and whether the write-up is illegitimately turning a correlation into a
   causal claim.
3. **Read an argumentative essay or op-ed** and map its claim, its reasons, its evidence, whether
   it addresses a real counterargument, and name any logical fallacy it leans on.
4. **Read a contract, policy, or terms-of-service document** and state, in their own words, what
   they are agreeing to, what conditions apply, and what is buried in the fine print.
5. **Read data described in words** (percentages, a graph described in prose, a statistic used in
   an argument) and say whether the number supports the claim built on top of it.
6. **Open new tabs to check a source before trusting it** — lateral reading — without being
   prompted, the way SHEG's national study found most people do not (research/06 §1.9).
7. **Synthesize 2–4 sources with contrasting viewpoints** into one written answer that
   represents each side fairly and states where they actually disagree.
8. **Read literary text for figurative language, tone, irony, and an unreliable narrator**, and
   explain how the language creates a meaning beyond the literal words — including a short poem.
9. **Switch reading speed on purpose** — skim to find a fact, scan for a specific term, or slow
   down for a dense paragraph — and sustain deep-reading focus on a stretch of complex text for
   30–40 minutes.
10. **Use academic (Tier-2/Tier-3) vocabulary** met across this level correctly in their own
    writing (e.g., *corroborate, infer, fallacy, bias, correlation, causation, obligation,
    clause, synthesis, connotation, irony, credible, context, primary source, methodology*).

This is CEFR C1 ("can understand in detail lengthy, complex texts... provided he/she can reread
difficult sections") stretching toward C2 on literary/figurative text, and PIAAC Level 4–5
("search/integrate/evaluate across multiple dense texts; construct syntheses of similar/
contrasting viewpoints; evaluate evidence-based arguments" — research/06 §1.11).

## Design notes (why it's built this way)

- **Disciplinary literacy, not more generic strategy worksheets.** Shanahan & Shanahan (2008,
  research/06 §1.8) found generic content-area strategies plateau around middle school; advanced
  readers need discipline-specific moves — how a historian reads differs from how a scientist
  reads differs from how a lawyer reads. Sessions 2–9 are organized by discipline, not by a
  reused "5 reading strategies" checklist.
- **Strategy dosage stays brief.** Any strategy taught (sourcing, fallacy-spotting, lateral
  reading) gets explicit modeling in one session, then is folded into real texts for the rest of
  the level — never re-taught as a standalone drill (Willingham 2006/2014; Okkinga 2018,
  research/06 §1.1, §5.1).
- **Lateral reading is mandatory, not optional**, per SHEG's national finding that 96% of
  students did not factor in site bias and two-thirds could not spot labeled sponsored content
  (research/06 §1.9). Session 8 teaches it explicitly; Sessions 10–11 require it.
- **Writing-to-read every session** — every extended text ends in a written response
  (Graham & Hebert 2010, ES 0.40–0.77, research/06 §1.7). Never skip the Write step to save time.
- **Text stays above comfortable level, with scaffolding** (glossing, pre-teaching academic
  vocabulary, chunking, guided reread) rather than "just right" leveled text — Shanahan's
  complex-text stance (research/06 §1.6), flagged as a design choice, not settled science.
- **Two tracks collapse into one from here.** Per DESIGN.md §4, from Level 6 one text set works
  for teens and adults; Level 7 is fully one-track (advanced readers of any age read the same
  primary sources, articles, and literature — a capable 12-year-old and a 45-year-old adult are
  doing the same work here). A tutor working with a younger advanced reader should pre-read
  Session 3 (Boston Massacre depositions include period violence description) and Session 12–13
  (Chopin and Poe touch death/grief; both are standard school-anthology texts) and judge fit.
- **Reading volume/stamina escalates across the level**, ending at 30–40 minutes of sustained
  silent reading in Session 14, per Allington-line research on volume/stamina (research/06
  §1.10), while being explicit with tutors that the causal strength of "more volume →
  better comprehension" is not fully settled (research/06 §4) — treat it as good practice, not
  a magic lever on its own.

## Sequence

| # | Session | Discipline / focus | Text(s) |
|---|---|---|---|
| 7.01 | Orientation & baseline | What "advanced reader" means; academic vocabulary strand; stamina/speed baseline | Diagnostic passage (original, C1) |
| 7.02 | Reading like a historian I | Sourcing & contextualizing a primary source | Paul Revere's deposition, Boston Massacre (1770, public domain) |
| 7.03 | Reading like a historian II | Corroboration across conflicting accounts | Revere's deposition vs. Capt. Thomas Preston's account (public domain) |
| 7.04 | Reading a science article I | Claim, method, sample size | Original constructed research-summary article |
| 7.05 | Reading a science article II | Correlation vs. causation; data in text | Original article + ice-cream/drowning classic stats example |
| 7.06 | Reading an argumentative essay | Claim, reasons, evidence, counterargument | Original op-ed (school phone bans) |
| 7.07 | Argument evaluation | Logical fallacies; evidence strength | Original op-ed (four-day work week) |
| 7.08 | Lateral reading & source credibility | SHEG civic-online-reasoning moves | Two contrasting mock web sources (labelled, original) |
| 7.09 | Reading a contract / policy / ToS | Obligations, conditions, fine print | Original apartment lease + phone-app ToS excerpts |
| 7.10 | Synthesis I | Two sources, contrasting viewpoints | Two original op-eds (school phone bans, callback) |
| 7.11 | Synthesis II | 3–4 sources, full research question | 4 mixed sources (history + science + argument + data) |
| 7.12 | Literary reading I | Figurative language, tone, irony | Kate Chopin, "The Story of an Hour" (1894, public domain) |
| 7.13 | Literary reading II | Unreliable narrator + poetry | Poe, "The Tell-Tale Heart" (excerpt, 1843) + Frost, "The Road Not Taken" (1916, both public domain) |
| 7.14 | Stamina, speed & capstone bridge | Skim/scan vs. deep reading; sustained silent reading; review | Original C1 text (long-form) + review of Sessions 2–13 |

Then: `mastery-check.md` — the course finish line, a multi-source synthesis task with rubric.

## Materials

Paper/notebook or doc for written responses; a highlighter or two colors of pen is useful for
Sessions 2–3, 6–9; a phone or computer for Session 8 only (to actually open new tabs — if
unavailable, the tutor pre-prints two "web pages" and the learner role-plays the lateral check
verbally). No other materials required — every text is given in full in the lesson file.

## How to run a session (see DESIGN.md §4 for the L5–L7 template)

Retrieval warm-up (3–5 min) → Word work: academic vocabulary/morphology (5–7 min) → Close reading
with discipline-specific moves (15–20 min) → Discussion, reciprocal roles (7–10 min) → Write to
read (8–10 min) → Check (3–5 min, self- or tutor-scored against the model answer).
