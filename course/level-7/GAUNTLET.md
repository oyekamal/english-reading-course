### Comparison 1 — L7.02/L7.03 (history sourcing/corroboration) vs. real SHEG "Reading Like a
Historian" Boston Massacre lesson

**VERDICT: REFERENCE WINS.**

What the real lesson does that ours doesn't:
- SHEG's Boston Massacre unit opens with **visual sourcing** — students analyze competing
  engravings/illustrations of the event (a signature disciplinary-literacy move: images are
  primary sources too, and whose engraving you look at changes the story) before ever reading a
  written account. L7.02/03 never touches visual sourcing at all — a full move is missing, not
  just thinly covered.
- SHEG's document set is 3–4 items (a colonist deposition, Preston's account, John Adams' trial
  summation, an anonymous/secondary account per multiple descriptions of the unit), each run
  through the same explicit sourcing template: **who, when, why, what do you predict this will
  say**. Ours uses exactly 2 documents (Preston + Palmes) and only applies the explicit
  who/when/why template to Source A (L7.02); L7.03 skips straight to a corroboration table for
  Source B without ever asking the sourcing questions of Palmes himself — the learner is never
  asked "what is Palmes' own stake or vantage point?" before trusting his account, which is the
  opposite of the habit the lesson claims to teach.
- An expert teacher would pick the real lesson: more sources, richer sourcing practice, and it
  doesn't quietly skip the sourcing step on the second document in a *sourcing lesson*.

**Why this matters pedagogically, not just as a coverage gap:** L7.03 explicitly frames Palmes as
maximally neutral ("Palmes had no obvious reason to protect Preston... does that make his account
more or less trustworthy than Preston's") to make a clean "one biased + one independent" teaching
point. See fact-check below — this framing is not just simplified, it is factually wrong about
Palmes, and the error is load-bearing for the lesson's own argument.

### Comparison 2 — L7.08 (lateral reading) vs. real Civic Online Reasoning "Lateral Reading"
curriculum

**VERDICT: REFERENCE WINS.**

- COR's actual lessons have students lateral-read **real organizations and real websites** using
  **real tools** (a fact-checking org, Wikipedia and its references, a live search) — the skill is
  practiced on the open internet, which is the whole point (staying on-page vs. leaving it).
  COR's own assessment battery runs three parallel task types (Website Reliability, Webpage
  Comparison, Evaluating Videos) with pre/post parallel forms for real measurement of transfer.
- L7.08 uses two **invented** mock pages and marks the one thing that makes lateral reading
  lateral reading — actually opening a new tab and searching — as **optional** ("a phone or
  computer (optional but ideal)... if unavailable, the tutor pre-prints two 'web pages' and the
  learner role-plays the lateral check verbally"). A learner can complete this session, pass its
  Check, and never once have left a page to verify anything — the exact behavior SHEG/COR's
  research says 96% of students fail to do by default. The session teaches learners to reason
  *about* lateral reading, not necessarily to *do* it.
- There's also only one practice round here (two constructed pages), vs. COR's repeated,
  varied-format practice — a single exposure to an invented pair of pages is a thin base for a
  transferable habit the mastery check (A5) and Sessions 10–11 then lean on hard.

### FACT-CHECK findings (Round 2, new)

**1. CRITICAL — the Round 1 "fix" to L7.03's Palmes account is itself factually backwards, and
reintroduces the exact class of error Round 1 was correcting.**

L7.03 (lines ~64–75) now has Palmes testify: he was "aiming a blow of my own cudgel at the
Captain [Preston], I slipped on the icy ground, and my blow landed instead on Montgomery's
arm — whether before or exactly as his musket went off, I could not swear with certainty." The
GAUNTLET Round 1 "builder" section states this was "verified against Hugh Montgomery's Wikipedia
entry and the teachtnhistory.org trial-testimony compilation."

Independently re-verified via WebFetch/search this round, and it does not hold up:

- **Wikipedia's own Boston Massacre article** (fetched directly): "Palmes swung his cudgel first
  at **Montgomery**, hitting his arm, and then at **Preston**. He narrowly missed Preston's head,
  striking him on the arm instead" — i.e., two separate, deliberate swings (Montgomery first, on
  purpose; Preston second, narrowly missed) — not one swing at Preston that slipped and
  accidentally landed on Montgomery. The lesson has the target and the "slip" attached to the
  wrong blow.
- **Timing is also backwards.** Wikipedia and multiple independent sources (thehistoryjunkie.com,
  revwartalk.com) place Palmes' strike on Montgomery **after** Montgomery had already been struck
  by ice, stumbled, and fired the first shot — not "before or exactly as" the shot, as the lesson
  states. The actual deposition text (bostonmassacre.net/trial/d-palmes.htm, a primary-source
  transcription) is even more specific: Palmes struck the soldier **after** the first shot, in
  response to the soldier trying to bayonet him — a deliberate defensive counter-strike, not an
  accidental miss.
- **Most damning: the digitalhistory.uh.edu PDF that the lesson's own source note cites** as a
  supporting source states: "Richard Palmes... swung his club, striking Montgomery in the arm.
  Palmes **slipped as he aimed a blow at the Captain's head** and struck him on the arm" — i.e.,
  the cited source itself describes two blows (deliberate hit on Montgomery, then a slipped blow
  that hit *Preston's* arm), directly contradicting the lesson's one-blow, hits-Montgomery-by-
  accident-while-aiming-at-Preston version. The lesson appears to have conflated two separate,
  well-documented blows into one, and swapped which one involved the slip.
- **A source titled specifically to warn against this exact error was available and not
  followed.** Boston1775.blogspot.com's post "The Distortions of Richard Palmes" (2013) — which
  Round 1 itself lists as a verification source — states in its own text (confirmed via search
  snippet): "**Palmes's testimony was very clear: he swung his cane after the first shot.**" The
  blog's entire subject is correcting popular accounts that misattribute causal responsibility to
  Palmes for the shooting — which is precisely the reading the lesson's current framing invites
  ("Palmes... may have precipitated the shooting he then testified about" — Round 1's own summary
  of its fix). Round 1 cited a source that argues against the very claim it used it to support.

**Why this matters more than a simple factual slip:** this is a disciplinary-literacy lesson whose
entire teaching point is "source and corroborate every document, including ones with a stake."
The fix that was supposed to model this — teaching learners that Palmes had a real, nameable
stake — did so by inventing an incorrect stake narrative (that his own swing coincided with or
caused the shooting) rather than the true one (he struck a soldier in retaliation, after the
shooting had already started, in a chaotic scene with multiple thrown objects). A learner who
actually does the lateral-reading habit Session 8 teaches and looks this up will find the
course's own account contradicted, again — the same failure mode as Round 1's Finding 1
(loc.gov), now inside the fix for Round 1's Finding 2.
