# L3.16 — -ild, -ind, -old, -ost

**Goal:** Read words ending in **-ild, -ind, -old, -ost**, a small set of closed-syllable-*looking* words where the vowel is actually long, not short.
**New today:** -ild (wild, child), -ind (kind, find), -old (cold, gold), -ost (most, post)
**Review:** all Level 3 vowel teams so far
**Heart words:** none new — all 16 Level 3 heart words were completed at L3.08; today is decoding-only.
**Time:** 25–30 min
**Materials:** paper, pencil

## 1. Warm-up review (2–3 min)
Read: moon, book, look, food, good. Heart words: answer, buy, water.

## 2. Hear it (2 min)
Say *wind* (the moving-air kind, short i) vs *find* (long i). "Same letters -ind, but two different words rely on context of the whole word family — actually, listen again: kind, find, mind, blind, remind — do these all have the same vowel sound?" (Yes, long i — except the noun "wind," which is genuinely an exception, flag it explicitly.)

## 3. Meet it (3 min)
"Normally, a vowel followed by two consonants is **closed** and short (as in 'mist,' 'lost'). But four small word-families break that rule and keep the vowel **long** even though it looks closed: **-ild** (wild, child, mild), **-ind** (kind, find, mind, blind — but NOT the word 'wind' meaning moving air, which stays short — a true, flagged exception), **-old** (cold, gold, hold, fold, told, sold), **-ost** (most, post, host — but NOT 'cost' or 'lost,' which stay short — another flagged exception)." Model contrasting pairs: *lost (short) / most (long)*, *wind-the-air (short) / find (long)*.
**Flex-decode note:** for any new word in these four families, try the **long** vowel first (that's the pattern for most family members), then check — if it's not a real/known word, or if you already know it as an exception (wind, cost, lost), use the short vowel instead.
**Urdu-speaker tip:** these are genuine, named exceptions within English's own spelling system — even highly literate native English speakers just learn this short list; it is not a sign of a gap in the learner's understanding of the rules taught so far.

## 4. Blend it (5 min)
**-ild:** wild, child, mild
**-ind (long):** kind, find, mind, blind, behind
**-old:** cold, gold, hold, fold, told, sold, bold
**-ost (long):** most, post, host
**Exceptions to notice, short vowel:** wind (moving air), cost, lost
**Pseudowords:** grild, plind, snold, trost, flild

## 5. Spell it (4 min)
Dictate: 1) cold 2) find 3) most 4) "I am cold, so I will find my coat."

## 6. Heart word review (2 min)
No new heart word today. Flash all 16 Level 3 heart words (once, only, very, every, great, eye, busy, people, water, laugh, walk, talk, buy, answer, whole, earth) for instant recognition.

## 7. Read it (5–8 min)

### Track A (child)
**The Wild, Cold Day**

> It is a wild, cold day, and Sam and his mom go for a walk. / They see a child with a gold kite, and the wind is strong. / "Hold my hand," said Mom, "because the wind is wild today." / Sam is glad she told him to hold on. / They walk the whole way home, and Sam feels most glad once they are back and warm.

Questions: 1) What does the child have? (literal) 2) Why does Mom tell Sam to hold her hand? (literal) 3) What do you do on a wild, cold day? (think)

### Track B (adult)
**Finding a Cold-Weather Job**

> Kate lost her old job when the shop closed, so she must find a new one. / Because she is bold, she does not quit — she sends her whole plan to ten shops. / One shop gets back to her and gives her a spot to start. / "I am so glad I did not lose hope," said Kate, most glad to have a new start. / Kate holds on to the good news the whole day.

Questions: 1) Why does Kate need to find a new job? (literal) 2) What does Kate do to find work? (literal) 3) What helps you stay bold when things get hard? (think)

## 8. Listen & Talk (5 min)
Read aloud: *"When a whole industry changes quickly, many workers must adapt by learning a new skill, which can feel daunting at first but often opens up options a person had not considered before."*
Tier-2 word: **daunting** — friendly definition: "something that feels big and a bit scary to start." Examples: "Learning a new language can feel daunting at first." "The exam felt daunting, but she prepared well." Your turn: "Tell me about something daunting you did anyway."
Discussion: 1) Why might learning a new skill feel daunting? 2) What's something daunting you're proud you tried?

## 9. Check (2 min)
Real: cold, find, most, wild, gold. Pseudo: grild, plind, snold, trost, flild. Dictated: "It is cold, so I will hold my whole coat."
**≥90%?** → L3.17 (Review). **<90%?** → Reteach the four families as a set with the short-vowel exceptions named explicitly (wind/cost/lost); re-check with fresh words.

## Tutor notes
- **Common error:** applying the short vowel to every -ild/-ind/-old/-ost word (reading "cold" to rhyme with "sold" — wait, that's correct; error example instead: reading "gold" with a short o, like "god"). Correction: "try the long vowel first for this family — check if it makes a known word."
- **Common error:** forgetting the named exceptions and reading "wind," "cost," or "lost" with a long vowel. Correction: these three are memorized exceptions — flag and drill them separately from the rule.
- **For adults:** this is a good moment to note that English spelling has a small, finite number of these "exception families," and this course has now covered the most common one — normalizes irregularity as bounded, not endless.
