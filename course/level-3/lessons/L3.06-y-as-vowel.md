# L3.06 — Y as a Vowel

**Goal:** Read words where final **y** acts as a vowel: /ē/ in multisyllable words (happy, funny) and /ī/ in one-syllable words (my, fly, cry).
**New today:** y = /ē/ (end of longer words), y = /ī/ (end of one-syllable words)
**Review:** open syllables
**Heart words:** walk, talk (review: water, laugh)
**Time:** 25–30 min
**Materials:** paper, pencil

## 1. Warm-up review (2–3 min)
Read: robot, tiger, go, we, music. Heart words: water, laugh, great, eye.

## 2. Hear it (2 min)
Say *happy, funny, my, fly*. "You already know y can be a consonant (yes, yard). Today, listen for y acting like a vowel at the end of a word — sometimes it sounds like long e, sometimes like long i."

## 3. Meet it (3 min)
"Rule: if y is at the end of a **short, one-syllable** word, it says /ī/ (my, fly, cry, try, sky, why). If y is at the end of a **longer word (2+ syllables)**, it says /ē/ (happy, funny, silly, baby)." Model both, sorted into two columns.
**Flex check:** for a new/unfamiliar word ending in y, count the syllables first — that tells you which sound to try, rather than guessing.
**Urdu-speaker tip:** Urdu does not use a single letter to do double vowel duty like this — flag explicitly that context (word length) decides the sound, and this is a spelling-specific English rule.

## 4. Blend it (5 min)
**/ī/ words (1 syllable):** my, by, fly, cry, try, sky, why, spry, shy
**/ē/ words (2+ syllables):** happy, funny, silly, baby, puppy, sunny, muddy, windy, sticky, dusty
**Pseudowords:** flimy(ē), stry(ī), plemmy(ē), fladdy(ē), sladdy(ē)

## 5. Spell it (4 min)
Dictate: 1) my 2) happy 3) puppy 4) "My puppy is happy."

## 6. Heart words: walk, talk (2 min)
"Walk — the l is silent, and the 'al' says a sound not yet taught; whole word is a heart word. Talk — same silent l pattern; whole word is a heart word." Say each 3x. Use: "We walk to the shop." "We talk on the walk home."

## 7. Read it (5–8 min)

### Track A (child)
**My Puppy is Busy**

> My puppy is very busy. / He runs and hops in the sun by the shed. / My puppy is funny — when he digs in the mud, he gets sticky and muddy. / "Silly puppy!" I said, and gave him a rub. / We go home, and my puppy is happy because he is with me.

Questions: 1) What does the puppy do in the yard? (literal) 2) How does the puppy get muddy? (literal) 3) What makes you feel happy after a busy day? (think)

### Track B (adult)
**A Busy Shift**

> My shift was very busy, because I ran from the front to the back and did not stop by lunch. / When I got back, there was a note from a happy person: "Thank you, you made me very glad." / I am busy, but I am glad I had a busy, happy shift.

Questions: 1) Why was the shift described as busy? (literal) 2) What did the customer's note say? (literal) 3) What made a hard day feel worth it for you once? (think)

## 8. Listen & Talk (5 min)
Read aloud: *"A customer who takes the time to leave a kind note is showing genuine gratitude — a real, felt thanks, not just a quick 'thanks' said out of habit. Genuine gratitude tends to stick with a person much longer than a routine thank-you."*
Tier-2 word: **gratitude** — friendly definition: "a strong feeling of thanks for something someone did." Examples: "She wrote a thank-you card to show her gratitude." "He felt gratitude toward the stranger who helped him." Your turn: "Tell me about a time you felt real gratitude toward someone."
Discussion: 1) Why does a written note stick with someone longer than a quick "thanks"? 2) Who deserves your gratitude this week?

## 9. Check (2 min)
Real: my, happy, puppy, fly, funny. Pseudo: stry, flimy, sladdy, fladdy, plemmy. Dictated: "My puppy is happy."
**≥90%?** → L3.07. **<90%?** → Reteach the syllable-count check explicitly (clap syllables, then pick the sound) before re-testing.

## Tutor notes
- **Common error:** applying /ī/ to multisyllable y-words ("happ-eye"). Correction: "Clap the word — one clap or two? Two claps, longer word, so it's the /ē/ sound."
- **Common error:** applying /ē/ to one-syllable y-words ("m-ee" for "my"). Correction: same clap check, reversed.
- **For adults:** point out this rule quietly explains hundreds of common -y adjectives (funny, happy, sunny, sticky) they already use — connects new rule to existing vocabulary rather than presenting it as an isolated drill.
