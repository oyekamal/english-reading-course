# L2.02 — ch, tch

**Goal:** Read and spell words with `ch` (start, middle, or end) and `tch` (end of a
one-syllable word, right after a short vowel).
**New today:** `ch` /tʃ/, `tch` /tʃ/ (same sound, two spellings, different jobs)
**Review:** `sh`; Level 1 letters
**Heart words:** there, where
**Time:** 20–25 min
**Materials:** paper, pencil

## 1. Warm-up review (2–3 min)
Flash: `sh` words `ship, wish, cash, dish, shop`. Read 5 review words: `hush, fish,
shut, gosh, rush`.

## 2. Hear it (2 min)
Say: "Listen: **chip**. First sound?" Contrast with `ship` — /tʃ/ vs /ʃ/, both new
this week but different — have the learner feel the difference: `ch` starts with the
tongue tapping the roof of the mouth before the "sh" sound; `sh` alone has no tap.
Say pairs: *chip/ship, chop/shop, catch/cash* and ask which has the extra tap.

## 3. Meet it (3 min)
Show `ch` — say: "Two letters, one sound, at the start, middle, or end of a word:
**ch**at, ri**ch**." Then show `tch` — say: "Three letters, the SAME sound, but only
at the END of a short word, right after a short vowel you already know: ca**tch**,
fe**tch**." Give the rule plainly: "After a short vowel, at the end of a one-syllable
word, we usually write `tch`, not just `ch`" (ma**tch**, hu**tch**) — but `ch` alone
at the end is also possible in a few words (mu**ch**, su**ch**) which the learner will
just meet and know, not derive from a rule.
**Urdu-speaker tip:** /tʃ/ exists in Urdu (چ) and is easy to produce — the challenge
is purely visual/spelling: knowing when English uses `ch` versus `tch` at the end of
a word. Point at the short vowel right before the ending as the cue.

## 4. Blend it (5 min)
Real words: `chip, chat, chin, chop, chess, much, such, rich, catch, fetch, hutch,
match, witch, pitch, ditch`
Pseudowords: `chob, votch, chiss, nach, wetch`

## 5. Spell it (4 min)
Dictate: 1) sounds `/ch/` at the start, `/ch/` at the end after a short vowel
(dictate as `tch` since it follows a short vowel) 2) words: `chat`, `catch`, `much`
3) sentence: **"Much of the fish was rich."**

## 6. Heart word(s) (2 min)
- **there** — heart part: the whole word is irregular for a beginning reader (it
  contains `th`, not taught until next lesson, plus an irregular `ere`); mark it
  fully heart for now, note it points to a place ("put it there").
- **where** — heart part: same story as "there" — contains `wh` (not taught until
  L2.04) plus the same irregular `ere` ending; mark it fully heart, and point out
  "there" and "where" rhyme and share a spelling chunk, which helps memory.

## 7. Read it (5–8 min)

**Track A (child)**
> Min and Dad chat on the step. "Where did you go?" Min asks. "I ran to fetch the
> cat," Dad says. The cat sat in the hutch. Min and Dad catch the cat and shut the
> hutch. Min and Dad chat and chat. Min is rich in fun, if not in cash!

*(48 words.)*

Questions: (1) What did Dad go to fetch? (2) Where was the cat sitting? (3) What do
you think "rich in fun" means?

**Track B (adult)**
> Sam and his boss chat at the shop. "Where did you go?" the boss asks. "I had to
> fetch cash for the shop," Sam says. Sam has a job at ten. Sam's boss is not rich,
> but the shop is a big shop. Sam and his boss chat, and Sam rushes to catch the
> bus.

*(54 words.)*

Questions: (1) Where did Sam go before his job started? (2) What did Sam fetch for
the shop? (3) Why does Sam rush at the end?

## 8. Listen & Talk (5 min)
Read aloud only:

> A little cat named Pip was very **curious**. Every single day, Pip wanted to know
> what was happening on the other side of the garden wall. One afternoon, Pip's
> curious nose led her straight into a bush, where she found a family of hedgehogs
> curled up asleep! Pip was so curious that she gently poked one with her paw. The
> hedgehog woke up, uncurled, and gave a tiny, surprised squeak. Pip jumped back, but
> she was still curious — she sat and watched the hedgehogs for the rest of the
> afternoon, learning something new about her garden.

**Tier-2 word: curious** — friendly definition: "wanting to find out about something,
asking questions or looking closely because you want to know more." Examples: "She
was curious about how the clock worked, so she took it apart." "Being curious is a
great way to learn." Your turn: ask "What is something YOU are curious about?"

Discussion questions: (1) What did Pip find behind the bush? (2) Is being curious a
good thing? Why or why not?

## 9. Check (2 min)
5 real words: `chip, much, catch, fetch, chess`
5 pseudowords: `chob, votch, wetch, nach, chiss`
1 dictated word: `match`
**≥90%?** → L2.03. **<90%?** → Reteach: return to step 2, re-drill the /tʃ/ vs /ʃ/
contrast with 5 fresh minimal pairs (*chin/shin, chop/shop, catch/cash, rich/wish,
much/hush*), then repeat steps 4–9.

## Tutor notes
- **Common error:** writing `ch` at the end of every word regardless of vowel length
  (writing "mach" for "match"). Correction: always ask "short vowel right before the
  ending?" — if yes, `tch`; a few high-frequency exceptions (much, such, rich, which)
  are simply known by sight once met.
- **Common error:** confusing `ch` /tʃ/ with `sh` /ʃ/ when reading quickly.
  Correction: the tongue-tap physical cue from step 2 — have the learner touch the
  roof of their mouth right behind their teeth for `ch` and notice there's no tap for
  `sh`.
- **For adults:** the `ch`/`tch` spelling rule is genuinely useful metalinguistic
  content adults pick up quickly once stated explicitly — don't skip stating the
  rule in step 3 even though it feels like "just for kids."
