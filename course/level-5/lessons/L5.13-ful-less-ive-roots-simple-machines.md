**Goal:** Read, spell, and use words with *-ful, -less, -ive*; learn four more

### Tier-2 vocabulary

**assist** — friendly definition: "to help." Examples: "A lever assists you by
letting you use less force to lift something heavy." / "The older student assisted
the younger one with the tricky word." Generative use: "Name a tool that assists
you with a task you couldn't easily do alone."

**reduce** — friendly definition: "to make smaller or less." Examples: "A pulley
reduces the amount of force you need to lift something, by changing direction and
sharing the load." / "Wearing a helmet reduces the risk of a serious injury."
Generative use: "Name one tool or habit that reduces effort, time, or risk."

## 3. Prime the topic (3–5 min)

Before reading: if possible, show or describe a see-saw, a ramp, or a wheelbarrow.

### Track A — Simple Machines on the Playground (for a child)

A see-saw is not just for fun. It is a simple machine, called a lever. Levers are
everywhere once you notice them. A lever is a stiff bar. It turns around a fixed
point. We call that point a pivot. A lever is an effective way to lift something
heavy. You could not lift it alone. On a see-saw, the pivot is in the middle. So
both sides need about the same weight to balance.

A ramp is a different simple machine. You might see one leading up to a doorway.
We call it an inclined plane. It is careful, clever engineering, hiding as
something ordinary. Rolling a heavy object up a long, gentle ramp takes less
strength than lifting it straight up. This is true even though the object travels
farther. You are reducing the force needed. You spread the same amount of work
over a longer distance.

A wheelbarrow combines two simple machines at once. It has a wheel. The wheel
reduces friction, so it is easier to move things. It also has a lever. Those are
the handles you push down on. The wheel acts as the pivot. Next time you see a
see-saw, a ramp, or a wheelbarrow, look again. You are not looking at something
simple at all. You are looking at thoughtful, useful engineering. People figured
this out thousands of years ago. We still use it every single day.

(≈235 words. **FK grade: 3.8 · Flesch reading ease: 82.0**.)

**Comprehension check:** (1) What is the fixed point of a lever called? (2) Why does
rolling something up a ramp take less strength than lifting it straight up? (3) What
two simple machines does a wheelbarrow combine?

### Track B — Working Smarter, Not Harder: Simple Machines on the Job (for a teen/adult)

Anyone who has used a crowbar to open a stuck lid has used a simple machine. So has
anyone who has pushed a heavy load in a wheelbarrow. Understanding why these tools
work helps you use them better.

A crowbar is a lever. It helps you by multiplying the force you apply. In exchange,
your hand moves a longer distance than the load itself moves. Push the handle down
a long way. The short end near the load moves only a little. But it moves with much
more force. Where you place your hands matters too. Move your hands farther from
the pivot point, and you need even less effort for the same result.

A wheelbarrow uses the same idea in a different way. The wheel at the front acts as
the pivot. Your hands lift at the back. The load sits in between. This means you
are only supporting part of the load's weight yourself. The wheel and the ground
carry the rest. That is why a loaded wheelbarrow is much easier to push than
carrying the same weight in your arms.

None of this is just a fun fact. Anyone doing physical work, like construction,
farming, or moving furniture, gets a real advantage from knowing where to place
their hands, and why. It means less wasted effort, less strain on the body, and
often a faster result.

(≈229 words. **FK grade: 4.4 · Flesch reading ease: 83.1**.)

**Comprehension check:** (1) What does moving your hands farther from a crowbar's
pivot do? (2) Why is a loaded wheelbarrow easier to push than carrying the same
weight in your arms? (3) What three practical benefits does the text say this
understanding gives someone doing physical work?

## Tutor notes

- Common error: confusing *-ful* and *-less* when spelling from dictation (writing
  *carefull* with two *l*s, or reversing which suffix fits which meaning).
  Correction: say the meaning aloud first ("full of care" or "without care") before
  choosing the suffix, and note *-ful* keeps a single *l* even though *full* itself
  has two.
- This is the first session back to a single, standard fluency passage after three
  Readers Theatre sessions — tell the learner this explicitly; some may have
  preferred the script format and that's worth acknowledging.
- Adult learners: the crowbar/wheelbarrow physics is genuinely practical (real
  physical-work efficiency), not simplified for the sake of the lesson.
