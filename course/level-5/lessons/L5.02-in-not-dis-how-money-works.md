# L5.02 — Prefixes in-/im-/ir-/il- (not), dis- · How Money Works (barter → currency)

**Goal:** Read, spell, and use words with the "not" prefix family (*in-, im-, ir-,
il-*) and *dis-*; continue the fluency program on a new passage; keep clearing the
Fry heart-word list.
**New today:** *in-/im-/ir-/il-* ("not") and *dis-* — together with *un-*, these are
the **top 4 prefixes**, covering ~58% of all prefixed words in school reading
material (White, Sowell & Yanagihara 1989 — research/05 §1). Tier-2 words: *valuable*,
*convenient*.
**Review:** *un-, re-* — 3 quick words each (*unlock, uncertain, redo*) before moving
on.
**Time:** 30–45 min. **Materials:** paper, pencil, timer.

---

## 1. Retrieval warm-up (3–5 min)

Read: *unhappy, redo, uncertain, recycle*. Heart words (Fry 104–106): **only, little,
work**. *Little* and *work* are fully decodable (consonant-*le*; *or* r-controlled
spelling for /er/, both Level 4 patterns). *Only* has a genuine heart part: the *o*
says its letter-name sound in an unusual spot — mark just that letter, decode
*-nly* normally.

**Timed affix round (30 sec):** name as many of *un-, re-* (last session) as you
can, meaning or one example word each. Log the count — this is a rate check, not a
pass/fail gate (see README §3).

## 2. Word work (10–12 min)

### in-/im-/ir-/il- ("not") — one meaning, four spellings

Say: "This prefix means 'not.' It changes its spelling to match the first letter of
the word it attaches to — this makes the two sounds easier to say together, not a
random rule to memorize by feel: *in-* before most letters, *im-* before *m, b, p*,
*ir-* before *r*, *il-* before *l*."

| Word | Meaning check | Why this spelling |
|---|---|---|
| incorrect | not correct | starts with *c*, so *in-* |
| inactive | not active | starts with *a*, so *in-* |
| impossible | not possible | starts with *p* → *im-* |
| immature | not mature (grown-up) | starts with *m* → *im-* |
| irregular | not regular | starts with *r* → *ir-* |
| irresponsible | not responsible | starts with *r* → *ir-* |
| illegal | not legal | starts with *l* → *il-* |
| illegible | not readable (can't be read) | starts with *l* → *il-* |

Decode each: peel the prefix, read the base, blend. Dictate 3: *incorrect, impossible,
illegal* — for each, ask "why that spelling of the prefix?" before writing.

### dis- ("not" / "opposite of" / "away")

| Word | Meaning check | Spelling note |
|---|---|---|
| disagree | not agree | dis + agree |
| distrust | not trust | dis + trust |
| dishonest | not honest | dis + honest |
| disappear | go away, stop being visible | dis + appear |
| discomfort | lack of comfort | dis + comfort |
| dislike | not like | dis + like |
| disorder | lack of order, a mess | dis + order |
| disconnect | undo a connection | dis + connect |

Dictate 3: *disagree, dishonest, disappear*.

Meaning-check sentence: "The magician made the coin ______ (disappear/disappoint)."
→ *disappear* fits; discuss why *disappoint* — a real word with *dis-* — doesn't fit
this sentence even though it starts the same way.

**Self-correction drill (1 min):** say "the coin seemed to *dis-a-PEER*" (stress
shifted onto the wrong syllable) inside a full sentence and have the learner
correct it to *disappear*, using the sentence's meaning as the check, not just the
spelling. Repeat with one more word from either list.

**Word harvest:** can you find one more *in-/im-/ir-/il-* or *dis-* word inside
today's passage below? (Track A has one — *inconvenient* — in its first paragraph;
see if you can spot it before being told.)

### Tier-2 vocabulary

**valuable** — friendly definition: "worth a lot, important to keep." Examples: "Her
grandmother's ring was very valuable, both in money and in memory." / "Learning to
read is one of the most valuable things you can do." Generative use: "Name something
you own that is valuable to you, and say why."

**convenient** — friendly definition: "easy to use, doesn't cause extra trouble."
Examples: "Paying with a card is more convenient than carrying lots of coins." / "The
shop near our house is convenient because we don't have to travel far." Generative
use: "Is walking or taking a bus more convenient for you? Why?"

## 3. Prime the topic (3–5 min)

Before reading: ask "what do you already use to buy things — coins, notes, a card,
a phone?" Then give 2–3 quick facts: people didn't always have money; before it,
people traded goods directly for other goods; this session's passage explains why
that changed. Orienting question: "Can you think of a time trading directly (not
with money) would be awkward — like trying to swap your lunch for a haircut?"

## 4. Fluency & Knowledge Text (10–12 min) — phrase-cued

Model → echo → choral → **Perform it** (1–2 min: read the whole passage once more
aloud to someone — a family member, the tutor, or a voice note to a friend — as a
small performance; aim for expression that matches the content, not a flat
recitation) → independent (timed, a second and unannounced read — this is the one
that gets scored). WCPM + prosody check after.

### Track A — How Money Works (for a child)

> A long time ago, / people did not have money / like we do now. / Say a farmer /
> had extra eggs. / He needed shoes. / He had to find / a shoemaker / who wanted
> eggs / that day. / This was called *bartering*. / It means trading one thing /
> for another thing. / It worked, / but it was slow. / It was often
> inconvenient. / What if the shoemaker / did not want eggs / that week?
>
> So people started using / something everyone agreed / was valuable. / At
> first, / they used things like shells, / salt, / or metal rings. / Later, /
> countries began making coins. / They began printing paper money too. / Each
> note had a promise on it. / It said, / this piece of paper / is worth this
> much.
>
> Now the farmer / could sell his eggs / for money. / He could use that money /
> to buy shoes / from anyone. / He could buy them / anywhere. / He could buy
> them / any time. / Money did not replace bartering / because bartering was
> wrong. / It replaced bartering / because money is more convenient.
>
> Today, / many people do not even / carry coins. / They use cards or phones /
> to send money fast. / But it is still / the same idea. / People figured this
> out / thousands of years ago.

(≈192 words. **FK grade: 3.8 · Flesch reading ease: 81.8**.)

**Comprehension check:** (1) What is bartering? (2) Why did people start using coins
instead of bartering? (3) The text says money "is still the same idea" as thousands
of years ago — what idea is that?

### Track B — How Money Works (for a teen/adult, work/income framing)

> Every payday, / a worker trades hours of work / for money. / This feels normal /
> today. / But it is newer / than most people think, / and it solved / a real
> problem.
>
> Before money existed, / people bartered. / Bartering means / trading one thing /
> directly for another. / A carpenter might trade / a chair / for a sack of
> flour. / But this only worked / if the baker needed / a chair / that same
> week. / Most of the time, / the two people / did not want / the same things / at
> the same time. / So trade was slow. / Often, / it did not happen / at all.
>
> Money solved this problem. / A worker could sell / their labor / for money. /
> Then they could use that money / to buy whatever they needed, / whenever they
> needed it. / The other person / did not need to want / the worker's exact
> skill / in return. / They just had to accept money, / because everyone else /
> accepted it too.
>
> Today, / much of this happens / without cash at all. / Wages arrive / in a bank
> account / or a mobile wallet. / Payments move / with a single tap. / But the
> idea underneath / is exactly the same. / Money is a trusted token / that
> everyone agrees to accept. / That is the same idea / that solved the
> carpenter's problem, / long before banks existed.
>
> Understanding this idea / makes it easier / to understand other money
> topics too, / like saving, / borrowing, / and interest. / All of them depend /
> on one thing: / how well that trusted token / holds its value / over time.

(≈237 words. **FK grade: 5.7 · Flesch reading ease: 72.9**.)

**Comprehension check:** (1) What does "bartering" mean? (2) Why did trade using
barter often not happen at all? (3) What does the passage say money is — "a
trusted ___ that everyone agrees to accept"?

### Discussion — reciprocal roles

**Predictor** (before): "What do you think 'how money works' will explain?"
**Questioner**: one question the text answers, one it doesn't (e.g., it doesn't say
what happens when everyone stops trusting a currency).
**Clarifier**: pick one word — *bartering*, *trusted token*, *mobile wallet* — and
say how you worked out what it meant.
**Summarizer**: retell in 2–3 sentences.

## 5. Write to read (5 min)

Write 2–3 sentences answering: "Describe one thing that would be *inconvenient* if
money didn't exist."

## Tutor notes

- Common error: learner tries to memorize which spelling goes with which word instead
  of applying the rule (match the prefix's last letter to the base's first letter).
  Correction: cover the base, show only the first letter, ask "which spelling of
  'not' matches this letter?" before revealing the base.
- Adult learners: the Track B passage names *saving*, *borrowing*, and *interest* in
  its last line without teaching them yet — that's deliberate; they're previewed
  here and taught properly in session 5.07 ("How Money Works, deeper"). Don't stop
  to fully explain them now; a one-line gloss is enough if asked.
- Urdu-speaker note: the /v/ vs /w/ distinction can affect *valuable* — some Urdu-first
  speakers will say /waluable/. Model lip position explicitly if it comes up; it's a
  pronunciation note, not a spelling or decoding error.
