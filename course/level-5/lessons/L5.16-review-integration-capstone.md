# L5.16 — Review, Integration & Extensive Reading Capstone

**Goal:** Cumulative review of all 19 prefixes, 13 suffixes, 9 number roots, and 20
commonly-useful roots taught in this level; finish the Fry first-300 heart-word list
(251–300); read a spiral-wrap knowledge text connecting all five topics; run an
extensive-reading capstone activity; prepare for the Level 5 mastery check.
**New today:** nothing new — this is consolidation. Tier-2 review: all 30 words
taught this level (list below).
**Review:** everything (see §2).
**Time:** 45 min (longer than usual — this is a wrap-up session; split across two
sittings if needed, doing §2–3 in one and §4–6 in another). **Materials:** paper,
pencil, timer, and whatever graded reader/article the learner has been assigned or
has chosen for the extensive-reading capstone (§4).

## 1. Retrieval warm-up (3–5 min)

Read: *credible, conduct, generation, telephone*. Heart words (Fry 251–300) — final

irregular word and is simply reviewed. Everything else in this final batch of 50 is
fully decodable. **This completes the Fry first-300 heart-word list** — mark it done
on whatever progress tracker the learner/tutor is using.

**Timed affix round (30 sec):** name as many of *anti-, mid-, under-, -ity, -ist*
(the last two sessions) as you can, meaning or one example word each. This is the
last individual timed round before §2's full cumulative sweep.

## 2. Cumulative morphology review (12–15 min)

Run this as a fast-paced oral + written round, not a slow re-teach — the goal is
retrieval practice, confirming what's solid and flagging what needs a quick booster,
not reteaching from scratch.

**All 19 prefixes** — for each, learner gives one example word and its meaning (tutor

`un-, re-, in-/im-/ir-/il-, dis-, en-/em-, non-, over-, mis-, sub-, pre-, inter-,
fore-, de-, trans-, super-, semi-, anti-, mid-, under-`

**All 13 suffixes** — same format:
`-ly, -er/-or, -tion/-sion, -able/-ible, -al, -y, -ness, -ment, -ful, -less, -ive,
-ity, -ist`

**All 9 number roots** — same format:
`mono-, uni-, bi-, tri-, quad-, pent-, hex-, oct-, dec-`

**All 20 commonly-useful roots** — same format:
`spect, port, dict, struct, scrib/script, tract, rupt, vis/vid, aud, graph/gram,

**Scoring guide:** note which items needed a hint or were missed entirely. Anything
missed 2+ times across this review goes on a short "review again next level" list —

**All 30 Tier-2 words taught this level** (2 per session, 5.01–5.15) — quick

`gradually, constant` (5.01) · `valuable, convenient` (5.02) · `vital, circulate`

**Self-correction drill (1 min):** say "the storm was very *in-TENSE-uh*" (extra
syllable added) inside a sentence and have the learner correct it to *intense*,
using context. This is the last self-correction round this level — from Level 6
on, the skill should be internalized enough to apply without a dedicated drill.

**Word harvest:** as a wrap-up activity, ask the learner to find one prefix, one
suffix, and one root — any of the ones taught this level — inside the Spiral Wrap
passage below.

## 3. Prime the topic (3–5 min)

Before reading: ask "can you name the five topics we've covered this level?" (water
cycle, money, the heart, the Indus Valley, and the weather/science cluster) — list
them together first. Orienting question: "Do you think any of these topics connect
to each other, or are they all completely separate?"

## 4. Fluency & Knowledge Text — the Spiral Wrap (10–12 min)

Model → echo → choral/partner → **Perform it** (1–2 min: read the whole passage
once more aloud to someone — a family member, the tutor, or a voice note to a
friend — as a small performance; aim for phrasing that follows the punctuation) →
independent (timed, a second and unannounced read — this is the one that gets
scored). WCPM + prosody check after.

This final passage deliberately connects all five knowledge-spine topics from the
level (water cycle, money, the human heart, the Indus Valley, weather/solar
system/electricity/internet/trade), because that connective thinking — not just
recalling five separate facts — is the actual comprehension goal of a knowledge
spine (research/06's rationale for spiraling, carried over into this level's design).

### Track A — Everything Is Connected (for a child)

This year you learned about the water cycle. The sun lifts invisible water into
the sky. Then it falls back down as rain. You learned how people long ago started
using money instead of just trading. Money was more convenient. You learned how
your heart works. It pumps blood in a loop around your whole body. You explored
an old, buried city from the Indus Valley. Later, you learned about the long
trade routes. They connected that same place to the rest of the world for
thousands of years. You learned the difference between weather and climate. You
learned about the huge size of the solar system. You learned how electricity
flows in a loop. You learned how a message travels across the internet in tiny
pieces.

None of these things are really separate. The same sun that drives the water
cycle also warms the whole solar system. The same trade routes that carried silk
also carried the idea of money. It spread from one place to another. The same
idea of loops and cycles shows up again and again. Water rises and falls again.
Blood moves in a loop. Electricity flows in a loop too. The same pattern shows up
in very different topics. Learning to notice this is one of the best reading
skills there is. It is not just knowing one fact at a time. It is noticing when
the same idea wears a different costume.

(≈239 words. **FK grade: 4.4 · Flesch reading ease: 80.0**.)

**Comprehension check:** (1) Name two topics from this level that both involve a
loop or a cycle. (2) According to the text, what traveled along trade routes
besides physical goods? (3) What does the passage say is "one of the most valuable,
essential reading skills there is"?

### Track B — What Connects a Water Molecule, a Coin, and a Trade Route (for a
teen/adult)

This level covered five topics that seem unrelated. The water cycle. The history
of money. How the heart works. Ancient trade networks. And basic systems like
weather, the solar system, electricity, and the internet. But these topics share a
small number of repeating ideas. Noticing this pattern is a real reading skill. It
is not just a fun trick.

The clearest repeating idea is the cycle. Water evaporates, rises, cools, and
falls. Then it evaporates again. Blood moves from the heart, to the lungs, to the
body, and back again. Electricity flows around a closed loop. Even money works
this way. Once coins replaced bartering, money became something that moves from
hand to hand, again and again. It never gets "used up" the way a traded good
does.

A second repeating idea is the network. The internet sends data through a network
of routers. The Silk Road was a network of connected trade paths. Even the Indus
Valley's matching stone weights only worked because cities trusted each other,
like a network of trust. All three of these are really the same kind of thing.
Each one is a system made of many small parts. No single part needs to
understand the whole system. It only needs to know its own next connection.

Noticing these same patterns, in topics that seem completely different, is
exactly what separates reading for scattered facts from reading for real
understanding. Level 6 builds on this skill directly. It teaches you to notice
how a whole text is organized, not just one sentence at a time.

(≈260 words. **FK grade: 5.6 · Flesch reading ease: 71.2**.)

**Comprehension check:** (1) What does the text identify as the "clearest
repeating idea" across this level's topics? (2) What three things does the text
group under "the network" idea? (3) What does the text say separates "reading for
scattered facts" from "reading for real understanding"?

### Discussion — reciprocal roles

**Predictor**: before reading, "What connections do you expect this final passage
to draw between the level's topics?" **Questioner**: one answered, one unanswered
question. **Clarifier**: pick the hardest word or idea in either passage and explain
how you worked through it. **Summarizer**: retell the whole passage in 3–4
sentences — this is a longer summary than usual, appropriate for a wrap-up text.

## 5. Extensive reading capstone (8–10 min)

Review whatever wide-reading log the learner has built up over the level (README
§7 — a StoryWeaver or Global Digital Library title for Track A, or a BBC Learning
English article for Track B, ideally assigned every 2–3 sessions across the level).
If the log is thin or empty, use this session to start one: pick one short graded
reader or article now, read it with light scaffolding (audio support if available),
and answer:

1. Who/what was it about, and what happened? (literal)
2. What was one thing that surprised you, or that you didn't expect? (inference)
3. If you could ask the author one question, what would it be? (evaluative)

Log the title. This ongoing habit — not a one-time assignment — is what actually
builds toward Nation's 95% text-coverage threshold over time (research/04 §1); one
session's reading doesn't get there, but a habit of wide reading, sustained across
Level 6 and beyond, does.

## Tutor notes

- This session is deliberately heavier on review and lighter on new content —
  resist the urge to add anything new; the job here is consolidation and honest
  gap-flagging, not new teaching.
- If the cumulative review in §2 turns up more than 4–5 genuinely shaky items
  (not just slow retrieval, but wrong answers even with a hint), that's a signal to
  add a short extra review session before the mastery check, rather than pushing
  through to Level 6 with real gaps in the morphology foundation Level 6 assumes is
  solid.
- The "Everything Is Connected" / structural-repeats framing in §3 is this course's
  own synthesis, not a claim sourced to research/06 directly — it's a reasonable,
  honest extension of the knowledge-spine rationale research/06 does support, and
  should be presented to a learner as "notice this pattern," not "researchers
  proved this specific connection."
