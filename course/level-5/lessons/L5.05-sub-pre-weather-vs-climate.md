# L5.05 — Prefixes sub-, pre- · Weather vs. Climate

**Goal:** Read, spell, and use words with *sub-* and *pre-*; continue the fluency
program (phrase-cued for the second-to-last time this level).
**New today:** *sub-* ("under" / "below") and *pre-* ("before"). Tier-2 words:
*predict*, *typical*.
**Review:** *over-, mis-* — *overflow, misplace*.
**Time:** 30–45 min. **Materials:** paper, pencil, timer.

---

## 1. Retrieval warm-up (3–5 min)

Read: *overreact, mistrust, misunderstand*. Heart words (Fry 113–115): **give,
most, very**. *Most* and *very* are fully decodable. *Give* has a real heart part:
the silent-*e* doesn't make the *i* say its letter name (compare *five*, where it
does) — mark the *i* as the irregular piece; the rest decodes normally.

**Timed affix round (30 sec):** name as many of *over-, mis-* (last session) as you

### Tier-2 vocabulary

**predict** — friendly definition: "to say what you think will happen before it
happens, based on what you already know." Examples: "You can predict rain if you see
dark clouds gathering." / "Nobody can predict every question on a test, but you can
predict the topics it will probably cover." Generative use: "Predict what the weather
will be like tomorrow where you live, and say why you think so."

**typical** — friendly definition: "usual, the way something normally is." Examples:
"A typical summer day here is hot and dry." / "It's typical for prices to go up a
little every year." Generative use: "Describe a typical morning for you."

## 3. Prime the topic (3–5 min)

Before reading: ask "what's the weather like outside right now?" then "what's it
usually like this time of year, most years?" Point out these are two different
questions. Give 2–3 quick facts: weather changes day to day; climate is the average
pattern over many years; forecasters can only reliably predict a few days ahead.
Orienting question: "Has the weather ever surprised you by being very different
from what's typical for the season?"

## 4. Fluency & Knowledge Text (10–12 min) — phrase-cued

Model → echo → choral → **Perform it** (1–2 min: read the whole passage once more
aloud to someone — a family member, the tutor, or a voice note to a friend — as a
small performance; aim for expression that matches the content) → independent
(timed, a second and unannounced read — this is the one that gets scored). WCPM +
prosody check after.

### Track A — Weather vs. Climate (for a child)

> "Weather" and "climate" / sound almost the same. / But they answer / two
> different questions. / Weather is what's happening / outside right now, / or
> this week. / Is it raining? / Is it sunny? / Is it cold / or warm? / Weather
> can change / very fast. / It can be sunny in the morning. / It can be rainy /
> by afternoon. / It can be different again / tomorrow. / That's why /
> weather forecasters / can only predict / a few days ahead. / It's a
> fast-changing system. / It's hard to predict / far into the future.
>
> Climate is different. / Climate is the usual pattern / of weather / in a
> place. / We measure it / over many, many years. / A desert's climate / is
> usually hot and dry. / That doesn't mean / it never rains there. / It means /
> rain is rare, / on average, / over time. / A place near the ocean / usually
> has a mild climate. / It is not too hot, / and not too cold. / This is true /
> even though / its daily weather / still changes / from day to day.
>
> So if someone asks, / "what's the weather like?" / they want to know / about
> today. / If they ask, / "what's the climate like?" / they want to know /
> about the usual pattern, / over many years, / not just today.

(≈198 words. **FK grade: 3.8 · Flesch reading ease: 81.2**.)

**Comprehension check:** (1) What question does "weather" answer? (2) What question
does "climate" answer? (3) Why can forecasters only reliably predict a few days
ahead?

### Track B — Weather vs. Climate (for a teen/adult)

> News reports / sometimes mix up / two words. / The words are / weather / and
> climate. / They do not mean / the same thing.
>
> Weather means / what is happening / in one place / right now. / It means the
> temperature, / the rain, / and the wind, / on a given day. / Weather is hard /
> to predict / far ahead. / Small changes today / can lead to / very different
> results / a week later. / Even the best forecasts / get shaky / after about /
> ten days.
>
> Climate is different. / Climate is the usual pattern / of weather / in a
> region. / It is measured / over many years, / not one day. / A region's
> climate / does not tell you / tomorrow's weather. / It tells you / the
> chances / of different kinds of weather / over a long time.
>
> This difference / matters in real life. / Say it snows / during a week / that
> is usually warm. / That is just / one weather event. / It does not prove /
> the climate is changing. / One cold day / does not undo the fact / that
> summer, / on average, / is hotter than winter.
>
> Knowing this difference / is a useful skill. / It lets you read a headline /
> about one storm / without panicking. / A single event / tells you about
> today. / It does not tell you much / about the bigger, / longer pattern.

(≈199 words. **FK grade: 4.5 · Flesch reading ease: 78.6**.)

**Comprehension check:** (1) Why does the text say weather is "hard to predict far
ahead"? (2) What does climate tell you, if not tomorrow's weather? (3) What example
does the text give of a weather event that doesn't prove the climate is changing?

### Discussion — reciprocal roles

**Predictor**: "From the title, what do you expect this text to explain?"
**Questioner**: one answered question, one unanswered (e.g., neither text explains
exactly how climate is measured or by whom). **Clarifier**: explain *forecast*,
*chances*, or *pattern* using context. **Summarizer**: retell in
2–3 sentences, using both words *weather* and *climate* correctly.

## Tutor notes

- Common error: confusing the meanings of *sub-* and *pre-* since both can appear
  before similar bases. Correction: ask "does this happen *underneath/below*
  something, or *before* something in time?" every time, rather than guessing from
  the first letter of the prefix.
- This session quietly previews two roots (*tract* in *subtract*, *dict* in
  *predict*) that get full root-family treatment in 5.12–5.13 — don't over-teach the
  roots now; a one-line mention ("that -tract/-dict part means something on its own —
  more on that later") is enough.
- Adult learners: the weather-vs-climate distinction is genuinely useful adult
  scientific literacy, not a simplified kids' science topic — worth saying so if it
  comes up.
