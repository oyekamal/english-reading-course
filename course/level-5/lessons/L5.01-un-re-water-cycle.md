# L5.01 — Prefixes un-, re- · The Water Cycle (intro)

**Goal:** Read, spell, and use words with *un-* and *re-*; read a phrase-cued fluency
passage on the water cycle at Model→Assisted→Independent; begin the Fry heart-word
completion sequence.
**New today:** prefixes *un-* ("not"/"opposite of") and *re-* ("again"/"back") — the
#1 and #2 highest-yield prefixes in English (White, Sowell & Yanagihara 1989: *un-*
alone ≈ 26% of all prefixed words; research/05 §1). Tier-2 words: *gradually*,
*constant*.
**Review:** none (first session of the level) — if coming straight from Level 4,
warm up with 5 multisyllable words from L4.16's mastery check instead.
**Time:** 30–45 min. **Materials:** paper, pencil; a way to time a 1-minute read
(phone timer); audio playback optional for the Model step.

---

## 1. Retrieval warm-up (3–5 min)

Read aloud, cold, no help: *reaction, understand, information, remarkable,
disconnect* (all Level 4 multisyllable words, decoded by syllable chunking — this
confirms Level 4 skills are solid before adding morphology on top).

Heart words (Fry 101–103): **new, sound, take**. All three are fully decodable with
Level 1–4 code (*new* = vowel team /ū/, *sound* = *ou* diphthong, *take* = VCe) — no
heart part to mark. Say each, segment it, blend it. These are "teach in L5" only in
the sense that nobody has explicitly reviewed them yet, not because they're irregular.

**Timed affix round (30 sec):** this is the first session, so skip the timed round
today — there's nothing yet to time. Starting 5.02, this slot names as many
previous-session affixes as possible in 30 seconds.

## 2. Word work (10–12 min)

### un- ("not" / "the opposite of")

Say: "*Un-* in front of a word usually flips its meaning to the opposite, or means
'not.' *Happy* → *unhappy* means 'not happy.'"

| Word | Meaning check | Spelling note |
|---|---|---|
| unhappy | not happy | un + happy, no change |
| unfair | not fair | un + fair |
| undo | reverse doing something | un + do |
| unlock | open something locked | un + lock |
| unable | not able | un + able |
| uncertain | not sure | un + certain |
| unclear | not clear, confusing | un + clear |
| unwrap | take the wrapping off | un + wrap |

Decode each aloud (peel *un-*, read the base, blend). Then dictate 3: *unhappy,
unlock, uncertain* — learner segments, writes, checks the peel-off boundary.

Meaning-check sentence: "The lost dog looked very ______ (unhappy/happily) until we
found its owner." → *unhappy* fits; discuss why "happily" doesn't (wrong word part —
save this distinction, it becomes explicit in the suffix sessions).

### re- ("again" / "back")

Say: "*Re-* in front of a word usually means 'again' or 'back.' *Write* → *rewrite*
means 'write again.'"

| Word | Meaning check | Spelling note |
|---|---|---|
| rewrite | write again | re + write |
| redo | do again | re + do |
| return | go/come back | re + turn |
| reread | read again | re + read |
| rebuild | build again | re + build |
| replay | play again | re + play |
| recycle | use again (in a new form) | re + cycle |
| reuse | use again | re + use |

Dictate 3: *redo, return, recycle*.

**Self-correction drill (1 min):** say "she felt very *un-HAP-py*" (stress shifted)
inside a sentence and have the learner correct it to *unhappy*, using the
sentence's meaning as the check, not just re-reading the letters. Repeat with one
*re-* word.

**Word harvest:** find one more *re-* word inside today's passage — *recycle* does
not appear, but see if you can spot a *re-* or *un-* word in Track A or B below
(there may not be a clean match in this first session's passage — say so honestly
if not).

### Tier-2 vocabulary

**gradually** — friendly definition: "a little bit at a time, not all at once."
Examples: "The sky gradually got darker as the sun went down." / "She gradually got
better at reading, a little more every week." Generative use: "Finish this: '___
gradually got ___.'" (learner supplies both blanks from their own experience.)

**constant** — friendly definition: "something that keeps happening, or never
changes." Examples: "There was a constant drip from the tap all night." / "His
constant questions showed he really wanted to learn." Generative use: "Name one thing
in your life that is constant."

## 3. Prime the topic (3–5 min)

Before reading: ask "where do you think rain comes from?" — take a guess before
reading. Give 2–3 quick facts: the sun heats water until it rises as invisible
vapor; the vapor cools and forms clouds; clouds get heavy and the water falls back
down as rain. Orienting question: "Have you ever seen rain, or watched a cloud
form? What did it look like?"

## 4. Fluency & Knowledge Text (10–12 min) — phrase-cued

Model first (tutor/audio reads it once, well, before the learner touches it). Then
echo (phrase by phrase) → choral (together) → **Perform it** (1–2 min: read the
whole passage once more aloud to someone — a family member, the tutor, or a voice
note to a friend — as a small performance; aim for phrasing that follows the
punctuation, not just correct words) → independent (timed, 1 learner alone, a
second and unannounced read — this is the one that gets scored). Score WCPM +
prosody (README §4) after the independent read.

### Track A — The Water Cycle (for a child)

> Every morning / the sun comes up / and warms the sea. / The warm water / turns into
> something / you cannot see — / water vapor. / It floats up / into the sky, / where
> the air is cooler. / Up there / the vapor gathers / into tiny drops, / and the tiny
> drops / make a cloud. / A cloud is just / millions of tiny drops / of water, / all
> floating together.
>
> The cloud gets heavier / and heavier / as more drops join it. / Soon / the drops are
> too heavy / to float. / They fall — / as rain, / or snow / if the air is very cold. /
> The rain falls / on the hills / and the fields / and the roofs of houses. / Some of
> it / soaks into the ground. / Some of it / runs into rivers. / The rivers carry it /
> back to the sea. / And then, / gradually, / the sun warms the sea again, / and the
> whole thing / starts over. / This is called / the water cycle, / because it never
> really stops — / it just goes around and around, / again and again, / forever.

(≈205 words. **FK grade: 4.4 · Flesch reading ease: 84.4**.)

**Comprehension check (2 literal, 1 think):** (1) What does the sun do to the sea
water? (2) What falls out of a heavy cloud? (3) Why do you think the passage calls it
a "cycle" instead of just "the weather"?

### Track B — The Water Cycle (for a teen/adult, farming framing)

> A farmer checks the sky / every morning, / because the sky / can tell her a lot.
>
> Each day, / the sun heats the ocean / and the wet fields, / and some of that
> water / turns into vapor. / Vapor is water / you cannot see, / and it rises / into
> the air.
>
> You cannot see vapor, / but you can see / what it makes. / High in the sky, / the
> air is cooler, / so the vapor cools down too. / It turns back / into tiny drops
> of water, / and billions of these tiny drops / make one cloud.
>
> A cloud can only hold / so much water. / When it holds too much, / the drops
> fall / as rain.
>
> For a farmer, / rain at the right time / can mean a good harvest. / Rain at the
> wrong time, / or no rain at all, / can ruin a whole crop.
>
> This is why / many farms build reservoirs. / A reservoir is a large lake / that
> stores rainwater / during the wet season. / During the dry season, / farmers use
> that stored water / to water their fields, / instead of waiting for rain / to
> fall at exactly the right moment.
>
> Any rain / that is not stored / soaks into the soil, / or runs into a river. /
> Rivers carry that water / back to the sea, / and then the sun / warms the sea
> again, / and the whole cycle / starts over.
>
> For anyone / who depends on rain / to earn a living, / understanding this
> cycle / is not just science. / It is practical knowledge, / passed down / for
> many years.

(≈242 words. **FK grade: 5.2 · Flesch reading ease: 83.3** — measured with
`textstat.flesch_kincaid_grade()`; within the level's A2–B1 target of FK 3–6. See
GAUNTLET.md Round 1 for why this measurement is now recorded on every Track B
passage.)

**Comprehension check:** (1) What do reservoirs do? (2) What happens to water that
isn't stored in a reservoir? (3) Why does the passage say understanding the water
cycle is "practical knowledge," not just science?

### Discussion — reciprocal roles

Before reading: **Predictor** — "From the title, what do you think this will be
about?" After reading, rotate through: **Questioner** (ask one question the text
answers and one it doesn't — e.g. "it doesn't say how long the whole cycle takes for
one drop of water"), **Clarifier** (was any word confusing? *vapor*, *condensed*,
*reservoirs* are candidates — resolve using context or morphology, never "guess from
the picture"), **Summarizer** (retell in 2–3 sentences).

## 5. Write to read (5 min)

Write 2–3 sentences: "Describe one step of the water cycle in your own words, using
the word *gradually* or *constant* somewhere in your answer."

## 6. Check (3–5 min)

- Read and spell: *unhappy, unlock, uncertain, redo, return, recycle* (any 4 of 6
  correct on each = pass)
- Define, in your own words: *gradually*
- Read: *sound* (Fry heart word)

**≥5/8 correct → go to 5.02. Below that:** reteach the un-/re- peel-off routine with
new, simpler example words (*unzip, retie*) before adding more; the vocabulary word
alone failing is not a reteach trigger — just add one more exposure next session.

**Optional home practice:** reread today's passage once more at home, to a family
member or aloud to yourself, before 5.02.

## Tutor notes

- Common error: learner reads *un-* + base as one blended nonsense sound instead of
  peeling it off first. Correction: cover the base word, read *un-* alone, uncover,
  read the base alone, then blend the two — never "just try to remember the whole
  word."
- Adult learners: don't over-explain "vapor" as a chemistry term — the Tier-2 words
  this session are *gradually* and *constant*, not *vapor* (that's Tier-3,
  domain-specific vocabulary, mentioned in the text but not drilled — research/05 §1
  three-tier model).
- Urdu-speaker note: *water* and *warm* both have the /w/ sound, which some
  Urdu-first speakers substitute with /v/ — model the lip-rounded /w/ explicitly if
  this comes up; it's a pronunciation note, not a reading error, and doesn't affect
  the check above.
