## 2. Word work (10–12 min)

### -tion/-sion ("the act or result of doing something" — turns a verb into a noun)

Say: "This suffix turns an action word into a thing/idea word. *Act* → *action* =
'the act of doing something.' It's usually *-tion* after *t* or a base ending in
*-te* (drop the *e* first); it's usually *-sion* after *d* or *s* — *decide* →
*decision*, *discuss* → *discussion*; and it's usually *-ation* after most other
endings — *inform* → *information* is the clearest example in today's list, since
*inform* ends in neither *t*, *-te*, *d*, nor *s*."

| Word | Meaning check | Spelling note |
|---|---|---|
| action | the act of doing something | act + ion |
| invention | a thing invented | invent + ion |
| direction | the act of directing / a way to go | direct + ion |
| information | facts that inform | inform + ation |
| decision | the result of deciding | decid**e**→drop e + sion |
| television | (originally "far-seeing") a device for viewing broadcasts | tele + vis(ion) — root preview |
| discussion | the act of discussing | discuss + ion |
| celebration | the act of celebrating | celebrat**e**→drop e + ion |

Dictate 3: *action, decision, discussion*.

### -able/-ible ("capable of being done")

| Word | Meaning check | Spelling note |
|---|---|---|
| comfortable | capable of giving comfort | comfort + able |
| reliable | capable of being relied on | rely→rel**i** + able |
| breakable | capable of being broken | break + able |
| enjoyable | capable of being enjoyed | enjoy + able |
| agreeable | capable of agreeing / pleasant | agree + able |
| flexible | capable of bending/adapting | flex + ible |
| sensible | capable of good sense/judgment | sens(e) + ible |
| terrible | (root: *terr* = to frighten) capable of causing fear/very bad | terr + ible |

Dictate 3: *comfortable, breakable, flexible*. General tendency (not a hard rule):
*-able* usually attaches to a base that's a complete word on its own (*comfort,
break, enjoy*); *-ible* more often attaches to a base that isn't a free-standing
word by itself (*flex-, sens-, terr-*) — this becomes much clearer once roots are
taught from 5.11 on.

Meaning-check sentence: "Before making a big ______ (decision/discussion) about
money, it helps to talk it through with someone you trust." → *decision* fits.

**Self-correction drill (1 min):** say "she made a *dis-cuss-EE-un*" (vowel and

### Tier-2 vocabulary

**interest** — friendly definition, in the money sense: "extra money a bank pays you
for keeping your savings there, or extra money you pay a lender for borrowing from
them." Examples: "If you keep money in a savings account, the bank pays you a small
amount of interest over time." / "Borrowing money usually means paying it back with
interest, so it costs more than the amount you borrowed." Generative use: "Would you
rather be the person earning interest, or the person paying it? Why?"

**budget** — friendly definition: "a plan for how much money you'll spend and how
much you'll save, usually over a set amount of time." Examples: "A monthly budget
helps you make sure you don't spend more than you earn." / "The family budget set
aside a fixed amount for food and a fixed amount for savings." Generative use:
"Name one thing you'd put into your own budget if you made one."

## 3. Prime the topic (3–5 min)

Before reading: ask "if you saved a small amount of money and left it in a bank for
ten years, do you think it would grow?" Give 2–3 quick facts: banks pay you
interest for keeping savings with them; interest can itself earn more interest over
time (compounding); a budget is a plan for spending and saving made in advance, not
decided moment to moment. Orienting question: "Do you already keep any kind of
budget, even an informal one?"

### Track A — Saving Money (for a child)

Zeenat had five hundred rupees from her birthday. She wanted to decide what to do
with it. Her mother had an idea. Instead of spending it all right away, she could
put some of it in a savings account at the bank. It would stay safe there. It
would even grow, a little, all on its own.

"How can money grow on its own?" Zeenat asked. It was a very sensible question.
Her mother explained that banks pay you interest. This is a small extra amount for
keeping your money with them. It gets added regularly. The bank uses your saved
money to lend to other people. Then it shares a bit of what it earns back with
you. It is not a huge amount, especially at first. But it adds up over time.

Zeenat decided on a simple plan. It was her own small budget. She would spend half
of her birthday money on something fun, right away. She would save the other
half. She would check on it every few months, to watch it grow, bit by bit. It
was not a hard decision once she thought about it that way. Some now, some later,
and a small reward just for being patient.

(≈205 words. **FK grade: 3.1 · Flesch reading ease: 89.3**.)

**Comprehension check:** (1) What does the bank pay you for keeping your money
there? (2) What was Zeenat's simple budget plan? (3) Why does the text say interest
"isn't a huge amount, especially at first"?

### Track B — Interest, Compounding, and a Simple Budget (for a teen/adult)

Understanding interest is one of the most useful things an adult can know about
money. It comes down to one key idea: simple interest versus compound interest.

Simple interest is easy. It is counted only on the amount you first saved or
borrowed. Compound interest works differently. It is counted on that first amount,
plus any interest you have already earned. In other words, your interest starts to
earn its own interest.

That small difference matters a lot over time. Even a modest amount, saved often,
can grow into a large sum if you give it compound interest and many years. This is
why starting to save early matters more than the size of any one deposit.

The same idea works against you with debt. An unpaid credit card bill also grows
through compound interest. If you only pay the minimum each month, that debt can
grow fast, and it can catch people by surprise.

A simple budget helps with both sides of this. Here is one approach that works
well. First, pay for the essentials: rent, food, and bills. Next, set aside a
fixed amount for savings, right away. Only after that, decide what is left for
everything else. Do not just save whatever happens to be left at the end of the
month. For most people, that amount is close to nothing. A fixed plan, made once,
removes the need to decide it again every month.

(≈238 words. **FK grade: 5.1 · Flesch reading ease: 78.4**.)

**Comprehension check:** (1) What is the difference between simple and compound
interest? (2) Why can an unpaid credit card bill "grow fast"? (3) What does the
text recommend doing with savings, instead of "saving whatever happens to be left
over"?

## Tutor notes

- Common error: spelling *-tion* words as *-sion* or vice versa by guessing.
  Correction: say the base verb first (*decide, discuss, act*) — the base's own
  ending is usually the best clue.
- This session previews the root *vis(ion)* inside *television* — don't fully teach
  the root yet; it gets full treatment with the *vis/vid* family in 5.13.
- Adult learners: compound interest is real, load-bearing financial literacy —
  worth pausing on this concept even if the reading check passes quickly.
