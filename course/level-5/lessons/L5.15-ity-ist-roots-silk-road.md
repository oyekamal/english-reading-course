# L5.15 — Suffixes -ity, -ist · Roots cred, duc/duct, ver, corp, gen, log/logy, phon, therm · Trade Routes: The Silk Road

**Goal:** Read, spell, and use words with *-ity* and *-ist*; learn the remaining
eight commonly-useful roots (completing all 20); run a big heart-word review sweep
(Fry 201–250); connect the trade theme back to the Indus Valley spiral (5.04, 5.09).
**New today:** suffix *-ity* ("state/quality of") and *-ist* ("a person who does/
believes in"); roots *cred* (believe), *duc/duct* (lead), *ver* (truth), *corp*
(body), *gen* (birth/produce), *log/logy* (study/word), *phon* (sound), *therm*
(heat). Tier-2 words: *route*, *exotic*.
**Review:** *aud, graph/gram, ject, mit/mis* — one example word each.
**Time:** 30–45 min — the densest session in the level; it's fine to split the
root table across two sittings if needed, reviewing the rest next session's
warm-up instead of rushing. **Materials:** paper, pencil, timer.

### Roots — the remaining eight (completes all 20 in this level)

| Root | Meaning | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| cred | to believe | **cred**ible (believable), in**cred**ible (not believable — amazing), **cred**it (belief/trust given, or money owed), **cred**entials (proof that earns belief/trust) |
| duc/duct | to lead | con**duct** (lead together — to behave, or to guide), intro**duce** (lead into), e**duc**ate (lead out — to teach), re**duc**e (lead back — to make less) |
| ver | truth | **ver**ify (make true/confirm), ver**dict** (a true saying — callback to *dict* in 5.13), **ver**ity (truth itself — a rarer word, worth knowing); fun fact: *very* itself comes from the same Latin root (*verus*, "true") — its meaning has drifted over centuries from "truly" to just "to a high degree," a good example that a root doesn't always predict a modern word's exact meaning |
| corp | body | **corp**orate (relating to a body of people, a company), **corp**se (a dead body), incorporate (to bring into one body/group) |
| gen | birth/produce | **gen**erate (to produce), **gen**etic (relating to birth/inheritance), **gen**eration (a group born around the same time) |
| log/logy | see note below | bio**logy** (the study of life), geo**logy** (the study of the earth), dia**log**ue (words between people), mono**logue** (one person speaking alone) |
| phon | sound | tele**phon**e (far-sound, a device for hearing distant voices), micro**phon**e (a device for small/close sounds), **phon**ics (relating to sounds — the very method this course is built on!) |
| therm | heat | **therm**ometer (a tool that measures heat), **therm**al (relating to heat), **therm**os (a container that keeps heat in or out) |

Point out *phon* → *phonics*, the name of the whole approach this course uses — a
nice, concrete example of a root explaining a word the learner's used the entire
course without necessarily knowing why it's spelled that way.

**Note on *log/logy*:** this one root spelling actually carries two related but
different senses, worth keeping separate so a learner doesn't think *dialogue*
means "a study." **At the end of a word, *-logy* means "study of"** (bio**logy**,
geo**logy**). **Elsewhere, *log/logue* means "word/speech"** (dia**logue** = words
between people, mono**logue** = one person speaking alone). Same root family,
Greek *logos* ("word" — which itself grew to also mean "reasoned account," hence
"study of"), but teach the two senses as a pair, not one blended meaning.

### Tier-2 vocabulary

**route** — friendly definition: "a path taken to get from one place to another."
Examples: "Traders along the Silk Road followed established routes across deserts
and mountains." / "There are two routes to school — one faster, one with less
traffic." Generative use: "Describe your usual route to somewhere you go often."

**exotic** — friendly definition: "unusual or interesting because it comes from a
distant or unfamiliar place." Examples: "Silk was an exotic, highly valued good in
places far from where it was made." / "The market sold exotic spices that had
traveled thousands of kilometers." Generative use: "Name something you'd consider
exotic, and where it might have come from."

## 3. Prime the topic (3–5 min)

Before reading: show a map of Asia/Europe/the Middle East if possible, or describe
one. Give 2–3 quick facts: the Silk Road was not one single road, but thousands of
connected paths; it carried goods like silk and spices, and also ideas; it connects
back to the Indus Valley region you studied in 5.04 and 5.09. Orienting question:
"Can you think of something in daily life today that started far away and traveled
a long way to reach you?"

### Track A — The Silk Road (for a child)

Long before airplanes, trucks, or trains, there was a network of routes. Some went
over land. Some went by sea. They connected China all the way to Europe and the
Middle East. People call it the Silk Road. But it was not really one single road.
It was thousands of connected paths. Countless traders used it, over hundreds of
years.

Long lines of camels carried exotic goods across deserts and over mountains. They
carried silk from China. Silk was so valuable and so closely guarded that for a
long time, nobody outside China knew how it was made. They carried spices. They
carried paper. Later, they carried ideas too. New ways of writing. New
inventions. Even new religions. All of this traveled the same routes as the
goods.

No single trader traveled the whole route from end to end. Instead, goods passed
from one trader to the next. They moved region by region. It was like a very long
relay race. Sometimes it took years for one journey to cross the whole network.
This connects back to the Indus Valley traders you learned about earlier. That
same region, thousands of years later, was still a crossroads. Goods, ideas, and
people from very different, distant places met there. They shared what they
knew.

(≈212 words. **FK grade: 4.0 · Flesch reading ease: 80.8**.)

**Comprehension check:** (1) Name two exotic goods carried along the Silk Road. (2)
Why didn't any single trader travel the whole route? (3) How does the text connect
the Silk Road back to the Indus Valley?

### Track B — Trade Routes and the Exchange of Ideas (for a teen/adult)

People often picture the Silk Road as just a way to move goods. Silk, spices, and
other exotic items. But its biggest export may have been ideas.

Along the same routes that carried goods, information traveled too. Papermaking
skills moved along these routes. So did math and astronomy knowledge. So did
religious beliefs. Even diseases spread this way. All of this moved over hundreds
of years. No single trader ever crossed the whole network alone.

This matters for a bigger reason too. It challenges a common idea. Many people
think ancient civilizations lived cut off from each other. That idea is too
simple. The Indus Valley Civilization shows real evidence of trade with
Mesopotamia. That trade happened almost 4,500 years ago. The Silk Road came
roughly two thousand years after that. This shows something important. This same
wide region stayed a place where cultures met and traded. It did this for
thousands of years. It was not just a short, rare exception.

Here is one useful way to think about it. A route is not a single event. It is
more like a road or a bridge. Once a trade route is set up, and safe to travel,
it keeps carrying whatever is valuable. It does this for as long as it lasts.
What it carries changes over time. First silk and spices. Later, paper.
Eventually, new technology and religious texts. So studying trade routes is not
really studying goods. It is studying how ideas and technology spread across huge
distances. This happened long before any modern way to communicate existed.

(≈261 words. **FK grade: 5.5 · Flesch reading ease: 69.9**.)

**Comprehension check:** (1) What does the text say may have been the Silk Road's
"biggest export"? (2) How does the text connect the Silk Road back to the Indus
Valley Civilization? (3) What does the text mean by "a route is not a single
event... it is more like a road or a bridge"?

## Tutor notes

- This is genuinely the densest session in the level — if it runs long, it is fine
  to stop after 5–6 of the 8 roots and pick up the rest as 5.16's warm-up instead of
  rushing through all 8 in one sitting. Quality of the meaning-check over quantity
  of roots covered.
- Common error: trying to memorize all 20 roots as an isolated list instead of
  noticing them inside words already known (*phonics, education, generation*).
  Correction: for any root the learner struggles with, first ask "do you already
  know a word with this root in it?" before teaching a brand-new example.
- Adult learners: the "a route is like a road or a bridge, not a single event"
  framing is a genuine historical-thinking skill, not a simplified metaphor — worth
  pausing on if the learner is engaged with it.
