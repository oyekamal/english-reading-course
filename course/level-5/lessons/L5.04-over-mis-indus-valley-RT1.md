# L5.04 — Prefixes over-, mis- · The Indus Valley (intro) · Readers Theatre #1

**Goal:** Read, spell, and use words with *over-* and *mis-*; run this level's first
Readers Theatre script as the fluency vehicle instead of a solo passage.
**New today:** *over-* ("too much" / "above") and *mis-* ("wrongly" / "badly").
Tier-2 words: *ancient*, *evidence*.
**Review:** *en-/em-, non-* — *enable, nonstop*.
**Time:** 30–45 min. **Materials:** paper, pencil, timer; the script below printed or
copied out, one part per reader (or one reader taking all parts aloud, for a solo
adult learner — see Tutor notes).

---

## 1. Retrieval warm-up (3–5 min)

Read: *encourage, embrace, nonliving*. Heart words (Fry 110–112): **live, me, back**.
All three are fully decodable (*live* = VCe, or short-vowel closed-syllable
"live"/"lived" depending on meaning — flag that *live* is one of the rare words whose
pronunciation changes with meaning/part of speech, not irregular spelling, just
worth naming); *me, back* are straightforward review.

**Timed affix round (30 sec):** name as many of *en-/em-, non-* (last session) as
you can, meaning or one example word each.

## 2. Word work (10–12 min)

### over- ("too much" / "above")

| Word | Meaning check | Spelling note |
|---|---|---|
| overcook | cook too much/too long | over + cook |
| overflow | flow over the top/edge | over + flow |
| overjoyed | extremely joyful | over + joyed |
| overlook | fail to notice (or: look down from above) | over + look |
| oversleep | sleep longer than intended | over + sleep |
| overweight | weighing more than is healthy | over + weight |
| overreact | react more strongly than the situation needs | over + react |
| overcrowded | too crowded | over + crowded |

Dictate 3: *overcook, overflow, oversleep*.

### mis- ("wrongly" / "badly")

| Word | Meaning check | Spelling note |
|---|---|---|
| mistake | something done wrongly | mis + take |
| misunderstand | understand wrongly | mis + understand |
| mislead | lead someone the wrong way (on purpose) | mis + lead |
| misspell | spell wrongly | mis + spell (double *s*!) |
| misplace | put something in the wrong place | mis + place |
| mistrust | trust wrongly/not fully | mis + trust |
| mismatch | a wrong or bad match | mis + match |
| misbehave | behave badly | mis + behave |

Dictate 3: *mistake, misplace, misbehave*. Flag *misspell* specifically: point out
that *mis- + spell* naturally doubles the *s* — a spelling-rule pattern the learner
will meet again with other double-letter prefix/base boundaries.

Meaning-check sentence: "If you ______ (misunderstand/overreact) the instructions,
you might do the wrong thing without realizing it." → *misunderstand* fits best.

**Self-correction drill (1 min):** say "she was *o-ver-JOYED*" (stress shifted)
inside a sentence and have the learner correct it to *overjoyed*, using the
sentence's meaning as the check. Repeat with one *mis-* word.

**Word harvest:** find one more *over-* or *mis-* word inside today's script —
*overlook* and *misplace* both appear in the Track A script; see if you can spot
them before being told.

### Tier-2 vocabulary

**ancient** — friendly definition: "from a very, very long time ago — much older
than your grandparents." Examples: "The pyramids of Egypt are ancient — thousands of
years old." / "Archaeologists study ancient cities to learn how people used to
live." Generative use: "Name one ancient thing you've heard of, from anywhere in the
world."

**evidence** — friendly definition: "facts, objects, or signs that help prove
something is true." Examples: "The broken pottery was evidence that people had lived
there long ago." / "A detective looks for evidence before deciding who did
something." Generative use: "If you wanted evidence that someone had been in a room,
what might you look for?"

## 3. Prime the topic (3–5 min)

Before reading: show a picture of an archaeological dig, or describe one — people
carefully brushing dirt off old bricks and pottery. Give 2–3 quick facts: a very old
civilization once lived in this region, thousands of years ago; we call it the
Indus Valley Civilization; archaeologists dig very carefully to learn about it from
small clues. Orienting question: "Have you ever found something old and wondered
who it used to belong to?"

## 4. Fluency: Readers Theatre #1 — "The Buried City"

**How to run a Readers Theatre session (first time — full instructions; from 5.08
onward this shrinks to a one-line reminder):**
1. Assign parts (or read all parts solo, in different voices, if alone).
2. **Model read** — tutor (or the most confident reader) reads the whole script once,
   all parts, at natural pace with expression, so everyone hears the target before
   attempting it.
3. **Rehearsal read** — everyone reads their own part(s) once through, script in
   hand, no memorization, pausing to fix any word-attack problems.
4. **Performance read** — read it once more, all the way through, at performance
   pace and expression — to each other, to a phone recording, to whoever's in the
   room. This is the timed/scored read for WCPM + prosody (score the whole cast's
   combined reading using the group's slowest confident reader as the WCPM anchor,
   and the prosody rubric on the read as a whole).

No memorization, no costumes, no pressure — the point is repeated, purposeful oral
rehearsal toward a small performance, which research shows builds automaticity and
prosody at least as well as traditional drill, especially for readers who find
repeated-reading drills babyish (research/05 §1, §6).

### Track A script — for a child cast (2–3 readers)

```
CAST: NARRATOR, ZARA (age 10), UNCLE TARIQ

NARRATOR: Zara was digging in the garden when her spade hit something hard.

ZARA: Uncle Tariq! Come look. There's something buried here!

TARIQ: (kneeling down) Let's see. This looks like a piece of old pottery.
       Look at that pattern on it.

ZARA: Is it valuable? Like treasure?

TARIQ: Maybe more valuable than treasure. This could be evidence. It could
       show us something ancient. People lived here thousands of years ago.

NARRATOR: Uncle Tariq turned the piece over carefully in his hands.

TARIQ: A long time ago, people lived right here. We call them the Indus
       Valley Civilization. It is one of the oldest in the world. It is as
       old as ancient Egypt.

ZARA: Did they have houses? Did they have cities?

TARIQ: They had huge, carefully planned cities. They had straight streets.
       They had brick houses. They even had a way to carry dirty water away,
       under the ground. That kept the streets clean. That is evidence that
       they were very organized.

ZARA: (overjoyed) So this old broken piece of pottery is a clue?

TARIQ: Exactly. Every piece like this is evidence. Archaeologists dig very
       carefully. They do not want to misplace or damage anything. Each
       piece helps us understand people who lived here long ago.

ZARA: I'm not going to overlook anything else in this garden ever again!

NARRATOR: And from that day, Zara looked at the ground a little differently.
          She wondered what other ancient stories might be buried just
          beneath her feet.
```

(≈220 words. **FK grade: 4.3 · Flesch reading ease: 78.0**.)

**Comprehension check:** (1) What did Zara find in the garden? (2) What does Uncle
Tariq say the Indus Valley Civilization is as old as? (3) Why does he say
archaeologists dig "very carefully"?

### Track B script — for a teen/adult cast (2–3 readers)

```
CAST: NARRATOR, HOST, DR. AHMED (archaeologist)

NARRATOR: A radio interview, recorded on-site at an archaeological dig.

HOST: I'm standing at an important dig site. Dr. Ahmed, for listeners
      who have never heard of the Indus Valley Civilization, what are
      we looking at?

AHMED: We're looking at the remains of one of the world's oldest
       cities. It's about four and a half thousand years old. That's
       roughly the same age as ancient Egypt. What's most striking
       isn't just its age. It's how organized it was.

HOST: Organized how? When I think "ancient civilization," I picture
      something a bit more... rough and simple.

AHMED: That's a common misunderstanding. The evidence proves it wrong.
       These cities had straight streets, laid out in a grid. They
       used bricks of the same size across huge distances. They even
       had covered drains running under the streets, to carry dirty
       water away. Some cities elsewhere would not have anything like
       that again for thousands of years.

HOST: What convinced you this wasn't just a guess based on a few old
      ruins?

AHMED: Fair question. We don't want to overreach past what the
       evidence actually shows. But we're talking about two major
       cities, far apart, with almost the same city plan. That is not
       an accident. It points to shared standards and shared
       knowledge.

HOST: And yet we still don't fully know what happened to them.

AHMED: Correct. That mystery is real, and I won't pretend we know more
       than we do. What we do know, we know from evidence: pottery,
       seals, city layouts, tools. What we don't yet know, we're
       honest about not knowing. That's what keeps people like me
       digging.
```

(≈250 words. **FK grade: 4.9 · Flesch reading ease: 77.9**.)

**Comprehension check:** (1) About how old does Dr. Ahmed say the Indus Valley
Civilization is, and what other ancient civilization does he compare it to? (2)
Name two things he says these cities had that were surprisingly advanced. (3) What
does he say the archaeologists are "honest about not knowing"?

### Discussion — reciprocal roles

**Predictor** (before reading either script): "From the title, what do you expect
this script to be about?" **Questioner**: one answered, one unanswered question (both
scripts leave "what happened to the Indus Valley Civilization" open — that's
deliberate, matching the real historical uncertainty). **Clarifier**: *archaeologist*,
*civilization*, *overreach* — pick one and explain how you worked it out.
**Summarizer**: retell either script in 2–3 sentences.

## Tutor notes

- Readers Theatre works with as few as one reader (read every part aloud, changing
  voice slightly per character) — don't skip this format for a solo adult learner;
  the performance-read step still matters even performing for an empty room or a
  phone recording.
- Common error: rushing the performance read to "get it over with" instead of using
  the rehearsal read to actually fix word-attack problems first. Correction: no
  performance read until every hard word in the rehearsal read was solved by
  decoding, not skipped over.
- Both scripts leave open the real historical question of what happened to the
  Indus Valley Civilization — this is accurate, not a gap in the lesson; avoid
  inventing a tidy answer neither script gives.
