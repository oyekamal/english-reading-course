### Tier-2 vocabulary

**intense** — friendly definition: "very strong, at a high level, not mild."
Examples: "The monsoon brings intense rainfall that can flood streets in minutes." /
"She felt intense relief when the storm finally passed." Generative use: "Describe
something (weather, a feeling, a smell) that can be intense."

**moisture** — friendly definition: "small amounts of water present in the air, soil,
or a surface." Examples: "Warm ocean air carries a lot of moisture, which is why
coastal areas often feel humid." / "Check the soil's moisture before watering a
plant — it might already be damp enough." Generative use: "Name one place or thing
around you right now that has moisture in it."

## 3. Prime the topic (3–5 min)

Before reading: ask "what do you already know about the monsoon, or about big
storms?" Give 2–3 quick facts: storms form when warm, wet air rises and cools;
a slow-moving storm can drop more total rain than a fast one; forecasters give
details like wind direction and speed, not just "rain expected." Orienting
question: "Have you ever changed a plan because of a weather forecast?"

### Track A — What Makes the Monsoon? (for a child)

Every year, for a few months, the wind changes direction over South Asia. It
brings something dramatic with it. We call it the monsoon. Here is how it works.
During the hot months, the land heats up faster than the ocean does. Hot air over
the land rises. To replace it, cooler, wet air rushes in from over the ocean. This
air moves across the coast, straight onto land.

That ocean air carries an intense amount of moisture. It picked this up from
months of sun heating the sea. As the air moves inland, it rises over hills and
mountains. It cools down. All that moisture turns into thick, heavy clouds. These
clouds bring sudden, intense rain. Sometimes it rains for days at a time.

The monsoon is not just dramatic weather. For farmers across the region, it is
essential. Crops are planted to depend on this rain. It usually arrives on the
same schedule every year. Too little rain, and crops can fail from drought. Too
much rain, all at once, and rivers can overflow. Towns and fields can flood.
Understanding the monsoon means understanding both its gift and its danger, at
the same time.

(≈196 words. **FK grade: 3.7 · Flesch reading ease: 83.8**.)

**Comprehension check:** (1) Why does cooler, moisture-heavy ocean air rush in
during the monsoon? (2) What happens to that moisture as the air rises over
hills and mountains? (3) Why does the text call the monsoon both "a gift and a
danger"?

### Track B — Reading a Storm System (for a teen/adult)

A storm does not appear at random. It forms because moisture, heat, and air
pressure interact. This happens in a fairly predictable way. Warm, wet air rises
when it meets cooler air. It also rises when hills push it upward. This is the
same process behind the monsoon. It is just packed into a smaller, more intense
system. As the warm air rises, it cools. Its moisture turns to water fast. This
releases heat. The heat fuels the storm even more. It becomes a cycle that weather
experts watch closely.

This is why storm forecasts give more than just "rain expected." They give wind
direction. They give rainfall totals. They give the storm's speed. All of this
affects real risk. A slow storm can drop far more total rain on one spot than a
fast storm of the same strength. It simply spends more time over the same ground.
This is a major cause of flooding. A simple rain number does not show this on its
own.

For people living where monsoons happen, this detail is not just science talk. It
helps a farmer decide when to plant. It helps a family decide to move valuables
upstairs. It helps a local government decide to open flood shelters early. Reading
a forecast closely, not just the headline, is a genuinely useful adult skill.

(≈222 words. **FK grade: 3.8 · Flesch reading ease: 82.7**.)

**Comprehension check:** (1) What releases heat that "fuels the storm even more"?
(2) Why can a slow-moving storm cause more total rainfall than a fast-moving one of
similar strength? (3) What three examples does the text give of decisions this
detail helps someone make?

## Tutor notes

- Common error: reading *trans-* words as one long unbroken blend instead of peeling
  the prefix first, especially on longer words like *transatlantic*. Correction:
  cover the base after *trans-*, confirm the prefix alone, then uncover and blend.
- This session previews four roots (*port, mit, par(ent)*, and *flate*'s relative
  *flat*) — no full teaching yet; that comes in 5.12–5.15.
- Adult/Pakistan-context note: the monsoon and flood-risk framing in Track B is
  directly relevant, real-world content, not a hypothetical — treat it as genuinely
  useful adult reading, not a simplified science lesson.
