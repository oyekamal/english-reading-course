# L5.14 — Prefixes anti-, mid-, under- · Roots aud, graph/gram, ject, mit/mis · The Internet: How a Message Travels

**Goal:** Read, spell, and use words with *anti-, mid-, under-*; learn four more
commonly-useful roots; apply both to a passage on how a message actually travels
across the internet.
**New today:** *anti-* ("against"), *mid-* ("middle"), *under-* ("below" / "not
enough"); roots *aud* (hear), *graph/gram* (write/draw), *ject* (throw), *mit/mis*
(send). Tier-2 words: *network*, *instant*.

### Track A — A Message's Journey (for a child)

When you send a message on a phone, it feels instant. You type it. You tap send.
It arrives. It seems to travel in no time at all. But it actually takes a real
journey. It is more like a relay race than a single throw.

Your message does not travel as one whole piece. It gets broken into many small
chunks. We call these chunks packets. Each one has an address on it, like a tiny
envelope. It shows where the message should go. Each packet finds its own path.
It travels through a huge network of cables, computers, and routers. Think of
routers as relay runners. Each one reads the address. Each one passes the packet
to the next runner. That runner is closest to where the message needs to go.

Different packets from the same message might take different paths. They race
through the middle of that huge network. They travel at nearly the speed of
light. Then they get put back together in the right order, the instant they all
arrive. That is why, sometimes, a message is a little late. One packet took a
slightly longer path than the others. Everything has to wait for the last one.
Then the whole message can show up. It looks instant because the whole hidden
network is doing an enormous amount of work, incredibly fast.

(≈227 words. **FK grade: 3.5 · Flesch reading ease: 85.8**.)

**Comprehension check:** (1) What is a message broken into before it travels? (2)
What does the text compare a router to? (3) Why is a message occasionally delayed by
a second or two?

### Track B — Packets, Routing, and Why the Internet Sometimes Lags (for a teen/adult)

Sending a message online feels instant. But there is a fairly complex process
underneath. Knowing a simple version of it explains a lot of everyday tech
problems.

Data sent over the internet is broken into small pieces, called packets. This is
true for a message, a photo, or a video call. Each packet carries a small piece
of the data. It also carries an address. That address shows where the data came
from, where it is going, and where it fits in the order.

These packets do not always travel together. Each one finds its own path through
a network of connected devices. A router's job is simple. It looks at a packet's
address. Then it passes the packet toward the next point, closer to that
address. This happens again and again. It may happen dozens of times before the
packet arrives. Under normal conditions, this whole relay takes only a fraction
of a second. That is why a video call feels live, not delayed.

This also explains why a video call sometimes freezes mid-sentence. A few
packets might get delayed, arrive out of order, or get lost completely. This
usually happens because a router along the way gets overloaded, or a connection
drops for a moment. When that happens, your device has to wait for the missing
pieces, or rebuild the correct order. That shows up as a frozen picture or a
garbled word. A weak connection can cause this. So can a busy network. None of
it means your own device is broken. Knowing that is genuinely useful the next
time a call drops mid-word.

(≈267 words. **FK grade: 5.7 · Flesch reading ease: 72.5**.)

**Comprehension check:** (1) What three things does each packet's address say? (2)
What does the text say a router's job is? (3) What three causes does the text give
for why a video call might freeze or drop?

## Tutor notes

- Common error: reading *mit/mis* words and confusing them with the *mis-*
  ("wrongly") prefix from 5.04 — they look similar but are unrelated (one is a root
  meaning "send," the other a prefix meaning "wrongly"). Correction: check the whole
  word's meaning, not just the letters — *dismiss* (send away) vs *misplace*
  (place wrongly).
- Adult learners: the packet/routing explanation is a genuine, simplified-but-honest
  account of how the internet works, not a metaphor standing in for something more
  complicated that's being hidden — it's accurate as far as it goes.
