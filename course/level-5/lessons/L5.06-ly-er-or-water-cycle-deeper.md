# L5.06 — Suffixes -ly, -er/-or · The Water Cycle, Deeper (states of matter)

**Goal:** Read, spell, and use words with *-ly* and *-er/-or*; return to the water
cycle topic from 5.01 with more depth (states of matter, evaporation, condensation);
last phrase-cued session — the markup fades starting next session.
**New today:** suffix *-ly* (turns a word into an adverb — "in this way") and suffix
*-er/-or* (turns a verb into "a person/thing that does it," or makes an adjective
mean "more"). Tier-2 words: *transform*, *invisible*.
**Review:** *sub-, pre-* — *submarine, predict*.

## 2. Word work (10–12 min)

### -ly ("in this way" — turns an adjective into an adverb)

Say: "*-ly* usually turns a describing word (adjective) into a word that describes
*how* something is done (an adverb). *Quick* → *quickly* means 'in a quick way.'"

| Word | Meaning check | Spelling note |
|---|---|---|
| quickly | in a quick way | quick + ly, no change |
| slowly | in a slow way | slow + ly |
| carefully | in a careful way | careful + ly |
| happily | in a happy way | happ**y**→**i** + ly (drop-y-to-i rule from L4.13) |
| quietly | in a quiet way | quiet + ly |
| suddenly | in a sudden way | sudden + ly |
| extremely | to an extreme degree | extreme + ly |
| especially | in a special/particular way | especial + ly |

Dictate 3: *quickly, happily, suddenly* — for *happily*, ask "what happened to the
*y*?" before writing, connecting explicitly back to Level 4's suffix spelling rule.

### -er/-or ("a person or thing that does it" — or "more")

Say: "*-er* and *-or* have two jobs. On a verb, they usually mean 'a person or thing
that does this' — *teach* → *teacher* = 'a person who teaches.' On an adjective,
*-er* instead means 'more' — *big* → *bigger* = 'more big.' Read the base first and
you'll know which job it's doing."

| Word | Job | Meaning check |
|---|---|---|
| teacher | agent (person who...) | a person who teaches |
| worker | agent | a person who works |
| farmer | agent | a person who farms |
| actor | agent (-or spelling) | a person who acts |
| inventor | agent (-or spelling) | a person who invents |
| visitor | agent (-or spelling) | a person who visits |
| bigger | comparative | more big |
| faster | comparative | more fast |

Dictate 3: *teacher, inventor, faster*. No hard rule for choosing *-er* vs *-or* on
agent words — flag this honestly as one to learn word by word (most are *-er*; *-or*
is common after Latin-root verbs like *act, invent, visit, direct* — a pattern that
becomes clearer once roots are taught from 5.11 onward).

Meaning-check sentence: "The ______ (inventor/invention) carefully tested her new
machine." → *inventor* fits (a person), not *invention* (a thing) — a preview of the
part-of-speech distinctions -tion/-sion makes explicit next session.

**Self-correction drill (1 min):** say "she did it *sud-DEN-ly*" (stress shifted)
inside a sentence and have the learner correct it to *suddenly*, using context.
Repeat with one *-er/-or* word.

### Tier-2 vocabulary

**transform** — friendly definition: "to change completely from one form into
another." Examples: "Heat can transform liquid water into invisible water vapor." /
"A caterpillar transforms into a butterfly." Generative use: "Name one thing that
transforms from one state into another that you've seen with your own eyes."

**invisible** — friendly definition: "unable to be seen." Examples: "Water vapor in
the air is invisible until it cools and forms visible droplets." / "Wind is
invisible, but you can see and feel what it does." Generative use: "Name one
invisible thing that you know is there because of what it does."

## 3. Prime the topic (3–5 min)

Before reading: if possible, show or describe an ice cube melting, or a kettle
steaming. Give 2–3 quick facts: water can be solid (ice), liquid (water), or gas
(vapor/steam); heat and cold are what change it from one to another. Orienting
question: "Have you ever seen a window fog up, or a cold drink 'sweat'? What do you
think causes that?"

### Track A — Water, Ice, and Steam (for a child)

> Water / can be three different things. / It changes / from one to another /
> just by getting hotter / or colder. / As ice, / water is a solid. / It is
> hard / and cold. / It keeps its own shape. / Melt the ice, / and it
> transforms / into liquid water. / It is still the same water, / just
> warmer. / Now it flows. / It takes the shape / of whatever holds it. / Heat
> that water / even more, / all the way to boiling. / It transforms again, /
> into steam. / Steam is a gas. / Steam is invisible / water vapor. / The
> little cloud / you see / above a boiling pot / is not the vapor itself. /
> It is tiny water droplets. / They have already / cooled and formed again /
> in the cooler air.
>
> This is exactly / what happens / in the water cycle / you learned about
> earlier. / The sun heats water / until it transforms / into invisible
> vapor. / The vapor rises. / Higher up, / the air is colder. / So the vapor
> transforms back / into tiny liquid droplets. / That is a cloud. / It is the
> same water, / but three different forms. / It changes back and forth, /
> depending only / on how hot / or cold it is.

(≈184 words. **FK grade: 4.0 · Flesch reading ease: 80.0**.)

**Comprehension check:** (1) Name the three forms water can take. (2) What makes
water change from one form to another? (3) The text says the cloud above a boiling
pot isn't the vapor itself — what is it?

### Track B — Why Your Windows Fog Up (for a teen/adult)

> On a cold morning, / the inside of a window / can fog up. / The outside / stays
> clear. / This is a small, / everyday example / of the same physics / behind the
> whole water cycle.
>
> The air inside a warm room / holds invisible water vapor. / It comes from
> cooking, / from breathing, / even from a kettle / boiled earlier. / Warm air /
> can hold more water vapor / than cold air can. / When that warm, / vapor-carrying
> air / touches a cold surface, / like a window / with cold air outside, / it
> cools down fast, / right at that surface.
>
> Cooled air / cannot hold as much vapor. / So the extra vapor / turns back / into
> liquid water. / This is called / condensation, / and it happens / right there /
> on the glass. / It is the same change / that forms a cloud. / It is just
> happening / on your window / instead of / high in the sky.
>
> This is also why / a cold drink / sweats on a hot day. / It is why / bathroom
> mirrors / fog up / after a shower. / Builders even worry / about condensation /
> forming inside walls, / where nobody can see it / or wipe it away.
>
> Understanding this / is genuinely useful. / A dehumidifier / works by cooling
> air / on purpose, / to force this same / condensation / to happen. / Then it
> drains the water away. / This actually removes moisture / from a room, /
> instead of / just hiding the problem.

(≈216 words. **FK grade: 5.8 · Flesch reading ease: 73.5**.)

**Comprehension check:** (1) Why can warm air hold more water vapor than cold air?
(2) What happens when warm, vapor-carrying air touches a cold window? (3) How does
a dehumidifier use this same process?

## Tutor notes

- Common error: applying *-er* meaning "more" to an agent word by mistake (reading
  *teacher* as "more teach"). Correction: ask "is the base word a describing word
  (adjective) or an action word (verb)?" before deciding which job *-er* is doing.
- This is the last phrase-cued session — tell the learner explicitly next session:
  "no more slash marks; let's see if you can still hear where the phrases are on
  your own."
- Adult learners: the condensation/dehumidifier passage is real, useful household
  physics, not simplified science — a genuinely adult topic.
