**Goal:** Read, spell, and use words with *-al* and *-y*; return to the Indus Valley

**New today:** suffix *-al* (turns a noun into an adjective — "relating to") and
suffix *-y* (turns a noun into an adjective — "having the quality of, full of").
Tier-2 words: *resource*, *widespread*.

## 2. Word work (10–12 min)

### -al ("relating to" — turns a noun into an adjective)

| Word | Meaning check | Spelling note |
|---|---|---|
| natural | relating to nature | nature→drop e + al |
| national | relating to a nation | nation + al |
| cultural | relating to culture | culture→drop e + al |
| historical | relating to history | histor(y)→histori + c + al |
| musical | relating to music | music + al |
| personal | relating to a person | person + al |
| seasonal | relating to a season | season + al |
| magical | relating to magic | magic + al |

Dictate 3: *natural, national, musical*.

### -y ("having the quality of, full of" — turns a noun into an adjective)

| Word | Meaning check | Spelling note |
|---|---|---|
| rainy | full of rain | rain + y |
| sunny | having a lot of sun | sun**n** (double the *n*!) + y |
| dirty | full of dirt | dirt + y |
| rocky | full of rocks | rock + y |
| sandy | full of sand | sand + y |
| sleepy | having the quality of needing sleep | sleep + y |
| healthy | having good health | health + y |
| wealthy | having a lot of wealth | wealth + y |

Dictate 3: *sunny, rocky, healthy*. Flag *sunny*: a short, one-syllable base ending
in a single consonant after a single vowel doubles that consonant before *-y*
(the same 1-1-1 doubling rule from Level 4's suffix spelling work) — *sun* → *sunny*,
not *suny*.

Meaning-check sentence: "Archaeologists dig very carefully in ______ (historical/
history) sites so they don't damage what's buried there." → *historical* fits (it's
describing the sites — an adjective job), not *history* (a noun).

**Self-correction drill (1 min):** say "the site was very *his-TOR-i-CAL*" (extra
stress added) inside a sentence and have the learner correct it to *historical*,
using context. Repeat with one *-y* word.

**Word harvest:** find one more *-al* or *-y* word inside today's passage —
*widespread* isn't one (it's not built from either suffix), but see if you can spot
a genuine *-al* or *-y* word in Track A or B below.

### Tier-2 vocabulary

**resource** — friendly definition: "something useful that people can use, especially
something limited that has to be managed carefully." Examples: "Water was a valuable
resource, so ancient cities were usually built near a river." / "Time is a resource
too — you only have so much of it each day." Generative use: "Name one resource
(water, wood, time, money) and describe how someone might manage it carefully."

**widespread** — friendly definition: "happening or existing in many places, not
just one." Examples: "Evidence of trade with faraway regions was widespread across
Indus Valley cities." / "The habit of washing hands is now widespread, but it wasn't
always." Generative use: "Name one thing that is widespread today that probably
wasn't widespread a hundred years ago."

## 3. Prime the topic (3–5 min)

### Track A — Inside an Indus Valley City (for a child)

Imagine walking through Mohenjo-daro. It is a real, ancient city. It is almost
4,500 years old. The streets are straight. They cross each other like a grid,
just like graph paper. Most houses are built from the same size of baked brick.
This is true all across the city, not just in one part. This is widespread. It
tells us people agreed on shared rules for building.

One building stood out the most. It was a large pool called the Great Bath. It
was big enough for several people. It may have been used for special occasions,
not just everyday washing. Nearby, huge brick buildings stored grain. Grain was a
resource every city needed, in case of a bad harvest year.

But the most surprising clue is not a building at all. It is tiny stone seals.
They are carved with pictures of animals and symbols. Nobody has fully read these
symbols yet. The same style of seal has been found far away, in ancient
Mesopotamia. That place is hundreds of kilometers away, across land and sea. This
widespread trade tells us something important. Indus Valley traders were not
alone. They were connected to a much bigger, ancient world.

(≈199 words. **FK grade: 3.8 · Flesch reading ease: 82.8**.)

**Comprehension check:** (1) What tells us people across the whole city "agreed on
shared rules for building"? (2) What was the Great Bath possibly used for? (3) What
do the seals found far away, in Mesopotamia, suggest?

### Track B — Trade Networks of the Indus Valley (for a teen/adult)

Stone weights sound like a boring kind of evidence. But they turn out to be one of
the most important clues we have.

Across Indus Valley cities, hundreds of kilometers apart, archaeologists have found
stone weights that follow the same pattern. This is a strong sign of a shared
system. It was not just separate local habits in each city.

This matters for a simple reason. Trade only works well when both sides trust the
measurement. A trader moving between cities needed to know one thing. A weight
called by one name meant the same real amount everywhere. Without that trust, every
trade would need to start from nothing, every time. The same idea applies to
bricks. Cities across the whole civilization used bricks of the same standard
sizes. That kind of planning only makes sense if there was some shared authority.
Or at least a shared way of doing things, linking cities that were far apart.

The clearest evidence for trade with other lands is a set of small stone seals.
Each seal is carved with an animal and a script nobody has fully read yet. Nearly
the same style of seal has been found far away, in ancient Mesopotamia. This
suggests a real trade network. It stretched across the sea and up through the
Persian Gulf. It was working about four and a half thousand years ago.

(≈229 words. **FK grade: 5.4 · Flesch reading ease: 77.0**.)

**Comprehension check:** (1) Why does the text say trust in measurement matters for
trade? (2) What does using the same brick sizes across far-apart cities suggest? (3)
Where has the same style of Indus seal been found, far from the Indus Valley?

## Tutor notes

- Common error: forgetting the consonant-doubling rule before *-y* (writing *suny*
  or *roky*). Correction: ask "one vowel, one consonant, one syllable? Then double
  it" — the same check taught for other suffixes in Level 4.
- Both passages leave the Indus script genuinely undeciphered — this is historically
  accurate, not a gap; don't invent a translation.
- Adult learners: the standardized-weights argument is real archaeological reasoning
  (how trust enables trade), not simplified for children — a good moment to note
  that "evidence" from 5.04 and "resource management" connect directly to
  reasoning skills used in any field, not just archaeology.
