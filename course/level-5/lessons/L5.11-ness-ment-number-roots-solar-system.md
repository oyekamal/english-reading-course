# L5.11 — Suffixes -ness, -ment · Number Roots · The Solar System

**Goal:** Read, spell, and use words with *-ness* and *-ment*; learn the 9 number
roots (a self-evidently transparent, motivating starting point for root study —
research/05 §5f); apply both to a solar-system knowledge text.
**New today:** suffix *-ness* ("the state/quality of," turns an adjective into a
noun) and *-ment* ("the act/result of," turns a verb into a noun); number roots
*mono-, uni-, bi-, tri-, quad-, pent-, hex-, oct-, dec-*. Tier-2 words: *enormous*,
*distant*.
**Review:** *de-, trans-* — *decrease, transport*.
**Time:** 30–45 min (this session is content-dense — it's fine to run slightly long,
or to split the number-roots section into a short extra 10-minute add-on if time is
tight). **Materials:** paper, pencil, timer.

---

## 1. Retrieval warm-up (3–5 min)

Read: *deflate, transmit, decode*. Heart words (Fry 161–170): **because, turn, here,
why, ask, went, men, read, need, land**. Only *because* has a genuine heart part (it
doesn't break down predictably by sound at all — mark the whole middle section as
irregular, a rare case where more than one small part needs to be memorized). The
rest are fully decodable.

**Timed affix round (30 sec):** name as many of *de-, trans-* (last session) as you
can, meaning or one example word each.

## 2. Word work (10–12 min)

### -ness ("the state/quality of" — turns an adjective into a noun)

| Word | Meaning check | Spelling note |
|---|---|---|
| happiness | the state of being happy | happ**y**→**i** + ness |
| darkness | the state of being dark | dark + ness |
| kindness | the quality of being kind | kind + ness |
| weakness | the state of being weak | weak + ness |
| sadness | the state of being sad | sad + ness |
| awareness | the state of being aware | aware + ness |

Dictate 3: *happiness, kindness, darkness*.

### -ment ("the act/result of" — turns a verb into a noun)

| Word | Meaning check | Spelling note |
|---|---|---|
| movement | the act of moving | move + ment |
| agreement | the result of agreeing | agree + ment |
| enjoyment | the act/state of enjoying | enjoy + ment |
| government | the act/system of governing | govern + ment |
| statement | something stated | state + ment |
| achievement | the result of achieving | achieve + ment |

Dictate 3: *movement, agreement, statement*.

Meaning-check sentence: "The vast ______ (darkness/enjoyment) of space contains
billions of stars, most too distant to see without a telescope." → *darkness* fits.

**Self-correction drill (1 min):** say "the *a-CHEEVE-ment*" ... "no — *a-CHIEVE-
ment*" (vowel shifted) inside a sentence and have the learner correct it to
*achievement*, using context. Repeat with one *-ness* word.

**Word harvest:** find one more *-ness* or *-ment* word inside today's passage —
neither track uses one of today's curated words directly; it's fine to say so.

### Number roots (9 — a simple, transparent starting point for root study)

| Root | Meaning | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| mono- | one | monorail (a train on one rail), monotone (one unchanging tone) |
| uni- | one | unicorn (one horn), uniform (one form/shape for everyone), universe |
| bi- | two | bicycle (two wheels), bilingual (two languages) |
| tri- | three | triangle (three angles), tricycle (three wheels) |
| quad- | four | quadrilateral (four sides), quadruple (four times as much) |
| pent- | five | pentagon (five angles/sides), pentathlon (five events) |
| hex- | six | hexagon (six angles/sides) |
| oct- | eight | octopus (eight arms/legs), octagon (eight sides) |
| dec- | ten | decade (ten years), decimal (based on ten) |

Say: "These roots are the clearest, most self-evident ones in English — once you
know *bi-* means two, you can guess a lot about any new word that starts with it."
Decode each example word, peeling the root first. No dictation drill needed for this
list — instead, play a quick oral game: tutor says a root, learner says the number
and one example word (or vice versa).

Fun fact worth mentioning (not testable): the months *October* and *December* come
from *oct-* (eight) and *dec-* (ten) — because in the old Roman calendar they used to
be the 8th and 10th months. The calendar shifted later, but the names stuck — a good
small example of how word history survives even after the reason for it changes.

### Tier-2 vocabulary

**enormous** — friendly definition: "extremely large — much bigger than usual."
Examples: "The sun is enormous compared to Earth — over a million Earths could fit
inside it." / "She felt an enormous sense of relief when the exam was finally over."
Generative use: "Name something you consider enormous, and compare it to something
smaller."

**distant** — friendly definition: "far away, not close." Examples: "The outer
planets are so distant from the sun that sunlight takes hours to reach them." /
"They kept in touch even though they lived in distant cities." Generative use: "Name
one distant place you'd like to visit."

## 3. Prime the topic (3–5 min)

Before reading: ask "if the sun were the size of an orange, how far away do you
think Earth would be?" — take a guess before reading. Give 2–3 quick facts: the sun
is a huge ball of burning gas; eight planets travel around it; the outer planets are
so far away that even fast spacecraft take years to reach them. Orienting question:
"Have you ever looked up at night and wondered how far away a star or planet really
is?"

## 4. Fluency & Knowledge Text (10–12 min)

Model → echo → choral/partner → **Perform it** (1–2 min: read the whole passage
once more aloud to someone — a family member, the tutor, or a voice note to a
friend — as a small performance; aim for phrasing that follows the punctuation) →
independent (timed, a second and unannounced read — this is the one that gets
scored). WCPM + prosody check after.

### Track A — Our Solar System (for a child)

The sun is the center of our solar system. It is an enormous ball of burning gas.
It is so large that more than a million Earths could fit inside it. Eight planets
travel around the sun. Each one moves on its own path. We call this path an
orbit. Mercury, Venus, Earth, and Mars are the four closest planets. They are
rocky planets. Then come Jupiter and Saturn. These two are enormous planets made
mostly of gas. Jupiter alone is bigger than all the other planets put together.
Furthest out are Uranus and Neptune. They are icy and distant. They are so far
from the sun that they get only a faint bit of its light and warmth.

Earth is special among all eight planets. As far as we know, it is the only one
with the exact right conditions for life. It is not too close to the sun, and not
too distant. It has liquid water and air we can breathe. If you tried to walk
from the sun to Neptune, it would take longer than a human lifetime. It would
take many lifetimes. That is how enormous the distances really are. Pictures in
books make the planets look close together, in a neat little row. But that is
not really true.

(≈215 words. **FK grade: 3.6 · Flesch reading ease: 85.6**.)

**Comprehension check:** (1) How many planets orbit the sun? (2) Which planet is
the biggest? (3) What does the text say makes Earth "special among all eight"?

### Track B — The Scale of the Solar System (for a teen/adult)

Pictures of the solar system are almost always misleading in one way. They show
the planets close together. A picture drawn at the real scale would be mostly
empty space.

Here is a way to feel the real scale. Imagine shrinking the sun down to the size
of a large orange. At that size, Earth would be a tiny grain of sand, about ten
meters away. Jupiter is an enormous planet. Over a thousand Earths could fit
inside it. At our shrunk-down scale, Jupiter would be a marble, about fifty
meters from the orange. Neptune is the most distant planet. It would be a small
pea, almost three hundred meters away. That is the length of several city blocks.

This is not just a fun fact. It is the whole reason space travel is so hard.
Spacecraft travel at enormous speeds. But the distances are so vast that a trip
to Mars, one of Earth's closest neighbors, still takes many months each way. A
trip to a distant planet like Neptune has taken over ten years.

Knowing this scale changes how you read a space news story. "Reached the outer
solar system" does not mean a short trip that just took a bit longer. It means
something on a totally different scale of distance than anything most people
travel in daily life.

(≈222 words. **FK grade: 4.8 · Flesch reading ease: 80.1**.)

**Comprehension check:** (1) In the orange-sized-sun model, roughly how far away is
Earth? (2) Why does the text say pictures of the solar system are "almost always
misleading"? (3) Why does a trip to Neptune take over ten years?

### Discussion — reciprocal roles

**Predictor**: what do you expect each passage to explain about the solar system?
**Questioner**: one answered, one unanswered question (neither passage explains what
lies beyond the solar system). **Clarifier**: explain *orbit*, *scale*, or
*shrunk-down* using context. **Summarizer**: retell in 2–3 sentences, naming at
least two planets.

## Tutor notes

- Common error: mixing up *tri-* and *quad-* or *hex-* and *oct-* — this is normal at
  first; the oral back-and-forth game is more effective early on than a written
  spelling drill for these roots specifically.
- This session's number roots are self-evidently transparent by design (research/05
  §5f) — resist over-explaining; a learner who already half-knows *bicycle* has
  already done most of the work of learning *bi-*.
- Adult learners: the scale-model exercise is genuinely useful general-knowledge
  content (this comes up in real conversations about space missions) — not a
  simplified kids' activity.
