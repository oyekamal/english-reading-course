# Level 5 — Word Power & Fluency

## Entry

Finished Level 4 (The Full Code): can decode multisyllabic pseudowords at ≥90%, knows
syllable division and the schwa, reads simple ungraded text without guessing. If you
have not done Level 4, do that first — this level assumes the full phonics code is in
place and builds *on top of it*, not instead of it.

## Exit

- Reads an A2–B1 level passage aloud at **~100+ WCPM** with good prosody (phrasing,
  expression, self-correction) — see the rubric in §4 below. WCPM is a *floor*, not a
  target to chase; prosody is checked every time alongside it (research/05 §5b.6,
  Rasinski's warning that fluency ≠ speed).
- Knows the meaning of, can read, and can spell words built from all 19 priority-order
  prefixes, 13 high-utility suffixes, 9 number roots, and 20 commonly-useful Greek/Latin
  roots taught in this level (full lists in §2).
- Has a working vocabulary of roughly **2,000–3,000 word families** — Nation's rough
  midpoint between minimally-acceptable (95% coverage, ~4–5k families) and
  comfortable-unassisted (98%, ~8k families) text coverage (research/04, Nation
  2001/2006) — this level is a *step* toward 95%, not the finish line; Level 6–7 continue
  building it through wide/knowledge-rich reading.
- Has finished the Fry first-300 heart-word list (§5) — every remaining high-frequency
  word from Levels 1–4's partial lists is now decoded or heart-marked, none left as
  "just memorize this."
- Can read a multisyllabic word it has never seen before by combining Level 4's
  syllable-chunking routine with morpheme-peeling (prefix/root/suffix) from this level.

## Why this level exists (one paragraph)

Level 4 gave you the code: every grapheme, every syllable type, every spelling rule you
need to sound out almost any English word. Level 5 is where sounding-out becomes
*reading* — smooth, expressive, meaning-driven, and fast enough that you have spare
attention for what the text is actually saying. Two things make that jump happen,
per the evidence: (1) **assisted, scaffolded oral reading practice** of real connected
text, not more isolated phonics drill (NRP d=0.41; Kuhn & Stahl; research/05 §5b), and
(2) **morphology** — prefixes, suffixes, and roots — which lets you unlock a long
unfamiliar word (`disagreement`, `unbreakable`, `transportation`) by recognizing its
parts instead of sounding it out letter by letter every time (Goodwin & Ahn 2013,
d=0.59 for decoding — the largest effect of any morphology outcome; research/05 §1).
Vocabulary and knowledge-building begin here too, because from this level on your
biggest bottleneck stops being "can I decode this word" and starts being "do I know
enough words and enough about the world to understand what I just read."

## 1. What's honest and what's not (read this before you teach it)

This level is built only from what `research/05` supports, and it says so out loud
where the evidence is thin:

- The **20-prefix list is a real, quantified research finding** (White, Sowell &
  Yanagihara 1989: 20 prefixes = ~97% coverage of prefixed words in school reading
  material; *un-* alone ≈ 26%; top 4 ≈ 58%). Teach it in this priority order — it isn't
  arbitrary.
- The **suffix list and the 20-root list are NOT research-ranked the same way.** No
  study equivalent to White/Sowell/Yanagihara exists for suffixes or roots
  (research/05 §1, "Derivational suffixes" and "Greek & Latin roots" subsections). They
  are taught here as a **commonly-useful, convergent pedagogical set**, labelled that
  way throughout this level — never presented to a learner as "research says these are
  the top 20 roots." Say "these are the roots that show up most often in school and
  work reading, by common teaching practice," not "science proved these are the best."
- **Morphology instruction does not reliably improve comprehension or fluency on its
  own** (Goodwin & Ahn 2013: no significant effect on either). Every morphology
  activity in this level is framed as a **decoding + spelling + meaning-check** tool
  ("does that plausible meaning fit the sentence?"), never sold as a comprehension
  shortcut.
- The **"75% of English is Greek/Latin" and "100 roots unlock 5,000 words" claims are
  folk pedagogy, not research** — they do not appear anywhere in this level's teacher
  language.
- The **Hasbrouck & Tindal WCPM norms** used as a rough reference point are K-6
  U.S. school norms, never validated on adult or L2 learners. Every place this level
  cites a WCPM number for adult/ESL learners, it says so.
- **Reader's Theatre's "17 WCPM/year" figure** is repeated consistently across
  secondary sources but not independently re-verified against the 1999 primary source
  in this course's research pass — used here as "a well-supported and widely reported
  benefit," not an exact guaranteed number.
- **Round 1 gauntlet finding, logged not hidden:** the first version of this level
  shipped Track B passages at Flesch-Kincaid grade 10–17 against a stated A2–B1
  (~FK 3–6) target — a real validity failure a critic caught by actually running the
  numbers, which this level's own §1 hadn't done to itself despite auditing every
  other claim here. Every Track B passage (and the mastery-check cold read) was
  rewritten and re-measured; see §4 and GAUNTLET.md Round 1 for the full record.
  Left in this list on purpose, the same way the other caveats above are — the
  point of this section is "here's what we checked and what we didn't," not "here's
  a clean bill of health."

## 2. The 16-session sequence

Sessions rotate through the morphology scope below while a five-topic **knowledge
spine** spirals underneath (each topic is revisited at a deeper level later in the
sequence — this is deliberate: research/06 identifies knowledge-building as the
comprehension engine that starts here and runs through Level 7). Track A/B differ
where the topic itself would otherwise read as childish or as beneath an adult; where
a topic (the water cycle, the heart) has no natural "adult register," the two tracks
differ in framing/example (a farmer needing to predict rain; a nurse checking a pulse)
rather than in content difficulty.

## 3. Session template (30–45 min, every lesson uses this shape)

```
# L5.NN — <Title>
Goal · New today · Review · Time · Materials
1. Retrieval warm-up (3–5 min) — quick recall of the last 2 sessions' morphemes +
   heart words, PLUS a 20–30 second timed affix-automaticity round (see below)
2. Word work (10–12 min) — new prefix/suffix/root: meaning, 6–8 example words, decode +
   spell + meaning-check ("does that plausible meaning fit?"); a 1-minute
   self-correction ("fix my mistake") round on 2 of today's words; a "find one more
   in today's passage" word-harvest bonus; 1–2 Tier-2 vocabulary words with friendly
   definition + 2 examples + a generative task
3. Prime the topic (3–5 min) — brief world/topic knowledge before reading (IES Rec
   3A, strong evidence): a picture/map description or 2–3 key facts about today's
   topic, plus one orienting question linking it to what the learner already knows.
   This runs *before* the passage, not as part of the discussion after it — the point
   is to reduce unknown-topic load going in, not to re-explain after the fact.
4. Fluency & Knowledge Text (10–12 min) — the session's knowledge-spine passage, in
   both tracks; Model → Assisted → **Perform it** (1–2 min, non-RT sessions only —
   see below) → Independent read; WCPM + prosody check against §4
5. Discussion — reciprocal roles (5–7 min) — Predictor / Questioner / Clarifier /
   Summarizer rotate through 4 fixed prompts (see below); this is the "close reading"
   half of the template, folded into the same text as the fluency block rather than a
   second passage — see design note below
6. Write to read (5 min) — a short response task tied to the text and/or the day's
   morphemes
7. Check (3–5 min) — read + spell 6 target-morpheme words, define 1 Tier-2 word,
   read 1 heart word; ≥5/8 correct → next session; <5/8 → the reteach note at the
   bottom of the lesson. Close with an **optional home-practice loop**: reread
   today's passage once more at home, to a family member or aloud to yourself,
   before the next session — extra practice reps for a below-target reader, without
   drilling the same text to death inside the session itself (Rasinski's FDL steps
   9–10; this is the compensating mechanism for the Wide-FORI "don't re-drill"
   design choice in §4).
Tutor notes: common errors + corrections, adult-track notes, Urdu-speaker notes where relevant.
```

**Two new drills, in full (added after the Round 1 gauntlet — see GAUNTLET.md):**

- **Timed affix-automaticity round (warm-up, step 1):** "How many of today's + last
  session's affixes/roots can you name correctly (meaning, or one example word) in
  30 seconds?" Count and log the number; this is a rate check, not a pass/fail gate —
  it exists because the level's single strongest cited effect (Goodwin & Ahn 2013,
  d=0.59 for decoding — the largest morphology effect of any outcome, research/05
  §1) is a *decoding speed* effect, and nothing else in the template measured speed
  on the affixes themselves before this addition.
- **Self-correction / close-approximation drill (Word Work, step 2):** the tutor
  reads one of today's target words aloud *with a plausible but wrong* pronunciation
  (wrong stress, wrong vowel — "we visited the *hot-EL*" for *hotel*, or "she felt
  *un-HAP-py*" with the stress shifted) inside a full sentence, and the learner
  corrects it using sentence context and their knowledge of the word's real parts.
  This trains the specific self-monitoring step between "decode a plausible
  attempt" and "produce the real word" — the same close-approximation skill
  REWARDS Intermediate's preskill Activity F trains explicitly, which this level
  didn't have a version of before this addition.

**Perform it (Fluency & Knowledge Text, step 4, added after the Round 2 gauntlet —
added because Readers Theatre's small-performance payoff, present in 5.04/5.08/5.12,
was otherwise happening only 3 times across 16 sessions):** in every non-RT session
(5.01–5.03, 5.05–5.07, 5.09–5.11, 5.13–5.16 — 13 sessions), after the Assisted step
and before the timed, scored Independent read, spend 1–2 minutes reading the
passage once more out loud *to someone or something* — a family member, the tutor,
a sibling, or a voice note to a friend — as a small performance, not a drill. Give
one concrete thing to aim for each time (phrasing that follows the punctuation,
or expression that matches the content — vary which one is named session to
session). Then run the actual timed, scored Independent read as a second,
unannounced read straight after — this keeps the existing WCPM+prosody instrument
untouched while giving every session, not just the three Readers Theatre ones,
the daily "read for an audience" payoff that Rasinski's FDL treats as load-bearing
(FDL step 6) rather than optional.

**Word harvest (Word Work, step 2, bonus line):** after the day's curated example
words, ask "can you find one more word using today's affix/root inside today's
passage?" (not every passage will have one — that's fine, say so rather than
forcing a match). This ties the curated word list back to the specific text the
learner just read, the same connective step Rasinski's FDL "Word Harvest" step
uses, rather than leaving the two halves of the session (word study, reading)
fully independent of each other.

**Readability audit (Fluency & Knowledge Text, step 4):** every Track B passage in
this level is checked with `textstat.flesch_kincaid_grade()` and the score is
recorded directly under the passage, the same way WCPM/prosody scores are logged —
see GAUNTLET.md Round 1 for why this was added and what it fixed.

**Design note — why fluency and knowledge-text share one passage:** DESIGN.md §4 lists
"Fluency or close reading" and "Knowledge text + discussion" as two blocks. Levels 5–7
run on 30–45-minute sessions, and writing a *second* full 200–400-word passage every
session (on top of the fluency passage) would either shrink every other block to
nothing or blow the time budget. Level 5 uses **one substantial knowledge-spine text
per session** that does both jobs: it is the fluency passage (read aloud, timed,
scored for WCPM+prosody) **and** the close-reading/discussion text (the reciprocal-role
questions in block 5 are about it). This is a deliberate efficiency choice, not a
content cut — every prefix/suffix/root session still gets a full Track A + Track B
passage on a real, spiralling knowledge topic. Level 6 (which is where text-structure
and paragraph-level work becomes the main event) reintroduces a second, separate
close-reading text once the fluency passage's job narrows back down to fluency alone.

### Reciprocal roles (Palincsar & Brown's four roles, used every session from here on)

- **Predictor** — before reading: "What do you think this will be about, from the
  title/first line?" After reading: "Were you right?"
- **Questioner** — after reading: asks 1–2 questions the text answers, and 1 question
  it *doesn't* answer (where would you look next?).
- **Clarifier** — after reading: names one word or sentence that was confusing and how
  it got resolved (reread, morphology, context — never "the picture").
- **Summarizer** — after reading: retells the text in 2–3 sentences.

One learner + one tutor: take two roles each, swap on the next session. Two+ learners:
one role each, rotate. Solo adult learner: talk all four roles aloud, or write brief
answers to each — the value is in doing all four moves, not in the social format.

## 4. Fluency: the program, the rubric, the rotation

**Readability target (added after Round 1 gauntlet — GAUNTLET.md):** every passage in
this level, Track A and Track B alike, targets the level's stated A2–B1 CEFR band,
which maps roughly to **Flesch-Kincaid grade 3–6** (Track A generally sits at the
lower end, ~3–5; Track B, being adult-topic but not adult-*complexity*, targets
~4–6). Every Track B passage is measured with `textstat.flesch_kincaid_grade()`
after writing and the score is printed directly under the passage — the same
discipline this level already applies to WCPM/prosody. Round 1's critic found every
sampled Track B passage running FK 10–17 (upper-high-school-to-graduate-level
prose) despite the level's own A2–B1 claim; every passage in this file set was
rewritten and re-measured to close that gap. **Adult ≠ complex**: Track B stays
adult in topic, tone, and register (work, money, health, technology, history — never
a talking-animal story) while using short-to-medium sentences, minimal
subordination, and vocabulary the learner has actually been taught or that the
passage itself defines in context.

**Sequence, every session (research/05 §5b):**
1. **Model** — tutor/audio reads the passage aloud first, at natural pace with
   expression. The learner does *not* read cold. This sets the prosody target before
   the learner attempts it.
2. **Assisted** — echo (learner repeats phrase by phrase) → choral (read together) →
   partner (alternate sentences/paragraphs). Sessions 5.01–5.04 use full echo+choral;
   from 5.05 on, fade to choral+partner as automaticity builds; from 5.10 on, partner
   only, tutor stepping back further each session.
3. **Perform it** (non-RT sessions only, added after Round 2 — see below) — one more
   read aloud, to a real or imagined audience, as a small performance rather than a
   drill.
4. **Independent** — the learner reads the whole passage alone, once, timed, for the
   WCPM+prosody check. This is a second, unannounced read, separate from the
   performance read in step 3 — performing doesn't get scored; the following read
   does.

**Wide-FORI rotation, not drill-to-death (Kuhn & Stahl; Stahl & Heubach — research/05
§1, §5b.3):** each session's passage is read once, with the full Model→Assisted→
Perform it→Independent scaffold, and then the course *moves to a new passage next
session* — it does not
re-drill the same 300 words five times. This mirrors Wide-FORI (3 different
grade-level texts/week, each read once with scaffolding) rather than FORI (one text,
repeated all week), because Wide-FORI's gains showed up earlier in the year with equal
year-end results, and repeated drilling of a single passage is exactly the kind of
"babyish" fluency practice that discourages teen/adult learners (research/05 §6).
If a learner's independent read is badly below the target, the fix is **not** "read
this same passage five more times" — it is to re-run Model→Assisted on the *same*
passage once more that same session, then move on; a persistent pattern across 3+
sessions is a signal to drop back and check the phoneme-manipulation diagnostic
(research/05 §5c) before adding more fluency volume.

**Phrase-cued texts, sessions 5.01–5.06:** the Track A/B passages in the first six
sessions are printed with `/` marks at natural phrase boundaries (e.g. "The sun /
warms the ocean / and the lakes.") to make prosody's target visible, not just audible.
From 5.07 on the markup is faded — the tutor script says "no more slash marks; can you
still hear where the phrases are?"

**Readers Theatre, sessions 5.04, 5.08, 5.12:** three full scripts are provided (see
those lessons) as an alternative fluency vehicle — no memorization, no costumes,
repeated purposeful oral rehearsal of a script toward a small "performance" (reading
it aloud once more, well, at the end of the session — to the tutor, a sibling, a
phone recording, whoever is present). This is specifically for **older kids, teens,
and adults who find repeated-reading drills babyish** (research/05 §1, §6) — same
skill (automaticity + prosody), adult-compatible framing, and reliably reported gains
roughly equivalent to a year's typical fluency growth (Rasinski, cited widely; primary
source not independently reverified this pass — treat as "well-supported," not exact).

### WCPM + prosody rubric (use both, every time — never WCPM alone)

**WCPM** = words read correctly per minute on the independent read (self-corrections
within ~3 seconds count as correct; count errors, don't just count total words).
Rough reference points, borrowed cautiously from Hasbrouck & Tindal (2017) Spring,
50th-percentile, K-6 U.S. norms (research/05 §5a — **only this partial row was
recoverable in this course's research pass; the full 90-cell table was not verified
and is not reproduced here** — treat every number below as a rough anchor, not a
precise target, especially for adult/ESL learners for whom these norms were never
validated):

| Grade-level anchor | Spring 50th-pct WCPM (children's norm, reference only) |
|---|---|
| Grade 2 | ~100 |
| Grade 3 | ~112 |
| Grade 4 | ~133 |

Level 5's exit target of "~100+ WCPM" sits deliberately at the low end of this range —
it is a floor for "fluent enough to have spare attention for meaning," not a ceiling
to chase. An adult learner reading 80 WCPM with excellent prosody and full
comprehension is doing better than one reading 130 WCPM in a flat monotone who can't
answer a single question about what they read.

**Prosody** — a simplified 4-point scale, adapted from the "phrasing and syntax" and
"expression" dimensions used in oral-reading-fluency research (Rasinski & Samuels
2011's automaticity/pace/prosody framing — research/05 §1). This is an adaptation for
this course, not a verbatim reproduction of any single published rubric:

| Level | What it sounds like |
|---|---|
| 1 | Word-by-word, monotone, ignores punctuation, no phrasing |
| 2 | Some 2–3 word phrases, but choppy; occasional attention to punctuation |
| 3 | Mostly phrase-sized reading, mostly obeys punctuation, some expression |
| 4 | Consistently phrased, obeys all punctuation, expressive, sounds like talking |

Record both numbers every session (a simple log line: `WCPM 78 · Prosody 3`). A rising
WCPM with a flat prosody score is a signal to slow down and re-run the Model/Assisted
steps more carefully next session, not to push for more speed.

## 5. Heart words — finishing the Fry first 300

Levels 1–3 introduced heart words in small batches tied to the phonics being taught
at the time (DESIGN.md §3 master sequence: L1 gives 26 words, L2 gives 26, L3 gives
16 — 68 total, matched to the Dolch/heart-word method: decode the regular part, flag
only the truly irregular part; research/05 §5h, research/02 §1.7). Level 4's off-ramp
lesson (4.15) folds in whichever of the remaining high-frequency words became fully
decodable once r-controlled vowels, diphthongs, and syllable division were taught.
Level 5 is where the list gets **finished** — every word in the Fry first 300 (Fry,
1950s/1980 — research/05 §1, "High-frequency words / heart words") is accounted for,
decoded or heart-marked, by the end of session 5.16.

Each session from 5.01–5.15 folds **2–3 remaining Fry words** into its warm-up
(5–10 seconds each: say it, segment it, mark the heart part if any, move on — this is
review-embedded, not a new 9th block). Session 5.16 runs the **full checklist** below
as part of the mastery check, so nothing is skipped.

**How to read this checklist:** "Decodable by L5" = every grapheme in the word was
taught somewhere in L1–L5's code (including this level's r-controlled/diphthong/
vowel-team/morphology knowledge) — teach it by segmenting and blending, not by rote.
"Heart part" = the specific letter(s) that don't follow the taught code — name them,
mark them (underline, different color, a small heart drawn over just that part), and
that is the only piece committed to memory. Words already covered in L1–L3's explicit
heart-word lists are marked "done in L1–L3" and not re-taught, only reviewed once
if they come up.

## 6. Vocabulary routine (Tier 2, every session)

Every session's Word Work block introduces 1–2 **Tier 2** words (Beck, McKeown & Kucan
— cross-domain, high-utility, low-frequency-in-casual-speech academic/written-register
words; research/05 §1, §5e) using the same four-part routine every time:

1. **Friendly definition** — plain-English, not a dictionary copy ("*reluctant* means
   you don't really want to do something, and it shows").
2. **Contextual example(s)** — 2 sentences in different contexts.
3. **Generative use** — the learner produces their own sentence or answers a
   "would you rather" / "which is more X" prompt using the word actively, not passively.
4. **3+ exposures** — the word resurfaces at least twice more across later sessions
   (a warm-up mention, a "spot it in today's text" check) — never a single encounter
   (Stahl & Fairbanks 1986, d=0.97 for taught-word comprehension when instruction
   combines definitional + contextual + generative use with multiple exposures;
   Nagy, Herman & Anderson 1985/1987 — a single incidental exposure is unreliable
   for learning a new word, roughly ~1 in 20 by their rough figure, exact percentage
   unverified this pass — research/05 §1).

## 7. Extensive reading — real, free sources (verified to exist, checked this pass)

Wide reading is a **supplement, not the primary vocabulary-growth engine** (a single
incidental exposure to a new word is unreliable — research/05 §5e) — pair it with the
explicit Tier-2 routine above, and always attach a comprehension check (Liu & Zhang
2018 meta-analysis: extensive-reading gains are larger when learners answer
comprehension questions about what they read, and when graded readers rather than
unleveled authentic text are used — research/04 §1). Aim for Nation's 95% text-coverage
threshold (research/04 §1: ~4,000–5,000 word families) as the target "comfortable"
reading level by the end of Level 5/into Level 6 — this level's ~2–3k families is a
step toward that, not the finish line.

- **StoryWeaver** (Pratham Books, storyweaver.org.in) — free, Creative-Commons,
  illustrated stories organized into 5 reading levels, in English and 380+ other
  languages (useful if a learner's Urdu is stronger and a bilingual version helps
  bridge). Best for Track A and for adult beginning readers who want short, graded,
  non-childish-content options at the lower levels.
- **Global Digital Library / Global Storybooks** (globaldigitallibrary.io /
  globalstorybooks.net) — free multilingual illustrated storybooks with audio,
  organized by level; similar use case to StoryWeaver, different catalog.
- **ReadWorks** (readworks.org) — free (registration required), 4,800+ nonfiction and
  fiction passages for K-12, sortable by grade/Lexile, with built-in comprehension
  questions — good fit for the knowledge-spine topics in this level (water cycle,
  solar system, simple machines all have ReadWorks passages at multiple levels) and
  for the comprehension-question requirement above.
- **CommonLit** (commonlit.org) — free (registration required), 2,000+ texts sortable
  by readability/grade/genre/theme, many with paired informational texts — good for
  Track B teen/adult content once a learner is reading independently at a B1-ish level
  (Level 6 territory, but strong Level-5 finishers can start sampling it).
- **Project Gutenberg** (gutenberg.org) — 70,000+ free public-domain full texts; not
  graded/leveled, so only use it for adult/teen learners who are already reading
  comfortably at the top of this level, and pick short, plain-language 19th/early-20th
  century texts (fables, simplified classics) rather than dense literary prose.
- **BBC Learning English** (bbc.co.uk/learningenglish) — free adult-oriented ESL site
  with short audio+text lessons, news-in-easy-English style content, and explicit
  vocabulary teaching — a strong Track B fit specifically because it is built for
  adults, not repurposed children's content.

**Practical routine:** once every 2–3 sessions, assign one short graded reader (from
StoryWeaver/Global Digital Library for Track A, or a BBC Learning English short article
for Track B) as **outside-session wide reading** — read once, with light scaffolding
(audio support if available), then answer 2–3 comprehension questions (who/what
happened; one inference; one "what surprised you" or "what would you ask the author").
Log the title; over the 16 sessions this builds a small personal "books/articles I've
read" list, which the mastery check reviews as evidence of the ~2–3k word-family
exposure goal, not as a separate graded assignment.

## 8. Spelling tied to morphology

Every session's Word Work dictation ties spelling directly to the morpheme taught that
day (Graham & Santangelo 2014, d=0.54 for formal spelling instruction vs. none —
research/05 §1, §5g) — never a separate, disconnected spelling list. The rule: **spell
what you can already read and define.** Dictation order each session: (1) the affix/root
alone, (2) 3 words built from it, (3) one full sentence using at least one target word.
Spelling errors that are morpheme-boundary errors (e.g. writing "disagreable" for
"disagreeable," dropping a doubled letter at a prefix/root boundary) get corrected by
re-showing the boundary ("dis- + agree + -able — three pieces, don't lose one"), never
by "sound it out again" alone, since the whole point of morphological spelling is that
the boundary is often not fully sound-predictable.
