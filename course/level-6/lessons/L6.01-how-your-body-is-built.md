# L6.01 — How Your Body Is Built

**Goal:** Learn the description text structure and its signal words; be introduced to the reciprocal-teaching routine (Predictor, Questioner, Clarifier, Summarizer) through a fully modeled tutor dialogue.
**New today:** Text structure — Description (TEACH). Strategy — Question generation (TEACH). Reciprocal teaching routine introduced (MODELED stage).
**Review:** None — this is the first lesson of Level 6.
**Syntax focus:** None yet (intro lesson) — syntax work begins next lesson.
**Time:** 30–45 min
**Materials:** This lesson file, pencil/paper; optional audio recorder for the fluency passage.

---

## 1. Retrieval warm-up (2–3 min)

Since this is the first lesson of Level 6, use these instead of "last lesson" recall:

1. "What is one thing you already know about how your body works?"
2. "Name one part of your body that's on the inside, not the outside."
3. "When you read something new and hard, what do good readers do in their heads?" (Any answer is fine — we're about to name this explicitly.)

## 2. Word work (5–7 min)

Two Tier-2 words from today's text.

**Word 1: organ**
- Friendly definition: a part inside your body that does a specific job — like the heart, the lungs, or the stomach.
- Example 1: "The heart is an organ that pumps blood."
- Example 2: "The skin is actually the body's largest organ."
- Generative task: "Name two organs you can think of, and say one job each one does."
- Urdu note: no direct loanword — this one needs to be taught fresh. (The everyday Urdu word for this concept is عضو / *uzw*, but the English word "organ" itself isn't used in casual speech the way "doctor" or "virus" are.)

**Word 2: structure**
- Friendly definition: the way something is built or put together, so it can do its job.
- Example 1: "A bridge needs a strong structure so it doesn't collapse."
- Example 2: "Bones give your body its structure — without them, you'd be a puddle on the floor."
- Generative task: "Which has a more rigid structure, a bone or a muscle? Why do you think the body needs both kinds?"
- Urdu note: no direct loanword — this one needs to be taught fresh. (Some educated/urban speakers borrow "structure" loosely in conversation, but it isn't a settled everyday loanword the way "doctor" or "hospital" are.)

*Morphology note:* "structure" shares the Latin root *struct-* (to build) with "construct," "instructor," and "destruction." No need to drill this today — just point it out once: "struct- means 'build' — keep an ear out for it in other words."

## 3. Fluency or close reading (5–8 min)

**Phrase-cued passage** — read aloud together, first the tutor models it, then the learner reads it back, pausing naturally at each `/`:

> Your body / is made of trillions of tiny living cells. / Cells group together / to form tissues, / tissues group together / to form organs, / and organs work together / in systems — / like the digestive system / or the circulatory system. / Each level / builds on the one below it, / like bricks / becoming a wall, / and a wall / becoming a house.

Read it twice: once for accuracy, once for expression (pausing at the slashes, letting the voice fall slightly at the end of each phrase).

## 3.5 Prime the topic (3–5 min)

Before reading, briefly build the picture: "Imagine looking inside your own chest, the way an X-ray at a local hospital would show it — you'd see it isn't just 'stuff,' it's built in layers, like a building, from the smallest living pieces all the way up to whole working systems."

Key facts, stated plainly:
1. Your body has trillions of cells, far too small to see without a microscope.
2. Cells group into tissues, tissues group into organs, and organs group into systems — each level built from the one below it.
3. The body has about eleven major systems total, and every one of them depends on its cells staying healthy.

**Orienting question:** "Think of a family elder who's had a checkup at a local clinic — the doctor might have listened to their heart or checked their blood. Which of those do you think is a single cell, which is a tissue, and which is a whole organ?"

## 4. Knowledge text + discussion (15–20 min)

### Naming the strategy and structure

Before reading, tell the learner directly:

> "Today's text uses a pattern called **description** — it explains what something is like by listing its parts and characteristics. Look out for words like **for example**, **such as**, **characteristics**, **for instance**, and **in fact** — those words are signals that the writer is describing something."

> "We're also going to learn a new way of reading together, called **reciprocal teaching**. It has four jobs: the **Predictor** guesses what's coming next, the **Questioner** asks a real question about what we just read, the **Clarifier** finds one confusing word or sentence and works it out, and the **Summarizer** says the gist in one sentence. Today I'll do all four jobs out loud so you can see how it works — then you'll try one."

### The Text: How Your Body Is Built

> Your body is not one single thing — it is a structure built from smaller and smaller parts. Each part is stacked on the one below it, like layers of a building, and each part has its own job. Scientists describe this using four levels: cells, tissues, organs, and systems.

> The smallest living unit of your body is the **cell**. A single cell is too small to see without a microscope, and you have somewhere between 30 and 40 trillion of them. Cells come in many different shapes and jobs. **For example**, red blood cells are shaped like flattened discs, which helps them carry oxygen easily through narrow blood vessels. Nerve cells look very different: they have long, thin arms that stretch out to carry signals over long distances. **Characteristics** of a healthy cell include a nucleus and an outer membrane. The nucleus holds the instructions for how the cell should behave, and the outer membrane controls what enters and leaves.

> **Stop and check (Literal):** About how many cells does your body have? *Answer: Somewhere between 30 and 40 trillion.*

> When similar cells group together to do a shared job, they form a **tissue**. **For instance**, muscle tissue is made of cells that specialize in contracting and pulling. Nerve tissue is made of cells that specialize in sending electrical signals instead. The human body has four main types of tissue: muscle, nerve, connective, and epithelial. Connective tissue includes bone and blood, and epithelial tissue forms the covering of your skin and the lining of your organs.

> Tissues then combine to form an **organ** — a structure built from two or more types of tissue, all working together for one purpose. The heart is a good example. It contains muscle tissue to squeeze, nerve tissue to control the rhythm, and connective tissue to hold it all together. Its overall job is to pump blood. Other major organs include the lungs, the stomach, the liver, the kidneys, and the skin. **In fact**, the skin is the single largest organ in the human body, covering nearly two square meters in an adult.

> **Stop and check (Inferential):** Why does the heart need three different kinds of tissue instead of just one? *Answer: Because pumping blood needs squeezing (muscle), a controlled rhythm (nerve), and something to hold it all together (connective) — no single tissue type can do all three jobs alone.*

> Finally, organs that work together toward a shared goal form a **system**. The heart, blood vessels, and blood together make up the circulatory system, which delivers oxygen and nutrients around the body. The stomach, intestines, liver, and other organs make up the digestive system, which breaks food down into a usable form. The human body has about eleven major systems in total. These include the skeletal system (bones), the muscular system, the nervous system, and the respiratory system (lungs and airways).

> **Stop and check (Literal):** Name two of the eleven major body systems mentioned in the text. *Answer: Any two of — circulatory, digestive, skeletal, muscular, nervous, respiratory.*

> Every one of these levels depends on the one below it. Damage to enough cells can damage a tissue. Damage to a tissue can disable an organ, and a failing organ can affect an entire system. Understanding this layered structure — cell, tissue, organ, system — is the starting point for understanding almost everything else about the body. It explains how the body stays healthy, and what happens when it gets sick.

### Reciprocal teaching — full scripted MODELED dialogue

Read paragraphs 1–2 together, then run this exact dialogue, tutor speaking every role:

> **Tutor as Predictor:** "Before I even start reading, I look at the title — 'How Your Body Is Built' — and I predict this is going to describe body parts from smallest to biggest, or maybe the other way round. Let's see if I'm right."
>
> *(Read paragraph 1 aloud.)*
>
> **Tutor as Questioner:** "Now I ask myself a real question about what I just read. Here's mine: 'Why does the text say the body is built like a building, with layers stacked on each other?' I think it's because each layer is made from the layer below it — cells make tissues, and so on. That's a good comprehension check for myself."
>
> *(Read paragraph 2 aloud — the cells paragraph.)*
>
> **Tutor as Clarifier:** "Here's a word that might be confusing: 'membrane.' I'm not 100% sure what that means just from this sentence. Let me reread it: 'an outer membrane, which controls what enters and leaves.' So a membrane sounds like it works like a gate or a skin around the cell — that makes sense with the word 'outer.' I've clarified it enough to keep reading."
>
> **Tutor as Summarizer:** "One-sentence gist of what I've read so far: 'The body is built in four levels — cells, tissues, organs, and systems — each one made from the smaller one before it.'"

Now hand off the **Questioner** role to the learner for paragraph 3 (the tissues paragraph):

> "Your turn — you're the Questioner for this next paragraph. After I read it aloud, ask me one real question about it — not a yes/no question, a question that needs a real answer."

Read paragraph 3 aloud, then let the learner ask their question. If they struggle, prompt: "What's something in that paragraph you'd want explained more, or that connects two ideas?" Model a sample if needed: "What is the difference between a tissue and an organ?"

Finish reading paragraphs 4–5 together at a normal pace, tutor narrating structure signal words aloud when they appear ("there's 'for instance' again — description signal word").

**Gate before advancing:** If the learner could not attempt even the Questioner role on paragraph 3 with a real question, repeat this lesson's modeled dialogue with a new short passage before moving to L6.02's shared stage.

## 5. Write-to-read (5–8 min)

**Task:** In 2–4 sentences, explain the four levels of the body's structure (cell, tissue, organ, system) in your own words, using at least one signal word from today's list (for example, such as, characteristics, for instance, in fact).

**Model answer:** "The body is built in four levels. Cells are the smallest living parts, for example red blood cells and nerve cells. Similar cells group into tissues, such as muscle tissue or nerve tissue. Tissues combine into organs like the heart, and organs that work together form a system, such as the circulatory system."

## 6. Check (3–5 min)

1. **(Literal)** What are the four levels of body structure named in the text, in order from smallest to biggest?
2. **(Literal)** Which organ is described as the largest in the human body?
3. **(Inferential)** Why does the text compare the body's structure to "layers of a building"?
4. **(Inferential)** Based on the last paragraph, what would likely happen to a system if enough of its cells were damaged?
5. **(Evaluative)** Do you think it's more useful to think about your body one organ at a time, or as a whole set of connected systems? Why?

**Answer key:**
1. Cells → tissues → organs → systems.
2. The skin.
3. Because each level is built from, and depends on, the level below it — the way a building's floors rest on each other.
4. Damage could spread upward — damaged cells could weaken a tissue, which could disable an organ, which could affect the whole system it belongs to.
5. Either answer is acceptable if justified — e.g., "as connected systems, because organs don't work alone" or "one organ at a time is easier to learn first."

**Pass rule:** 4/5 or more correct → move to L6.02. Below that → reread the cells/tissues/organs paragraphs, then retry questions 3 and 4 before continuing.

**Support:** If the learner scores below 4/5, don't just have them reread — draw four labeled boxes (cell, tissue, organ, system) and have them place two examples from the text in each box before retrying questions 3 and 4.
**Challenge:** If the learner scores 5/5 well under time, ask them to name a specific organ not mentioned in the text (e.g., the brain or kidneys) and explain which system it belongs to and why.

---

**Tutor notes:**
- Common error: learner may confuse "tissue" (a group of similar cells) with "organ" (a structure of multiple tissue types) — have them point to the sentence that defines each before answering.
- On an unfamiliar word ("membrane," "epithelial"): sound it out / chunk it, don't guess from the first letter — then use context to check the meaning, as modeled in the Clarifier turn above.
- If the learner freezes on the Questioner role, offer the sentence starter: "Why does...?" or "What would happen if...?" rather than doing it for them.
- Keep the four reciprocal-teaching roles visible on paper or a sticky note during the lesson — the names are new and the learner shouldn't have to hold them all in memory yet.
- Praise any genuine question, even a simple one — the goal today is trying the role, not asking a perfect question.

**Kids-track note:** For a younger strong reader (roughly 8–12), swap the "bricks becoming a wall, a wall becoming a house" image for something they've built themselves — e.g., "like Lego blocks becoming a piece, pieces becoming a model." The biology content stays identical.
