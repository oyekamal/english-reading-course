## 2. Word work (5–7 min)

**purchasing power** — *how much a fixed amount of money can actually buy* (e.g., "When prices rise faster than wages, **purchasing power** falls"). Example 1: "If your salary stays the same but bread, rent, and fuel all get more expensive, your purchasing power has dropped." Example 2: "A currency's purchasing power can be very different in two countries, even if the numbers look similar."
Generative task: "If your allowance stayed exactly the same for five years but everything you buy slowly got more expensive, what would happen to your purchasing power?"
*Urdu note:* no direct loanword — this is a compound economics phrase with no single-word Urdu equivalent in everyday use; teach fresh.

**erode** — *to gradually wear away or reduce something over time* (e.g., "Inflation can slowly **erode** the value of savings"). Example 1: "The riverbank was eroded by years of flowing water." Example 2: "Trust between two countries can be eroded by years of small broken promises."
Generative task: "Name one thing besides money that can be 'eroded' gradually — think of something physical and something more abstract."
*Morphology note:* erode shares a root with "corrode" and "erosion" — all built on a Latin root meaning "to gnaw away."
*Urdu note:* no direct loanword — teach fresh.

**fixed income** — *money a person regularly receives that does not automatically go up when prices rise* (e.g., "People living on a **fixed income**, like a pension, are hit especially hard by inflation"). Example 1: "A retired person on a fixed pension has to plan carefully if prices keep rising." Example 2: "Unlike a fixed income, some jobs give raises that at least partly keep up with rising prices."
Generative task: "Why might inflation be more painful for someone on a fixed income than for someone whose pay regularly increases?"
*Urdu note:* no direct loanword for the whole phrase — though note "fixed deposit" (a bank term, sometimes shortened to "FD") is a widely used loanword-adjacent term in South Asian banking; "fixed income" itself isn't established the same way, so teach it fresh.

## 3. Fluency or close reading (5–8 min): Passive voice unpacking (review)

Pull this sentence from today's text:

> "When prices are pushed upward across almost everything at once, the cause is usually found in one of two places: either too much money is being spent chasing too few goods, or the cost of producing and delivering those goods has itself gone up."

**Task:** Find the passive-voice verbs and rewrite each as an active sentence, naming who or what is actually doing the action.

- "prices **are pushed** upward" → passive; active version: *"[Various forces, e.g., strong demand or rising costs,] push prices upward."* (No single named agent — that's part of the point: inflation is rarely caused by one visible actor.)
- "the cause **is usually found**" → passive; active version: *"Economists usually find the cause..."*
- "too much money **is being spent**" → passive; active version: *"Buyers are spending too much money..."*

Ask the learner: "Why might a writer choose passive voice here instead of naming an exact 'who'?" (Expected: because inflation's cause is often spread across millions of buyers and sellers, not one identifiable actor — passive voice fits when the *doer* is unknown, unimportant, or genuinely diffuse.)

## 3.5 Prime the topic (3–5 min)

Before reading, build the world knowledge that will make the text easier to understand — this is not a quiz, just plain-language framing.

**Picture it:** "Picture a mother doing the monthly grocery shopping, noticing that the same bag of flour or bottle of cooking oil costs more than it did a year ago, even though nothing about the product itself has changed."

**A few facts, plainly stated, before we read:**
1. Inflation means prices for most everyday things slowly rise over time.
2. It happens in almost every country, including Pakistan, to some degree every year.
3. Inflation reduces how much your money can actually buy, even if the number of rupees you have stays exactly the same.

**Orienting question:** "Has your family noticed a local market price — like flour, cooking oil, or fuel — going up over the past year? What do you remember about that?"

## 4. Knowledge text + discussion (15–18 min)

Reciprocal teaching runs **fully independently** — the learner uses all four roles alone or with a partner, pausing after each paragraph.

> **Predictor:** what do you expect the next paragraph to cover? **Questioner:** ask one real question about what you just read. **Clarifier:** name one confusing word or sentence and work out its meaning. **Summarizer:** state the paragraph's main idea in one sentence.

### The Text: Inflation — One Cause, Many Effects on Your Wallet

> Imagine a loaf of bread that cost 100 rupees last year now costs 110 rupees this year, with no change to the bread itself — same size, same ingredients, same bakery. That plain, everyday experience — prices generally rising over time — is called **inflation**, and almost every economy in the world experiences some amount of it, in good years and bad.
>
> **Stop and check (Literal):** In the bread example, how much did the price rise from last year to this year? *Answer: From 100 rupees to 110 rupees, with no change to the bread itself.*
>
> **Why** does this happen? When prices are pushed upward across almost everything at once, the cause is usually found in one of two places: either too much money is being spent chasing too few goods, or the cost of producing and delivering those goods has itself gone up. In the first case, **because** people or businesses have more money to spend than there are goods available to buy, sellers raise prices simply because they can — demand outpaces supply. In the second case, **because** the raw materials, fuel, wages, or transport needed to make and deliver a product have become more expensive, that higher cost is passed along to the customer at the till. Often, both causes act together, tangled up with each other, which is part of why inflation can be hard to fix quickly.
>
> Whatever the original cause, the effects of inflation ripple outward in several directions at once. **As a result** of rising prices, a fixed amount of money buys less than it used to — this loss of **purchasing power** is the most direct effect, and it hits some people much harder than others. **Because** wages do not always rise as fast as prices, many workers find that even though the number on their paycheck looks the same or slightly higher, what that paycheck can actually buy has quietly shrunk. People on a **fixed income** — a pension, for example, that doesn't automatically increase — are especially exposed, since their money's real value is eroded year after year without any adjustment.
>
> **Stop and check (Inferential):** Why are people on a fixed income especially exposed to inflation? *Answer: Because their income doesn't automatically rise along with prices, so their money's real value — its purchasing power — keeps shrinking year after year.*
>
> Inflation also changes people's behavior, not just their bank balances. **Because** money loses value while it sits still, people and businesses are often encouraged to spend or invest sooner rather than later, and to avoid holding large amounts of idle cash. **Consequently**, savers who keep money in a low-interest account may find that even with the small interest a bank pays (as we read in an earlier lesson), the interest doesn't fully keep pace with rising prices — meaning the money is technically growing in number, but shrinking in what it can actually buy.
>
> Governments and central banks take inflation seriously precisely because of these wide-reaching effects, and they have tools — such as adjusting interest rates — aimed at keeping price rises modest and predictable rather than fast and destabilizing. A small, steady rate of inflation is generally considered normal and even healthy for a growing economy; it is *rapid, unpredictable* inflation — where prices rise so quickly that wages, savings, and planning can't keep up — that causes the most damage to ordinary households.
>
> **Stop and check (Literal):** According to the text, what tool do governments and central banks use to try to keep inflation modest and predictable? *Answer: Adjusting interest rates.*
>
> The plain lesson for a family budget is this: inflation doesn't attack any one bill directly, the way a single unpaid debt might. **Instead**, it quietly raises the cost of nearly everything at once — food, rent, transport, school fees — which is exactly why its effects can be harder to notice day to day than a single dramatic price hike, even though, added up over a year, the impact on a family's budget can be just as real.

*(≈640 words · ASL 30.5 words/sentence · FK grade 14.6 · max sentence 56 words — L6.10-16 intentionally run higher, post-syntax-toolkit ramp per Round 2's ruling)*

**Fact-check note:** the 100-to-110-rupee bread example is explicitly illustrative and hypothetical, not a real historical or current price. The description of inflation's causes (demand-pull, cost-push), effects (purchasing power loss, fixed-income exposure), and government responses (interest-rate tools) reflects standard, textbook-level economics and consensus among economists; no specific country's actual inflation rate or currency value is stated or implied.

## 5. Write-to-read (8–10 min): Unit C synthesis task

This closes Unit C (Government & Money). The learner has now read about banks and interest (L6.10), how a government decides where money goes (L6.11), why taxes exist (L6.12), and inflation (L6.13, today).

**Task:** In a short paragraph (5–8 sentences), compare how **taxes** and **inflation** both affect a family's monthly budget. Use at least one idea from the L6.12 taxes text and at least one idea from today's inflation text. Consider: How is a tax bill different from inflation's effect, in terms of how visible or predictable it is? Do both reduce how much a family can actually buy or do with their money? Is either one entirely avoidable for an ordinary family?

**Model answer:** "Taxes and inflation both reduce how much a family can actually do with its money, but in quite different ways. A tax is usually a specific, visible amount — deducted from a paycheck or added at a shop till — and in exchange, it pays for public goods like schools, roads, and hospitals that the family also benefits from, even if they don't see that connection directly. Inflation is much less visible: it doesn't arrive as a single bill, but quietly raises the price of food, rent, and transport all at once, without buying the family anything extra in return. A family can, in principle, reduce some tax through legal means, but almost no ordinary family can protect itself much from inflation, especially if their income doesn't rise as fast as prices do. In both cases, though, the end result for the family's budget is similar: the money they have buys a little less than it otherwise would."

**Rubric (if not using the model answer as a direct check):**
- Mentions at least one specific idea from the taxes text (public goods, contribution, evasion vs. legal avoidance).
- Mentions at least one specific idea from the inflation text (purchasing power, fixed income, rising prices without a single visible bill).
- Makes at least one real comparison (a similarity or a difference), not just two separate summaries side by side.
- Is written in the learner's own words, not copied sentences from either text.

## 6. Check (3–5 min)

1. *(literal)* According to the text, what are the two general causes of inflation?
2. *(literal)* What does "purchasing power" mean, according to the text?
3. *(inferential)* Why are people on a fixed income "especially exposed" to inflation?
4. *(inferential)* Why does the text say inflation's effect on a family budget can be "harder to notice day to day than a single dramatic price hike"?
5. *(evaluative)* The text says a small, steady amount of inflation is "generally considered normal and even healthy." Do you find this claim convincing, based only on what's in the text? Why or why not?

**Answer key:**
1. Too much money chasing too few goods (demand-pull), and rising costs of producing/delivering goods being passed on to customers (cost-push) — often both together.
2. How much a fixed amount of money can actually buy; when prices rise faster than income, purchasing power falls.
3. Because their income (like a pension) does not automatically rise with prices, so the real value of their fixed money is eroded year after year without adjustment, unlike wages that might rise somewhat.
4. Because inflation raises the cost of many small things at once (food, rent, transport, fees) rather than showing up as one large, obvious bill, so the total impact is easy to miss until it's added up over time.
5. Accept reasoned answers either way — a strong answer notes the text only asserts this briefly, contrasts it with "rapid, unpredictable" inflation being harmful, and that the text doesn't fully explain *why* small inflation is considered healthy, so a skeptical reader might want more evidence before fully accepting the claim.

**Pass rule:** 4/5 or more correct → this closes Unit C; move on to L6.14 (Unit D: History Thread). Below that → reread paragraphs 3 and 4 (purchasing power and fixed income) together, then retry questions 3–5 before continuing.

**Support:** If the learner is below the pass rule, reread paragraph 3 (purchasing power and fixed income) sentence by sentence, and have the learner explain in their own words why a fixed pension is hit harder than a rising wage, then retry question 3.
**Challenge:** If the learner passes quickly and easily, have them recall or estimate one everyday item's price a year ago versus now (flour, fuel, a snack) and roughly calculate how much purchasing power was lost on that item.

## Tutor notes

- If the learner treats "inflation" and "a price going up once" as the same thing, redirect to the text's framing: inflation is prices rising *generally, across almost everything*, not one item getting more expensive for its own reason.
- On the passive-voice task, if the learner struggles to find a "who," that's the point of this particular passage — prompt them to notice when a text deliberately avoids naming a single actor because the cause really is spread across many buyers and sellers, rather than treating "no clear who" as a flaw in their own understanding.
- For the synthesis task, watch for the learner writing two separate, unconnected summaries (one about tax, one about inflation) instead of a real comparison — prompt with: "Now write one sentence that uses the word 'but' or 'unlike' to connect the two."
- If the evaluative question (5) gets a flat "yes, because the text says so," push gently: "What would you want to know before you fully believed that claim?" — this reinforces the skeptical-reading habit from L6.09/L6.10 without turning it into a full lateral-reading task.

## Kids-track note

For a younger strong reader, anchor purchasing power in something concrete: "If your favorite snack cost 20 rupees last year and costs 25 rupees now, but your allowance is still exactly the same, you can now buy less of it with the same money — that's inflation shrinking your purchasing power."
