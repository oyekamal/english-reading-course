# L6.06 — The Water Cycle: A Journey With No End

**Goal:** Read a sequence text about the water cycle, running the full reciprocal-teaching
routine independently, and learn to unpack passive-voice sentences to find out who — or what —
is really doing the action.
**New today:** Syntax focus — passive voice. (No new strategy or structure — see below.)
**Review:** Text structure — **Sequence** (first taught in L6.02, "How Your Heart Keeps You
Alive"). All 6 comprehension strategies are now fully folded in — the course does not teach a
7th strategy; instead, use whichever of the six fits naturally as you read.
**Time:** 30–45 min.
**Materials:** This lesson only; pencil/paper.

## 2. Word work (5–7 min)

**evaporate** — *verb* — to change from a liquid into a gas, usually because of heat.
- Example: "A puddle left in the sun will evaporate by the afternoon."
- Example: "Sweat evaporates off your skin, which is part of how your body cools down."
- **Your turn:** Name two liquids you have watched evaporate, and describe what made them
  evaporate faster or slower (heat, wind, size of the puddle).

*Urdu note:* no direct everyday loanword — Urdu speech describes this as "بھاپ بننا" (baap
banna, "turning into steam"), not a borrowed form of "evaporate" — teach fresh.

**condense** — *verb* — to change from a gas back into a liquid, usually because of cooling.
- Example: "Water droplets condense on the outside of a cold glass on a hot day."
- Example: "Steam from a hot shower condenses on the bathroom mirror."
- **Your turn:** Which is more like evaporation and which is more like condensation: fog
  forming on a cold morning, or a wet street drying after rain? Explain your answer.

*Urdu note:* no direct everyday loanword for the verb "condense" itself in this scientific
sense — teach fresh. Worth noting, though: **"condensed milk"** is a genuine, widely recognized
loanword used as-is in everyday Pakistani households (a common grocery item, e.g. "Milkmaid"
brand) — the tutor can use it as a familiar anchor, even though it doesn't teach the general
verb meaning directly.

*Morphology note (optional):* **evaporate** contains the root **vapor** (a Latin-derived root
meaning steam/mist) — the word literally means "to turn into vapor." Recognizing "vapor" inside
"evaporate," "vaporize," and "vaporizer" is a useful pattern-spotting habit, not a required
drill.

## 3. Fluency or close reading (5–8 min)

**Close reading — passive voice.** Pull this sentence from today's text:

> "Water vapor **is carried** by winds across huge distances before it **is finally released**
> as rain or snow."

Work through it with the learner:

1. Find the two passive verbs: "is carried," "is released." Notice the pattern: a form of
   *to be* (is) + a past-participle verb (carried, released).
2. Ask: "Who or what is doing the carrying? Who or what is doing the releasing?" Find the
   answers inside the sentence itself: winds do the carrying; the clouds/weather system does
   the releasing (implied, not always stated).
3. Now try rewriting the sentence in **active voice**, putting the doer first: "Winds carry
   water vapor across huge distances before finally releasing it as rain or snow."
4. Ask: "Why might a science text choose the passive version instead? " (Because the *process*
   — what happens to the water — matters more here than who's doing it; passive voice lets the
   writer keep "water vapor" as the main subject the reader is tracking through the whole
   cycle, rather than jumping between different "doers" like winds, clouds, and the sun.)
5. Have the learner find one more passive-voice sentence in today's text and identify the
   hidden "doer."

## 3.5 Prime the topic (3–5 min)

Picture rain falling on a mountain, soaking into the ground, running down into a stream, and
eventually reaching the sea — the same basic pattern many villages across Pakistan see every
monsoon, when fields fill with rainwater in July and August, only for that same water to
disappear again months later.

Three facts to hold onto before reading: (1) the sun's heat pulls water up off oceans, lakes,
and rivers as invisible water vapor; (2) that rising vapor cools and clusters into the
droplets we see as clouds; (3) clouds eventually release that water back down as rain or snow,
which runs into rivers, soaks into the ground, or freezes — before the whole process starts
again.

**Orienting question:** Think about the monsoon season where you live — have you noticed how
the rain that fills the fields and drains in July eventually seems to vanish by winter, only for
a new round of rain to arrive again the following year?

## 4. Knowledge text + discussion (15–20 min)

### Text structure review: Sequence

Remind the learner: "This text uses **sequence** structure again — like the heart lesson back
in L6.02. Watch for words like **first, next, then, after, finally, meanwhile** that mark each
stage." This is a lighter review, not a full reteach — name it, then move on.

### Strategies: folded in, no new one

All six strategies (question generation, summarizing, monitoring/fix-up, clarifying/inference,
predicting, and graphic organizers as a summarizing tool) are now standing tools. Before
reading, the learner should make a one-sentence prediction from the title, same as last lesson
— but without a card or prompt this time; it's now just part of how they approach any new text.

### The text

> Rain falls on a mountain, soaks into the ground, runs into a stream, and eventually reaches
> the sea. But the story doesn't end there — and it doesn't really have a beginning, either.
> That same water will rise back into the sky and fall again, somewhere else, days, months, or
> even years later. This endless loop is called the **water cycle**. It has no true start or
> finish — only stages that keep repeating.

> **First**, heat from the sun causes water on the surface of oceans, lakes, and rivers to
> **evaporate**. It changes from liquid water into invisible water vapor and rises into the
> air. Plants add to this too — they release water vapor from their leaves, in a related
> process called transpiration. **Meanwhile**, this rising vapor cools as it climbs higher into
> the atmosphere, where temperatures are colder.

> **Stop and check (Literal):** What does the text say plants add to the evaporation process? *Answer: They release water vapor from their leaves, through a process called transpiration.*

> **Next**, that cooling vapor **condenses**. It turns back from gas into tiny liquid droplets,
> which cluster together around dust and other tiny particles in the air. Billions of these
> droplets, clustered together, are what people see as clouds. Water vapor **is carried** by
> winds across huge distances before it **is finally released** as rain or snow. This happens
> once the droplets in a cloud grow heavy enough to fall. This stage is called precipitation.

> **Stop and check (Inferential):** Why do clouds eventually release their water as precipitation? *Answer: Because the water droplets inside a cloud keep clustering together until they grow heavy enough to fall.*

> **After** precipitation falls, it can take several possible paths. This is where the cycle
> branches, rather than running in one straight line. Some water runs directly over the surface
> into streams, rivers, and eventually oceans or lakes — this is called runoff. Some soaks down
> into the ground, becoming groundwater. It can sit there for a long time before slowly
> emerging into a river or a well. Some falls as snow and freezes into ice, staying locked away
> for years or centuries, just like the polar ice caps. A little of it is taken up by plant
> roots. It's released back into the air through transpiration, rejoining the cycle almost
> immediately.

> **Stop and check (Literal):** Name two of the possible paths water can take after precipitation falls. *Answer: Any two of — runoff (over the surface), groundwater (soaking down), ice/snow (freezing), or transpiration (taken up by plant roots).*

> **Finally**, whichever path the water takes, it ends up back in an ocean, lake, river, or the
> ground. From there, it's ready to evaporate again and repeat the whole sequence. This is why
> the water cycle is called a cycle, not a process with a beginning and an end. The water on
> Earth today is, roughly speaking, the same water that has existed for billions of years. It
> just keeps moving, changing form, and moving again. Remember from L6.05 how fresh water is
> such a small, hard-to-reach share of Earth's total water budget? The water cycle is the
> engine that keeps refilling that small share, over and over — so rivers and lakes don't
> permanently run dry.

*(≈452 words · ASL 14.6 words/sentence · FK grade 7.2 · max sentence 24 words)*

### Reciprocal teaching — INDEPENDENT

The learner runs Predictor, Questioner, Clarifier, and Summarizer alone across the whole text,
paragraph by paragraph, with no role cards needed this time — they know the routine now. The
tutor's only job is to sit quietly, take notes on anything worth discussing afterward, and
resist jumping in. Discuss briefly once the text is finished, not mid-reading.

## 5. Write-to-read (5–8 min)

**Task:** In 3–4 sentences, summarize the water cycle in your own words, using at least two
sequence signal words (*first, next, then, after, finally, meanwhile*).

**Model answer:** "First, the sun heats water on the surface of oceans and lakes, and it
evaporates into the air. Next, this water vapor cools and condenses into clouds. Then, once the
droplets grow heavy enough, the water falls as rain or snow. Finally, that water either runs
into rivers, soaks into the ground, or freezes as ice, before eventually evaporating again to
repeat the cycle."

---

## 6. Check (3–5 min)

1. **(Literal)** What is the name for water changing from liquid into gas due to heat?
2. **(Literal)** What are the three main paths water can take after it falls as precipitation?
3. **(Inferential)** Why does the text say the water cycle "has no true start or finish"?
4. **(Inferential)** Find one passive-voice sentence in the text and rewrite it in active voice,
   naming the hidden doer.
5. **(Evaluative)** Is it accurate to say "we're running out of fresh water," given how the
   water cycle constantly refills it? Explain, using something from both this text and L6.05.

**Answer key:**
1. Evaporation.
2. Runoff (into rivers/streams/oceans/lakes), soaking into the ground as groundwater, or being
   taken up by plants and released again as transpiration. (Freezing into ice is also
   acceptable as a fourth path.)
3. Because it's a loop — any point you pick as a "start" (evaporation, condensation,
   precipitation, collection) is really just where you decided to begin describing something
   that was already in motion before and keeps going after.
4. Accept any of: "Water vapor is carried by winds..." → "Winds carry water vapor..."; "it is
   finally released as rain or snow" → "clouds finally release it as rain or snow." Full credit
   for correctly identifying the doer even if the rewrite isn't perfectly worded.
5. Accept reasoned answers. Strong answer: it's partly true and partly misleading — the total
   amount of water on Earth doesn't disappear (the cycle keeps refilling rivers and lakes), but
   L6.05 showed that *usable, accessible* fresh water is still a small, unevenly distributed
   share, so local or regional shortages are real even though the planet's total water supply
   isn't vanishing.

**Pass rule:** 4/5 or more, move to L6.07. Below that, reread the second and third paragraphs
(evaporation → condensation → precipitation) together, then retry questions 3 and 4.

**Support:** If the learner scores below 4/5, draw the cycle out as a simple loop diagram with
the tutor — sun → vapor → cloud → rain/snow → river/ground/ice → ocean → sun again — labeling
each of the four named stages (evaporation, condensation, precipitation, collection) on the
arrows before retrying questions 3 and 4.

**Challenge:** If the learner scores 5/5 quickly, ask them to predict what might happen to how
fast rain runs off versus soaks into the ground if the forests on a hillside were cut down —
this previews the flood/drought connection L6.08 covers directly.

## Tutor notes

- Common error: learners describe the water cycle as a straight line (evaporate → rain → done)
  rather than a loop with branches. Correction: "Where does the water go *after* it falls as
  rain? Does it just stop there?"
- Common error: identifying passive voice by the word "by" alone, missing sentences like "it is
  finally released" where the doer isn't even named. Correction: "Look for 'is/was' plus a verb
  ending like -ed or -en first. Then ask who's doing it — sometimes the sentence doesn't say,
  and that's worth noticing too."
- Common error: treating "runoff" and "groundwater" as the same thing. Correction: "One runs
  over the surface, the other soaks down and out of sight. Which word in the text tells you
  which is which?"
- Keep reciprocal teaching moving — at INDEPENDENT stage, the biggest risk is the tutor
  quietly slipping back into modeling out of habit. Hold the silence.
