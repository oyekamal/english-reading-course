# L6.08 — Floods and Droughts: Naming the Problem, Finding the Fix

**Goal:** Read a problem/solution text about floods and droughts, choose your own sentence to
unpack using any syntax focus learned so far, and complete Unit B with a synthesis task
comparing this unit's texts.
**New today:** Nothing new — this is a consolidation lesson for Unit B.
**Review:** Text structure — **Problem/solution** (first taught in L6.04, "Clean Water, Fewer
Diseases"). Syntax — learner's choice among long-sentence chunking, pronoun reference tracing,
connectives, or passive voice (all previously taught). All 6 strategies folded in.
**Time:** 30–45 min.
**Materials:** This lesson only; pencil/paper.

## 2. Word work (5–7 min)

**variability** — *noun* — how much something changes or varies, rather than staying steady or
predictable.
- Example: "The variability of mountain weather means it can be sunny in the morning and
  snowing by afternoon."
- Example: "Farmers plan around rainfall variability by storing water during wet seasons."
- **Your turn:** Name one thing in your own life with high variability (changes a lot, hard to
  predict) and one thing with low variability (stays steady). Explain the difference.

*Urdu note:* no direct everyday loanword — teach fresh.

**absorb** — *verb* — to take in or soak up a liquid, or (more broadly) to take in something and
hold onto it.
- Example: "Dry soil absorbs rainwater quickly, but soil that's already soaked can't absorb any
  more, so extra water runs off instead."
- Example: "A sponge absorbs spilled water off a counter."
- **Your turn:** Name one surface that absorbs water well and one that doesn't (for example,
  concrete versus soil, or a towel versus glass). What happens to rain that falls on the
  surface that doesn't absorb it?

*Urdu note:* no direct everyday loanword — Urdu uses "جذب" (jazb, an Arabic-derived word) for
this idea, not a borrowed English form — teach fresh.

## 3. Fluency or close reading (5–8 min): Learner's choice

Today the learner picks which sentence to unpack, and which tool to use. Offer these three
candidate sentences from today's text — read all three aloud first, then let the learner choose
one:

> (A) *Long-sentence chunking:* "When heavy rain falls faster than soil, drains, and rivers can
> carry it away — especially in places where forests have been cleared or cities have paved
> over the ground that used to soak up water — the extra water has nowhere to go but sideways,
> across streets and fields, which is what a flood actually is."

> (B) *Pronoun reference tracing:* "A drought does not always mean it never rains; it can also
> mean that rain, when it does come, is too little, too late, or falls somewhere other than
> where it is needed."

> (C) *Passive voice:* "In many flood-prone regions, natural water absorption has been reduced
> by deforestation and paved surfaces, while in many drought-prone regions, water reserves have
> been strained by both reduced rainfall and increased demand."

Whichever the learner picks, apply the matching tool from earlier lessons: for (A), break it
into meaning-sized chunks at the dashes and commas and reread whole; for (B), trace each "it" to
what it refers to (first "it" = rain in general; the "it" instances after = the specific rain
event); for (C), identify the passive verbs ("has been reduced," "have been strained") and name
the hidden doers (deforestation and paving; reduced rainfall and rising demand).

## 3.5 Prime the topic (3–5 min)

Picture a map of one country in two different seasons: in one season, a river has overflowed
its banks and streets are underwater; in another season — or in a different region at the same
time — farmland is cracked and dry, and wells are running low. Both pictures can be true of the
same country, sometimes even the same year.

Three facts to hold onto before reading: (1) floods happen when water arrives faster than the
ground, drains, and rivers around it can carry it away; (2) droughts happen when rain is too
little, too late, or falls somewhere other than where it's needed; (3) the same tools — better
drainage, water storage, restoring the ground's natural absorption — can help with both
problems, because they share the same underlying cause: how well a region manages water.

**Orienting question:** You may have heard about the 2022 floods in Pakistan, when large parts
of Sindh and Balochistan were underwater for weeks, while other regions — like parts of
Tharparkar — still worry about drought and dry wells most years. Why might one country face both
problems, sometimes in the same year?

## 4. Knowledge text + discussion (15–20 min)

### Text structure review: Problem/solution

Remind the learner: "Watch for signal words: **problem, issue, challenge, solution, solve,
address, one answer, in response.**" Light review.

### The text

> Too much water and too little water sound like opposite problems. But they often come from
> related causes, and sometimes even strike the same region in different seasons. Both are
> **problems** worth understanding together — the tools that help with one often help with the
> other too.

> Start with floods. A flood happens when water arrives faster than the ground, drains, and
> rivers around it can carry it away. Heavy or unusually intense rainfall is the most direct
> trigger. But the underlying **issue** is often made worse by human choices. When forests are
> cleared, the ground loses the roots and soil structure that normally soak up rainwater, like a
> sponge. Without that natural absorption, more water runs straight off the surface instead.
> Cities make this **challenge** even more extreme. Roads, parking lots, and rooftops pave over
> land, and pavement absorbs almost nothing. A hard rain in a paved city can flood streets within minutes. The same rainfall on natural ground would have soaked in harmlessly.

> **Stop and check (Literal):** According to the text, what does clearing forests do to the ground's ability to handle heavy rain? *Answer: It removes the roots and soil structure that normally soak up rainwater, so more water runs straight off the surface instead of being absorbed.*

> Now consider droughts — the opposite-looking but related **problem**. A drought doesn't
> always mean it never rains. It can also mean that rain, when it does come, is too little, too
> late, or falls somewhere other than where it's needed. Rainfall **variability** makes water harder to plan around. Long stretches of dry weather, followed by short bursts of intense rain, are less predictable than steady rainfall would be. Rising demand adds to the strain.
> More people, more farming, and more industry all draw from the same rivers, lakes, and
> groundwater. If the water supply doesn't grow along with demand, a temporary dry spell can
> turn into a serious shortage.

> **Stop and check (Inferential):** Why does rainfall variability make droughts harder to plan for than steady rainfall would? *Answer: Because instead of predictable rain, water arrives in long dry stretches broken by short, intense bursts, so it's hard to know when or how much water will actually be available.*

> Neither problem is unsolvable. Most **solutions** work by managing water, rather than
> fighting it outright. **One answer** to flooding is restoring the ground's natural ability to absorb water. This means replanting forests, protecting wetlands, and designing cities with permeable surfaces and green spaces, instead of solid pavement everywhere. Reservoirs and better drainage
> systems can also catch and redirect extra water before it reaches homes and streets. **In
> response** to droughts, communities can store water during wet periods for use during dry
> ones. They can also use water more efficiently in farming — watering only what's needed, when it's needed. They can build early-warning systems too, so people can prepare before a shortage becomes a crisis. Some places even use the same reservoir to **address** both problems at once. It holds back flood water when rain is heavy, then releases it slowly during dry stretches later on.

> **Stop and check (Literal):** Name one solution the text gives for flooding and one for drought. *Answer: Flooding — e.g., restoring natural absorption, reservoirs, permeable city surfaces. Drought — e.g., storing water in wet periods, efficient farming, early-warning systems.*

> Floods and droughts, in other words, are not two separate weather stories. They are two different symptoms of the same underlying issue. That issue is how well, or how poorly, a region manages the uneven, sometimes unpredictable way water moves through it.

*(≈472 words · ASL 13.5 words/sentence · FK grade 7.9 · max sentence 23 words)*

## 5. Write-to-read (8–10 min): Unit B synthesis task

This is longer than a normal write-to-read task because it closes out Unit B (Graham & Hebert,
research/06 §1.7 — response-type writing tasks show the strongest effect, ES≈0.77, and a
synthesis across texts is exactly that kind of demanding response task).

**Task:** In 5–6 sentences, compare the water-cycle text (L6.06) and the climate-change text
(L6.07). Answer: How does the water cycle connect to climate change? What happens to the water
cycle when the climate changes, based on what both texts told you? Use at least one signal word
from each of the two structures (sequence and cause/effect).

**Model answer:** "The water-cycle text described a steady sequence: first evaporation, then
condensation, then precipitation, and finally water returning to oceans, rivers, or ice, ready
to start again. The climate-change text explained that rising temperatures are shifting rainfall
patterns and melting ice faster than before. Because a warmer atmosphere holds and moves water
differently, the same cycle described in L6.06 doesn't run as evenly anymore — some regions get
heavier rain, and others get longer dry stretches. As a result, the water cycle itself hasn't
stopped, but climate change is making its timing and location less predictable. This connects
directly to today's text: it's exactly that unevenness that drives both floods and droughts."

## 6. Check (3–5 min)

1. **(Literal)** According to the text, what does paving over land with roads and parking lots
   do to rainwater?
2. **(Literal)** Name two solutions the text gives for reducing flood risk.
3. **(Inferential)** Why does the text say floods and droughts are "two different symptoms of
   the same underlying issue" rather than two unrelated problems?
4. **(Inferential)** How can the same reservoir help with both flooding and drought, according
   to the text?
5. **(Evaluative)** A city official says, "We should just build higher walls around the river to
   stop floods, and dig deeper wells to stop droughts." Using ideas from the text, explain one
   way this plan might miss a better solution.

**Answer key:**
1. It stops the rainwater from soaking into the ground, so more of it runs off the surface
   instead — which can cause flooding.
2. Any two of: restoring forests/wetlands, designing cities with permeable surfaces/green
   spaces, building reservoirs, improving drainage systems.
3. Because both trace back to how well a region manages the uneven, unpredictable movement of
   water — too much absorption capacity lost causes floods, too little reliable supply causes
   droughts, and both stem from the same mismatch between water supply and water management.
4. By holding back extra water during heavy rain (preventing flooding) and then releasing that
   stored water slowly during dry periods (easing drought) — the same reservoir manages both
   the surplus and the shortage.
5. Accept reasoned answers. Strong answer: walls and deeper wells only treat the symptoms
   (where floodwater goes, and how far down you can dig for water) without addressing why the
   ground can't absorb water in the first place or why demand keeps rising — the text's
   preferred solutions (restoring natural absorption, using water efficiently, planning ahead)
   address the underlying cause rather than just holding water back or chasing it deeper
   underground.

**Pass rule:** 4/5 or more, move to L6.09. Below that, reread the final two paragraphs
(solutions) together and retry questions 4 and 5.

**Support:** If the learner scores below 4/5, draw two arrows for the reservoir example with the
tutor — one labeled "holds back extra water during heavy rain" and one labeled "releases stored
water slowly during dry spells" — and have the learner point to the text sentence that matches
each arrow before retrying questions 4 and 5.

**Challenge:** If the learner scores 5/5 quickly, ask them to propose one additional dual-purpose
solution not mentioned in the text (for example, rainwater harvesting, or restoring a natural
wetland near a city) and explain, in their own words, how it could help with both flooding and
drought.

## Tutor notes

- Common error: treating floods and droughts as unrelated topics rather than two effects of one
  underlying management issue. Correction: "What single sentence at the end of the text ties
  both problems together? Read it again."
- Common error: on the synthesis task, learners summarize each text separately instead of
  actually connecting them. Correction: "You've told me what each text said on its own. Now
  finish this sentence: 'Because of what L6.07 said about climate change, the water cycle from
  L6.06 now...'"
- Common error: choosing the passive-voice sentence (C) in Section 3 but only naming the passive
  verbs without identifying the hidden doer. Correction: "You found the verb. Now — who or what
  is actually doing that action?"
- This is a consolidation lesson; don't introduce anything new even if the learner finishes
  early — use extra time for a second attempt at the synthesis task instead.
