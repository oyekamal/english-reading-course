# L6.02 — How Your Heart Keeps You Alive

**Goal:** Learn the sequence text structure and its signal words; use a sequence-chain graphic organizer to support summarizing; move the reciprocal-teaching routine to the SHARED stage.

## 2. Word work (5–7 min)

**Word 1: circulate**
- Friendly definition: to move continuously around a loop or system, coming back to where it started.
- Example 1: "Blood circulates through your body, delivering oxygen everywhere it goes."
- Example 2: "Warm air circulates through the house from the heater's vents."
- Generative task: "Name one other thing — not blood — that circulates somewhere, and describe its loop."
- Urdu note: no direct loanword — this one needs to be taught fresh. (The everyday Urdu term is گردش کرنا / *gardish karna*, "to go around/circulate," but the English word itself isn't borrowed into casual speech.)

**Word 2: contract** *(used here as a verb: to squeeze/tighten, not an agreement)*
- Friendly definition: to become smaller or tighter by squeezing, usually followed by relaxing again.
- Example 1: "The heart muscle contracts to push blood out, then relaxes to let it refill."
- Example 2: "Your bicep contracts when you bend your arm to lift something."
- Generative task: "Which is more X, a contracting muscle or a relaxed one — tighter or looser? Give one example of your own body doing each."
- Urdu note: many Urdu speakers already use the loanword "contract" (کانٹریکٹ) — but only for the legal/agreement sense, not this squeeze-and-tighten sense. That makes it a false-friend risk: flag the mismatch, since this science meaning still needs to be taught fresh.

*Morphology note:* "contract" shares the root *-tract-* (to pull/drag) with "attract," "subtract," and "tractor" — worth a quick mention since "tract" reappears often in science vocabulary.

## 3. Fluency or close reading (5–8 min)

**Phrase-cued passage:**

> Every time your heart beats, / it squeezes / and pushes blood out / to every part of your body. / First, / blood low in oxygen / flows into the right side of the heart. / Next, / it is pumped to the lungs / to pick up fresh oxygen. / Then, / the oxygen-rich blood / returns to the left side of the heart, / and finally, / it is pumped out / to the rest of the body.

Read once for accuracy, once for expression — notice how the sequence words (first, next, then, finally) naturally get a small pause or emphasis before them.

## 3.5 Prime the topic (3–5 min)

Before reading, briefly build the picture: "Picture a water pump pushing water through pipes to every room of a house, non-stop, day and night, then pulling used water back to be cleaned and sent out again — your heart does something very similar for your whole body."

Key facts, stated plainly:
1. Your heart beats about 100,000 times a day without you ever having to think about it.
2. Blood travels in one continuous loop: to the lungs to pick up oxygen, then out to the rest of the body to deliver it.
3. The heart has four chambers, and blood always moves through them in the same fixed order, never backward.

**Orienting question:** "Have you ever felt someone's pulse — maybe your own wrist, or a family elder's, at a local clinic? What do you think is actually happening inside the chest each time you feel that beat?"

## 4. Knowledge text + discussion (15–20 min)

### Naming the strategy and structure

> "Today's text uses **sequence** structure — it explains something as a series of steps or stages, in order. Watch for signal words like **first, next, then, after, finally, before, meanwhile, stages,** and **steps**."

> "We're also adding a new tool for summarizing: a **sequence chain**. It's just a row of boxes, one per step, that you fill in as you read — it makes summarizing a step-by-step process much easier than trying to hold it all in your head."

> "For the reciprocal-teaching routine today, we move to the **SHARED** stage: I'll model the **Summarizer** role, and you'll take on **Questioner** and **Clarifier**."

### The Text: How Your Heart Keeps You Alive

> Your heart is a fist-sized muscle. It beats about 100,000 times a day, without you ever having to think about it. Its job is to move blood in a continuous loop through your body. It does this through a precise sequence of steps, and that sequence repeats with every single heartbeat.

> First, blood returns to the heart through your veins. This blood has already delivered its oxygen to your body's cells. It flows into the **right atrium**, the upper-right chamber of the heart. This blood is low in oxygen. It also carries carbon dioxide, picked up from the body's tissues. Next, the right atrium contracts. It pushes the blood down into the **right ventricle**, the lower-right chamber. The blood passes through a one-way valve. That valve stops blood from flowing backward.

> **Stop and check (Literal):** According to this paragraph, what kind of blood enters the right atrium? *Answer: Low-oxygen blood, carrying carbon dioxide, returning from the body's cells.*

> After the right ventricle fills, it contracts. It pumps the blood out along the pulmonary artery to the **lungs**. This short loop is called **pulmonary circulation**. Its only job is to trade carbon dioxide for fresh oxygen. Meanwhile, the left side of the heart is getting ready — it will soon receive that newly oxygenated blood.

> **Stop and check (Inferential):** Why is the trip to the lungs described as a "short loop"? *Answer: Because its only job is trading carbon dioxide for oxygen, a much smaller task than pumping blood all the way around the whole body.*

> Then, the oxygen-rich blood flows back from the lungs. It enters the **left atrium**, the upper-left chamber. This chamber contracts and pushes the blood down into the **left ventricle**. The left ventricle is the heart's strongest and most muscular chamber. That's because it has the biggest job of all: pumping blood all the way around the body. This wider loop is called **systemic circulation**.

> Finally, the left ventricle contracts hard. It sends oxygen-rich blood out through the **aorta**, the body's largest artery. From there, blood reaches every organ, muscle, and cell that needs it. Along the way, one-way valves sit between each chamber. They make sure blood only ever flows forward, never backward. This keeps the whole sequence moving in one direction.

> **Stop and check (Literal):** Name one thing the one-way valves between the heart's chambers prevent. *Answer: They prevent blood from flowing backward through the heart.*

> This four-stage sequence — right atrium, right ventricle, lungs, left atrium, left ventricle, out to the body — happens roughly once every second. It repeats like this, at rest, for an entire lifetime. Every chamber first fills with blood, and only then does it squeeze. Filling and pumping alternate in a steady, repeating rhythm, rather than happening all at once. Public health research shows that a healthy heart rhythm keeps this pump working efficiently for decades. Regular exercise and not smoking both help support that rhythm. A damaged or overworked heart, by contrast, can struggle to complete the sequence properly. That's why heart disease is treated as such a serious health concern worldwide. Doctors can even listen to this sequence directly. The familiar "lub-dub" sound of a heartbeat is simply the valves closing, in order, as blood moves through each stage.

### Reciprocal teaching — SHARED stage

Read the first two paragraphs together, then run this dialogue:

> **Tutor as Summarizer** (modeled, after paragraph 2): "My one-sentence gist: 'Blood low in oxygen enters the heart's right side and gets pushed toward the lungs.' That's the summarizer's job — one sentence, the core idea, nothing extra."

Hand off for paragraphs 3–4:

> "Now you take two of the four jobs. After I read paragraph 3, you be the **Questioner** — ask a real question. After paragraph 4, you be the **Clarifier** — find one word or sentence that's a little confusing and work out what it means using the words around it."

If the learner's Clarifier turn stalls, prompt toward "systemic circulation" or "aorta" and model reasoning: "The text says the aorta is 'the body's largest artery' right after saying blood is sent 'to every organ, muscle, and cell that needs it' — so an artery must be some kind of tube blood travels through."

Read paragraphs 5–6 together at normal pace.

**Gate before advancing:** If the learner struggled with both assigned roles, repeat this lesson's split before advancing to L6.03's rotating-roles stage.

### Sequence-chain graphic organizer

On paper, draw six boxes in a row connected by arrows. Fill them in together using the text:

```
[Right atrium] → [Right ventricle] → [Lungs] → [Left atrium] → [Left ventricle] → [Out to body (aorta)]
```

Ask the learner to point to the text sentence that supports each box before writing it in.

## 5. Write-to-read (5–8 min)

**Task:** Using your sequence chain, write 3–4 sentences describing the path blood takes through the heart, using at least two sequence signal words (first, next, then, after, finally).

**Model answer:** "First, blood low in oxygen enters the right atrium. Next, it moves to the right ventricle, which pumps it to the lungs. Then oxygen-rich blood returns to the left atrium and moves to the left ventricle. Finally, the left ventricle pumps it out through the aorta to the whole body."

## 6. Check (3–5 min)

1. **(Literal)** Which chamber of the heart pumps blood out to the lungs?
2. **(Literal)** What is the name of the body's largest artery?
3. **(Inferential)** Why do you think the left ventricle is described as "the heart's strongest and most muscular chamber"?
4. **(Inferential)** What is the purpose of the one-way valves between the heart's chambers?
5. **(Evaluative)** The text says a healthy heart rhythm is supported by "regular exercise and not smoking." Based on the sequence you just learned, why might smoking make the heart's job harder?

**Answer key:**
1. The right ventricle.
2. The aorta.
3. Because its job is to pump blood all the way around the entire body (systemic circulation), which is a much bigger task than the short trip to the lungs — so it needs more muscle power.
4. To make sure blood only flows forward through the sequence, never backward, keeping the loop moving efficiently.
5. Reasonable answers include: smoking can damage blood vessels or reduce how much oxygen the blood carries, forcing the heart to work harder to deliver enough oxygen through the same sequence — any answer connecting smoking to extra strain on the pumping sequence should pass.

**Pass rule:** 4/5 or more correct → move to L6.03. Below that → rebuild the sequence chain together, then retry questions 3 and 5.

**Support:** If the learner scores below 4/5, rebuild the sequence chain together on paper, having them physically point to the text sentence that supports each of the six boxes before retrying questions 3 and 5.
**Challenge:** If the learner scores 5/5 well under time, ask them to explain, without looking at the text, why blood must go to the lungs before it can be pumped out to the rest of the body — reasoning from the sequence, not memory.

---

**Tutor notes:**
- Common error: learner may reverse "atrium" and "ventricle" — anchor it with "atrium is upper, ventricle is lower, and blood always enters the atrium first."
- On an unfamiliar word: sound it out / chunk it, don't guess from the first letter — "pulmonary" chunks as pul-mo-nary; connect it to "pulmonology" or "pulmonologist" if the learner has heard those terms.
- If the learner tries to memorize the sequence by rote rather than reasoning from the chain, redirect them to point at each box and say why blood moves there next.
- Praise correct use of a sequence signal word in the write-to-read task even if a sentence is otherwise imperfect — the syntax habit is the target, not literary polish.

**Kids-track note:** If the learner is a younger strong reader (roughly 8–12), compare the heart's four chambers to a two-story house with two rooms on each floor, and blood "visiting" each room in order — the biology content and sequence stay identical.
