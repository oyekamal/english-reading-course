# L6.15 — From Mughal Court to Colonial Rule

**Goal:** Read and discuss an original text on the Mughal Empire's rise and its transition to
British colonial rule, independently running the reciprocal-teaching routine, reviewing
cause/effect and sequence structure, and completing a comprehensive syntax review (all 4 foci,
one sentence each).
**New today:** No new strategy or structure — comprehensive syntax review is this lesson's
distinct feature.
**Review:** Cause/effect structure, sequence structure, all 6 strategies, reciprocal teaching
(independent), long-sentence chunking, pronoun reference tracing, connectives, passive voice —
all four, one real sentence each from today's text.
**Time:** 30–45 min.
**Materials:** this text; pencil/paper.

## 2. Word work (5–7 min)

**Word 1: dynasty** — a series of rulers from the same family, one after another, often
lasting generations.
- Example: "The Mughal dynasty ruled parts of South Asia for over 300 years, passing power
  from father to son (and sometimes to whoever won a succession struggle)."
- Example: "A dynasty ends when no family member holds power anymore."
- Generative task: Can you think of another dynasty (from any country) you've heard of? What
  made it end, as far as you know?
- Urdu-cognate note: no direct loanword — teach fresh. Urdu already has its own words for this
  idea ("خاندان" *khandan*, "سلسلہ" *silsila*), both Persian/Arabic in origin, but neither shares
  a root with the Greek-derived English word "dynasty" — useful concept overlap, not a cognate.

**Word 2: annex** — to take control of a territory and add it to your own, often by force or
formal agreement rather than by a vote of the people living there.
- Example: "The East India Company gradually annexed one region after another rather than
  conquering the whole subcontinent in a single war."
- Example: "After the war, the victorious country annexed part of its neighbour's land."
- Generative task: What's different between a country being "annexed" and two countries simply
  agreeing to merge as equals? Use your own words.
- Morphology note: **annex** shares its root with **annexe** (a building added onto a larger
  one) — both come from a Latin root meaning "to attach." Useful check: does the new word feel
  like something being "attached" or "added on" to something bigger? That's often the annex-
  family meaning.
- Urdu-cognate note: no direct loanword — teach fresh. Urdu's word for an added-on section,
  "ضمیمہ" (*zamima*, from Arabic), covers a similar idea of "something attached" but comes from
  a completely different root than the Latin *annex* — a helpful parallel idea, not a true
  cognate, so don't teach it as one.

## 3.5 Prime the topic (3–5 min)

Picture a royal court with soldiers, traders, and a new flag being raised over a fort as one
ruling power quietly replaces another — not through one dramatic battle, but over centuries.

Key facts before we read: (1) the Mughal Empire ruled most of the Indian subcontinent for over
300 years, starting in 1526; (2) a private trading company, the British East India Company,
gradually grew stronger as Mughal power weakened, taking control region by region; (3) in 1858,
the British Crown took over direct rule, ending the Mughal dynasty for good.

Orienting question: Do you know of an old fort, gate, or building near where you live — like
Lahore Fort, the Red Fort, or a smaller local one — that was built under Mughal or colonial-era
rule? Has anyone in your family, a grandparent or great-grandparent, ever mentioned stories
connected to that period?

## 4. Knowledge text + discussion (15–20 min)

**Text structure:** Mainly **Cause/effect** (REVIEW), with a **Sequence** backbone (REVIEW).
Cause/effect signal words are bolded; sequence signal words are in *italics*.

### The Text: From Mughal Court to Colonial Rule

> In 1526, a Central Asian prince named Babur, a descendant of both Genghis Khan and Timur,
> defeated the ruling sultan at the First Battle of Panipat and founded what historians call
> the Mughal Empire. Over the next two centuries, Mughal rulers — Babur, his grandson Akbar,
> then later Shah Jahan and Aurangzeb — expanded and administered an empire that, at its peak,
> covered most of the Indian subcontinent and was among the wealthiest and most populous
> states in the world.

> Akbar, who ruled from 1556 to 1605, is often remembered for building a relatively stable
> administrative system and for policies of religious tolerance toward his largely Hindu
> subjects, at a time when many rulers elsewhere governed very differently. **As a result**, the
> empire under Akbar saw long stretches of relative internal stability, which historians
> generally credit as a foundation for the prosperity that followed. Shah Jahan, who ruled next
> in this line, is remembered above all for commissioning the Taj Mahal in Agra, built as a
> mausoleum for his wife Mumtaz Mahal — one of the most recognized buildings in the world today.

> **Stop and check (Literal):** What is Shah Jahan remembered for building, and in which city is
> it located? *Answer: The Taj Mahal, in Agra.*

> Aurangzeb, who ruled from 1658 to 1707, expanded the empire to its largest territorial size,
> but his long reign was also marked by costly wars and, according to many historians, a more
> rigid religious policy than his predecessors' — **which, combined with the strain of
> near-constant military campaigns, contributed to** growing unrest in several regions. **Because
> of** the financial and administrative strain of these campaigns, and **because** succession
> disputes between princes weakened central authority after Aurangzeb's death, the empire
> entered a long period of gradual decline over the 18th century. Regional governors and local
> rulers increasingly acted independently of the Mughal court in Delhi, even while nominally
> still recognizing the emperor's authority.

> **Stop and check (Inferential):** The text says Aurangzeb's reign involved "costly wars" and "a
> more rigid religious policy" than his predecessors'. Why might these two factors together have
> left the empire weaker after his death? *Answer: The wars drained money and resources, while the
> more rigid policy fed unrest — so when succession disputes followed, there was less strength and
> unity left to hold central authority together.*

> ***Meanwhile***, a very different kind of power had been growing along the coasts: the British
> East India Company, a private trading company chartered in 1600, had established trading posts
> and, over time, its own private army. **Because** the Mughal centre was weakening just as the
> Company was strengthening its military and administrative footholds, **the Company was able
> to** expand its control region by region — sometimes through alliances, sometimes through
> war — rather than through a single conquest. ***By the mid-1800s***, the Company effectively
> controlled most of the subcontinent, while the Mughal emperor in Delhi retained only symbolic
> authority.

> ***In 1857***, a major uprising against Company rule broke out, involving soldiers, former
> rulers, and civilians across northern and central India — known to history as the Indian
> Rebellion of 1857 (or the First War of Independence, depending on which historical tradition
> you read). The rebellion was suppressed after roughly a year of fighting. **As a direct
> result**, the British Crown formally ended Company rule and, ***in 1858***, took over direct
> administration of British-held India — a period historians call the British Raj — while the
> last Mughal emperor was exiled, formally ending the dynasty that Babur had founded 332 years
> earlier.

> Remember from L6.11 how a government decides where its money goes? Both the Mughal
> administration and the Company's rule depended on tax collection systems to fund armies and
> building projects — the same basic problem of "raising money and deciding how to spend it"
> that every government, ancient or modern, has to solve.

*(≈708 words · ASL 30.8 words/sentence · FK grade 15.5 · max sentence 65 words — L6.10-16 intentionally run higher, post-syntax-toolkit ramp per Round 2's ruling)*

**Fact check note:** Babur's founding of the Mughal Empire in 1526 (First Battle of Panipat),
Akbar's reign (1556–1605), Shah Jahan's commissioning of the Taj Mahal, Aurangzeb's reign
(1658–1707) and territorial peak, the East India Company's 1600 charter, the 1857 Rebellion, and
the 1858 transfer to Crown rule are all standard, well-established dates in mainstream
historiography. The naming of the 1857 event ("Rebellion," "Mutiny," or "First War of
Independence") differs by historical tradition and country — this text names both common
English terms neutrally rather than picking a side.

### Comprehensive syntax review — one sentence, one focus each

Work through all four, using these exact sentences from the text above:

1. **Long-sentence chunking:** *"Aurangzeb, who ruled from 1658 to 1707, expanded the empire to
   its largest territorial size, but his long reign was also marked by costly wars and,
   according to many historians, a more rigid religious policy than his predecessors' — which,
   combined with the strain of near-constant military campaigns, contributed to growing unrest
   in several regions."* Mark the natural chunk breaks with `/` before rereading aloud.
2. **Pronoun reference tracing:** *"Regional governors and local rulers increasingly acted
   independently of the Mughal court in Delhi, even while nominally still recognizing **the
   emperor's** authority."* — Whose authority is "the emperor's" referring to? (Trace back to
   the subject of the empire, not the nearest noun "Delhi.")
3. **Connectives in context:** Find **"as a result"** and **"because"** in paragraph 4–5. For
   each, name what specific cause it's pointing back to and what effect it's pointing forward
   to.
4. **Passive voice unpacking:** *"The rebellion was suppressed after roughly a year of
   fighting."* — Who is doing the suppressing? (Not named in this sentence — the text avoids
   naming the actor here; ask: why might a writer choose passive voice at this exact point?
   Model answer: to describe the outcome without assigning a specific named actor in a single
   short sentence — the actor, British forces, is clear from context two sentences later.)

## 5. Write-to-read (5–8 min)

**Task:** In 4–6 sentences, explain the chain of cause and effect that led from Mughal decline
to the start of British Crown rule in 1858. Use at least one connective from the text
(*because*, *as a result*, *meanwhile*).

**Model answer:** "Mughal power weakened over the 18th century because of costly wars under
Aurangzeb and succession disputes after his death. Meanwhile, the British East India Company was
growing stronger through trade and its own private army. Because the Mughal centre was weak just
as the Company was strong, the Company was able to expand region by region instead of through
one conquest. In 1857, a major rebellion broke out against Company rule. As a result of the
rebellion, the British Crown ended Company rule directly and took over administration itself in
1858, formally ending the Mughal dynasty."

---

## 6. Check (3–5 min)

1. **(Literal)** Who founded the Mughal Empire, and in what year?
2. **(Literal)** What event in 1858 formally ended Company rule and the Mughal dynasty?
3. **(Inferential)** The text says regional governors "increasingly acted independently... even
   while nominally still recognizing the emperor's authority." What does this suggest about the
   real difference between the emperor's *formal* power and his *actual* power in the empire's
   later years?
4. **(Inferential)** Why does the text describe the East India Company's expansion as happening
   "region by region" rather than through a single conquest?
5. **(Evaluative)** The text mentions two different names for the 1857 event depending on
   historical tradition. Why might a text choose to mention both names rather than only one?

**Answer key:**
1. Babur, in 1526.
2. The British Crown formally ended East India Company rule and took over direct
   administration, exiling the last Mughal emperor.
3. It suggests that by this point the emperor's power had become mostly symbolic or ceremonial
   — real decision-making and control had shifted to regional governors and local rulers, even
   though they still publicly acknowledged him as emperor.
4. Because the Company grew gradually, through alliances and separate conflicts over many
   decades, taking advantage of a weakening Mughal centre bit by bit, rather than defeating one
   unified opponent in one war.
5. Accept reasoned answers — e.g., "so readers understand the event is viewed differently
   depending on perspective, without the text taking a side" or "to be historically accurate and
   fair to more than one tradition of telling the story."

**Pass rule:** 4/5 or more → move on to L6.16 (capstone). Below that → reread the cause/effect
paragraphs (bolded connectives), retry questions 3 and 4, then continue.

**Support:** If the learner scores below 4/5, reread paragraphs 3–4 together aloud, physically
tracing each "because" and "as a result" to its cause and its effect on paper, before retrying
questions 3 and 4.

**Challenge:** If the learner passes quickly and easily, have them build a one-paragraph
timeline connecting Akbar's stability → Aurangzeb's strain → the Company's rise → the 1857
rebellion → 1858 Crown rule, using at least three different connectives from the text.

## Tutor notes

- This is a dense lesson with several names and dates — remind the learner they do NOT need to
  memorize every date; the goal is following the chain of cause and effect, not rote recall.
  Corrective language: "You don't need to remember 1707 — you need to be able to say what
  happened before, and what happened because of it."
- If a learner conflates "the Mughal Empire" with "India" as a single unchanging place across
  all of history, gently clarify: the empire ruled *over* a region that later became parts of
  modern India, Pakistan, and Bangladesh — it wasn't a version of any one modern country.
- On the 1857 naming difference: if a learner asks "which name is right," the honest answer is
  that historians and countries genuinely use different names depending on perspective — this is
  a good moment to model intellectual honesty rather than picking a side.
- Watch for passive-voice sentences being misread as "nobody did this" rather than "someone did
  this, just not named here" — the check question models the corrective move: ask "who did the
  action, even if it's not written down?"

**Kids-track note:** For a younger strong reader, the Taj Mahal detail (a building built as a
symbol of love/grief) is often the most engaging entry point — consider starting discussion there
before moving into the political decline sequence.
