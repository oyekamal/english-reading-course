# L6.12 — Taxes: Why We Pay Them and What They Buy

**Goal:** Read and discuss an informational text on why taxes exist and what they fund; review problem/solution structure and pronoun-reference tracing.

**Review:** Problem/solution structure (L6.04, L6.08), pronoun reference tracing (L6.04), reciprocal teaching (fully independent).
**Time:** 30–40 min

## 2. Word work (5–7 min)

**public good** — *something everyone in a community can use and benefit from, which usually isn't provided well if left only to individuals or private companies* (e.g., "A **public good** like a road benefits everyone who uses it"). Example 1: "Clean air is a public good — everyone breathes it, whether or not they paid for it." Example 2: "Street lighting is a public good most people wouldn't pay for individually, but everyone benefits from having it."
Generative task: "Is a public library more like a public good, or more like a private business? Explain."
*Urdu note:* no direct loanword — this is a compound economics concept with no everyday Urdu equivalent phrase; teach fresh.

**contribute** — *to give something (money, time, effort) as a share toward a larger shared purpose* (e.g., "Taxes are the main way citizens **contribute** to shared services"). Example 1: "Everyone in the group contributed a small amount to buy the gift." Example 2: "She contributes her time every weekend to a local clinic."
Generative task: "Name one way you or your family already 'contribute' to something shared, besides paying tax."
*Morphology note:* contribute breaks into "con-" (together) + "tribute" (a payment or gift given) — the same root family as "tribute" and "distribute." A quick nod is enough; don't force a full detour.
*Urdu note:* no direct loanword — the everyday Urdu word for a shared, voluntary contribution is چندہ (chanda), which is native, not borrowed from English; worth mentioning as a parallel concept, not a cognate.

**evade** — *to deliberately avoid doing something you are supposed to do, often by dishonest means* (e.g., "A person who hides income to **evade** tax is breaking the law"). Example 1: "He tried to evade the question by changing the subject." Example 2: "Evading a fee is different from legally avoiding one — one is dishonest, the other isn't."
Generative task: "What's the difference between 'evading' tax and 'avoiding' tax legally (for example, by claiming a legitimate deduction)?"
*Urdu note:* no direct loanword — teach fresh.

## 3. Fluency or close reading (5–8 min): Pronoun reference tracing (review)

Pull this sentence from today's text:

> "When a family sends a child to a government school, or takes an injured relative to a public hospital, they are usually not paying the full cost of that service in that moment — it was already paid for, gradually, by everyone who contributed tax over the years before them."

**Task:** For each underlined pronoun, find exactly what it refers to.
- "**they**" → refers back to *the family* (the one sending the child / taking the relative).
- "**it**" → refers back to *the full cost of that service* (not the school or hospital itself — the cost).
- "**them**" → refers back to *the family*, again — the people currently using the service, as opposed to everyone who paid tax "before them."

Ask the learner: "What would go wrong if a reader assumed 'it' referred to 'the hospital' instead of 'the cost'? Would the sentence still make sense?" (Expected: it would garble the meaning — a hospital isn't something that gets "paid for" in the same sense as a bill; the sentence is about the *cost* being pre-paid by past taxpayers.)

## 3.5 Prime the topic (3–5 min)

Before reading, build the world knowledge that will make the text easier to understand — this is not a quiz, just plain-language framing.

**Picture it:** "Picture a family's monthly electricity or gas bill sitting on the kitchen table, or a market receipt where a small extra sales-tax charge has been added to the price of what was bought."

**A few facts, plainly stated, before we read:**
1. Taxes are money citizens give so the government can pay for things everyone shares, like roads, schools, and hospitals.
2. No single family could afford to build its own road network or its own hospital alone.
3. Different taxes come from different places — some from income, some from sales, some from property.

**Orienting question:** "Has your family ever noticed tax being added to a bill or a market receipt — on a utility bill, or as a small extra charge at a shop? Where have you seen that happen?"

## 4. Knowledge text + discussion (15–20 min)

Reciprocal teaching runs **fully independently** — the learner uses all four roles alone or with a partner, pausing after each paragraph.

> **Predictor:** what do you expect the next paragraph to cover? **Questioner:** ask one real question about what you just read. **Clarifier:** name one confusing word or sentence and work out its meaning. **Summarizer:** state the paragraph's main idea in one sentence.

### The Text: Taxes — Why We Pay Them and What They Buy

> Imagine a street with no lighting, a city with no paved roads, a neighborhood with no fire service, and a country with no army to defend its borders. Very few individual people, acting alone, would ever choose to pay for all of that themselves — a single family cannot reasonably build its own road network or fund its own hospital. This is the core **problem** taxes exist to solve: some things benefit everyone in a community, but no single person has enough reason, or enough money, to pay for them alone.
>
> **Stop and check (Literal):** According to this paragraph, what is the core problem that taxes exist to solve? *Answer: Some things benefit everyone in a community, but no single person has enough reason or money to pay for them alone.*
>
> Economists call these things **public goods** — services and infrastructure that are available to everyone, whether or not any one person paid directly for them. A lit street helps every driver and pedestrian who passes, not just whoever paid the electricity bill. A **solution** to the public-goods problem is to collect a smaller, shared contribution from everyone — a tax — and use the combined total to pay for things no one household could afford, or would bother to build, alone.
>
> Governments generally collect tax in a few common ways. Income tax takes a share of what people earn from work. Sales tax (sometimes called VAT or GST) adds a small amount to the price of goods and services people buy. Property tax charges owners a yearly amount based on the value of land or buildings they hold. Import duties add a charge to goods brought in from other countries. Each method reaches a slightly different group of people and a slightly different kind of economic activity, which is one reason most governments use several tax types together rather than relying on just one.
>
> **Stop and check (Inferential):** Based on this paragraph, why might a government prefer several different tax types instead of just one? *Answer: Because each method reaches a slightly different group of people and kind of economic activity, so relying on just one would miss others.*
>
> What does this money actually buy? In most countries, tax revenue funds roads and public transport, government-run schools, public hospitals and clinics, police and courts, national defense, and social support for the elderly, the unemployed, or people with disabilities. When a family sends a child to a government school, or takes an injured relative to a public hospital, they are usually not paying the full cost of that service in that moment — it was already paid for, gradually, by everyone who contributed tax over the years before them. This is one reason tax is best understood less like a punishment and more like a shared subscription to services no individual could build alone.
>
> Not everyone agrees on how much tax should be collected, or exactly how it should be spent — that argument is a normal, healthy part of how societies decide their priorities, and different countries make different choices. What is far less debatable is the underlying problem taxes are trying to solve: without some shared contribution, public goods tend to be underbuilt, poorly maintained, or not built at all, because it is always tempting for any one person to hope someone else will pay for it instead — a pattern economists sometimes call the "free-rider problem."
>
> **Stop and check (Literal):** According to this paragraph, what do economists call the pattern where everyone hopes someone else will pay? *Answer: The "free-rider problem."*
>
> This is also why **evading** tax — deliberately hiding income or lying about earnings to avoid paying what is legally owed — causes real harm beyond just the person doing it. Every rupee evaded is a rupee that has to be made up elsewhere, either through higher rates on everyone else, or through a road that doesn't get repaired, a clinic that runs short of supplies, or a school that goes without new books. Legally reducing what you owe through allowed deductions is a different matter entirely from evasion — one follows the rules that were set, the other breaks them.

**Fact-check note:** the taxes described (income, sales/VAT/GST, property, import duties) and the categories of public spending (roads, schools, hospitals, defense, police, social support) are standard, general-purpose civics content and are not tied to any single country's actual current rates or budget figures.

## 5. Write-to-read (5–8 min)

**Task:** In 3–5 sentences, explain the "free-rider problem" in your own words and why taxes are one common solution to it.

**Model answer:** "The free-rider problem is when everyone benefits from something, like a lit street or a public hospital, but no single person wants to pay for it alone, hoping someone else will cover it instead. If everyone thinks this way, the public good never gets built or properly maintained. Taxes solve this by collecting a smaller, shared contribution from everyone, so services that benefit the whole community can be paid for together instead of depending on any one person's goodwill."

---

## 6. Check (3–5 min)

1. *(literal)* According to the text, what is a "public good"?
2. *(literal)* Name three different ways governments collect tax, according to the text.
3. *(inferential)* Why does the text compare tax to a "shared subscription" rather than a punishment?
4. *(inferential)* Why does the text say tax evasion "causes real harm beyond just the person doing it"?
5. *(evaluative)* The text says people disagree on how much tax should be collected and how it should be spent, but not on the underlying problem taxes solve. Do you think this distinction is fair? Explain using one detail from the text.

**Answer key:**
1. A service or piece of infrastructure available to everyone in a community, whether or not any one person paid directly for it (e.g., street lighting, roads).
2. Any three of: income tax, sales tax/VAT/GST, property tax, import duties.
3. Because when you use a government service, you are drawing on money already contributed gradually by many taxpayers over time — you are not paying its full cost in that single moment, much like drawing on a subscription you and others have been paying into.
4. Because money not paid through evasion has to be made up somewhere else — either higher taxes on everyone else, or cuts to services like roads, clinics, or schools — so the harm spreads beyond the individual who evaded.
5. Accept reasoned answers either way; a strong answer references the free-rider problem as the "underlying problem" (widely agreed) versus specific rates/spending choices (which the text explicitly calls debatable and country-dependent).

**Pass rule:** 4/5 or more correct → move on to L6.13. Below that → reread the second and fifth paragraphs (public goods and the free-rider problem) together, then retry questions 3 and 5 before continuing.

**Support:** If the learner is below the pass rule, reread paragraphs 1–2 (the public-goods problem) together sentence by sentence, have the learner name three examples of public goods in their own words, then retry question 1.
**Challenge:** If the learner passes quickly and easily, have them pick one real local public good (a park, a public school, a paved road) and explain in 2–3 sentences what would likely happen to it if nobody paid tax.

## Tutor notes

- If the learner conflates "tax" with "government taking your money for no reason," redirect to the public-goods paragraph — the point isn't to argue tax rates are always fair, it's to understand the actual problem the concept of tax is designed to solve.
- On pronoun tracing, if the learner picks the nearest noun by default rather than checking meaning, ask: "Read the sentence with your guess substituted in — does it still make sense?" This is the same fix-up move used in L6.04, just applied to a longer, more layered sentence.
- Keep the evasion-vs-legal-avoidance distinction crisp; don't let the discussion wander into specific real-world tax controversies, which is out of scope here.
- If the learner struggles with "free-rider problem" as a term, let them describe the idea first in plain words, then attach the term afterward — term-first can feel like jargon-memorization.

## Kids-track note

For a younger strong reader, use a classroom cleanup as the anchor: "If everyone in class hopes someone else will tidy up the shared shelf, it never gets tidied — that's the same problem as the 'free-rider problem' in this text, just with a classroom instead of a country."
