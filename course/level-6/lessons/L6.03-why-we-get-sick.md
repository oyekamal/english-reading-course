# L6.03 — Why We Get Sick — and How the Body Fights Back

**Goal:** Learn the cause/effect text structure and its signal words; learn monitoring/fix-up as a comprehension strategy; practice long-sentence chunking on a real sentence from today's text.
**New today:** Text structure — Cause/effect (TEACH). Strategy — Monitoring/fix-up (TEACH). Syntax — Long-sentence chunking (TEACH).
**Review:** Description (L6.01), Sequence (L6.02), question generation, summarizing + sequence chain.
**Reciprocal-teaching stage:** SHARED, roles rotate — learner takes 2 of the 4 roles, tutor takes the other 2 (mix it up from L6.02's fixed split).

## 2. Word work (5–7 min)

**Word 1: infection**
- Friendly definition: when harmful germs — like bacteria or viruses — get into the body and start to multiply, often making you feel sick.
- Example 1: "A cut that isn't cleaned properly can develop an infection."
- Example 2: "The doctor prescribed antibiotics to treat the infection."
- Generative task: "Which is more likely to cause an infection, a clean bandage or a dirty one? Explain why."
- Urdu note: many Urdu speakers already use this exact word, borrowed from English — "انفیکشن" (infection) is common in everyday and medical speech.

**Word 2: immune** *(as in "immune system")*
- Friendly definition: able to resist or fight off something, especially germs and disease.
- Example 1: "Your immune system is the body's defense force against infection."
- Example 2: "Getting a vaccine can help make you immune to a specific disease."
- Generative task: "Describe one thing that might weaken a person's immune system, and one thing that might strengthen it."
- Urdu note: no direct loanword — this one needs to be taught fresh. (The everyday Urdu term is قوتِ مدافعت / *quwwat-e-mudafiat*, "power of defense," not a borrowing of the English word "immune.")

*Morphology note:* "infection" comes from the root *-fect-/-fic-* (to make/do), shared with "effect" and "perfect" — a light mention is enough; don't force a full lesson on it today.

## 3. Fluency or close reading (5–8 min) — Syntax focus: Long-sentence chunking

Today's syntax slot is long-sentence chunking. Real writing sometimes strings a whole causal
chain into one long sentence. Here is a deliberately long, constructed example on today's exact
topic — one sentence carrying the same idea today's text tells in several shorter ones — built
specifically to give the learner something substantial to chunk:

> "When a germ such as a virus or bacterium enters the body, the immune system detects it as foreign, sends specialized white blood cells to the site, and begins producing antibodies — proteins shaped to recognize and attack that specific invader — which is why recovering from one illness often protects you from catching the exact same one again soon after."

**How to chunk it:** Break the sentence into its natural clause-sized pieces, reading each chunk as its own breath group, then reassemble:

1. "When a germ such as a virus or bacterium enters the body,"
2. "the immune system detects it as foreign,"
3. "sends specialized white blood cells to the site,"
4. "and begins producing antibodies —"
5. "proteins shaped to recognize and attack that specific invader —"
6. "which is why recovering from one illness often protects you from catching the exact same one again soon after."

Ask: "What is the cause in chunk 1, and what are all the effects that follow from it in chunks 2–6?" (Cause: a germ enters the body. Effects: detection → white blood cells sent → antibodies produced → future protection.)

## 3.5 Prime the topic (3–5 min)

Before reading, briefly build the picture: "Picture a busy local clinic waiting room, full of people with coughs, fevers, and cuts — nearly all of them are there because of something too small to see: a germ."

Key facts, stated plainly:
1. Germs are tiny living things — bacteria, viruses, and fungi — that can get inside the body and make you sick.
2. Your immune system is a defense system already built into your body, and it starts fighting germs before you even feel anything.
3. Symptoms like fever aren't the sickness itself — they're often a sign the body is actively fighting back.

**Orienting question:** "Think about the last time you or someone in your family had a fever and went to a local doctor or dawakhana — what do you think was actually happening inside the body at that moment?"

## 4. Knowledge text + discussion (15–20 min)

### Naming the strategy and structure

> "Today's structure is **cause/effect** — it explains why something happens and what results from it. Watch for signal words like **because, as a result, therefore, due to, so, consequently,** and **leads to**."

> "Today's strategy is **monitoring/fix-up** — this means noticing, while you're reading, the moment you stop understanding, and having a plan to fix it: reread the sentence, read on a bit and come back, or look at a word's parts. Good readers don't just barrel through confusion — they notice it and pause."

> "For reciprocal teaching today we're **SHARED with rotating roles** — I'll take two jobs, you'll take the other two, and we'll switch partway through so you get practice with different roles than last time."

### The Text: Why We Get Sick — and How the Body Fights Back

> Getting sick usually has a clear cause: a germ. Germs are tiny organisms — mainly bacteria, viruses, and fungi. They are too small to see without a microscope. Some of them can make you ill when they get inside your body. Public health research has taught us something very useful. It's the cause-and-effect chain from "a germ enters the body" to "you feel sick" to "you get better."

> **Stop and check (Literal):** What does the text say germs are too small to see without? *Answer: A microscope.*

> When a germ such as a virus or bacterium enters the body, the immune system detects it as foreign. It sends specialized white blood cells to the site. These cells begin producing antibodies — proteins shaped to recognize and attack that specific invader. This is why recovering from one illness often protects you from catching the exact same one again soon after. This whole process is called an **immune response**. It is the direct effect of the germ's presence. It is also the reason many of the uncomfortable symptoms of being sick actually appear.

> **Because** the immune system's response to infection includes raising body temperature, a fever is often a good sign. It usually means your body is actively fighting something, not that something has gone wrong. **As a result**, a mild fever is sometimes best left to run its course, rather than being suppressed right away. A very high or long-lasting fever, though, should always be checked by a doctor. Similarly, a runny nose or cough is often the effect of the body trying to flush germs out of the airways and sinuses.

> **Stop and check (Inferential):** Why might it be a mistake to always try to bring a mild fever down right away? *Answer: Because the fever is part of the immune system actively fighting the infection, so suppressing it isn't automatically helpful — though a very high or long-lasting fever still needs a doctor.*

> Some germs spread from person to person more easily than others. This has a direct effect on how fast an illness can travel through a community. **Due to** this, public health guidance has long emphasized a few simple habits. These include covering a cough or sneeze, staying home when contagious, and — most famous of all — washing your hands. Handwashing physically removes germs from the skin. This stops them from being transferred to the mouth, nose, or eyes, or passed to another person through touch. **So**, it remains one of the single most effective, low-cost ways to reduce the spread of illness.

> **Stop and check (Literal):** According to the text, name two simple habits public health guidance recommends. *Answer: Any two of — covering a cough or sneeze, staying home when contagious, washing your hands.*

> This basic cause-and-effect understanding is often called **germ theory**. Germs cause illness. The body's immune response causes symptoms and eventual recovery. Hygiene habits reduce the spread of germs in the first place. Germ theory replaced much older, incorrect ideas about what caused disease. **Consequently**, modern medicine, clean water systems, and everyday habits like handwashing all trace back to one causal insight. Illness has physical causes we can identify, interrupt, and often prevent.
*(≈437 words · ASL 13.2 words/sentence · FK grade 7.7 · max sentence 23 words)*

### Reciprocal teaching — SHARED, rotating roles

Read paragraphs 1–2 together, then:

> **Tutor as Predictor** (before reading paragraph 3): "I predict this next paragraph is going to explain why we get symptoms like fever, since the last paragraph mentioned the immune response causing 'uncomfortable symptoms.' Let's check."
>
> *(Read paragraph 3.)*
>
> **Tutor as Clarifier:** "The phrase 'run its course' might be confusing. Rereading the sentence — 'a mild fever is sometimes best left to run its course' — I think it means letting the fever continue naturally instead of stopping it right away. That fits with the idea that the fever is doing a job."

Now switch — hand the learner the other two roles for paragraphs 4–5:

> "Your turn for **Questioner** after paragraph 4, and **Summarizer** after paragraph 5."

If needed, model a sample question for paragraph 4: "Why does the text call handwashing 'one of the single most effective, low-cost ways' to reduce illness?" And a sample summary for paragraph 5: "Germ theory says illness has physical causes we can find and prevent."

### Monitoring/fix-up, applied live

Pause mid-reading of paragraph 4 and ask: "Did that last sentence make full sense to you? If not, what's your fix-up move — reread it, or keep going and come back?" Model choosing to reread if needed.

**Gate before advancing:** If the learner needed heavy prompting on 2+ roles, repeat this lesson's rotation with a new passage before advancing to L6.04's guided stage.

## 5. Write-to-read (5–8 min)

**Task:** Write 3–4 sentences explaining the cause-and-effect chain from "a germ enters the body" to "you recover," using at least two cause/effect signal words (because, as a result, therefore, due to, so, consequently, leads to).

**Model answer:** "When a germ enters the body, the immune system detects it because it recognizes the germ as foreign. As a result, white blood cells and antibodies are produced to fight it. Because this immune response can raise body temperature, a fever is often a sign the body is fighting the infection. Consequently, once the antibodies deal with the germ, the person usually recovers."

## 6. Check (3–5 min)

1. **(Literal)** What are the three main types of germs named in the text?
2. **(Literal)** What does the text say handwashing physically does to germs?
3. **(Inferential)** Why might a fever be a sign that the body is working correctly, not a sign that something has gone wrong?
4. **(Inferential)** What is the cause-and-effect relationship between "germs spread easily between people" and "public health guidance emphasizes hygiene habits"?
5. **(Evaluative)** The text says a very high or long-lasting fever should always be checked by a doctor. Why do you think the text includes that caution, given everything else it says about fevers being a normal part of fighting illness?

**Answer key:**
1. Bacteria, viruses, and fungi.
2. It physically removes germs from the skin before they can be transferred to the mouth, nose, eyes, or another person.
3. Because the fever is described as part of the immune response actively fighting the germ, not a separate problem — it's an effect of the body doing its job.
4. Because germs that spread easily have a bigger effect on a whole community, public health guidance responds by promoting habits (handwashing, staying home, covering coughs) that interrupt that spread — the cause (easy spread) leads to the effect (stronger hygiene guidance).
5. Reasonable answers should note that while a mild fever is a normal, useful effect of the immune system working, a very high or prolonged fever can signal a more serious problem or a risk to the body itself — the text is being careful not to imply "never worry about a fever."

**Pass rule:** 4/5 or more correct → move to L6.04. Below that → reread the fever and handwashing paragraphs, then retry questions 3 and 4.

**Support:** If the learner scores below 4/5, reread the fever and handwashing paragraphs together, having them underline the exact cause/effect signal word in each sentence before retrying questions 3 and 4.
**Challenge:** If the learner scores 5/5 well under time, ask them to think of one everyday habit besides handwashing that interrupts the germ-to-illness chain, and explain which step it interrupts.

---

**Tutor notes:**
- Common error: learner may treat "fever" and "illness" as the same thing rather than fever being one *effect* of fighting illness — have them point to the sentence that frames fever as part of the immune response.
- On chunking the long sentence: if the learner tries to read it in one breath and loses the thread, physically point to each chunk boundary (comma, dash) and have them pause there before continuing.
- On an unfamiliar word: sound it out / chunk it, don't guess from the first letter — for "antibodies," note "anti-" (against) + "bodies" as two recognizable word parts.
- When rotating roles, name explicitly who has which job before each paragraph — don't assume the learner remembers the rotation without a verbal cue.

**Kids-track note:** If the learner is a younger strong reader (roughly 8–12), it can help to frame white blood cells and antibodies as the body's "defense team" and germs as "invaders" — a simple military metaphor already implicit in "immune response," just made a little more vivid.
