# L6.04 — Clean Water, Fewer Diseases

**Goal:** Learn the problem/solution text structure and its signal words; learn clarifying/inference as a comprehension strategy; practice pronoun reference tracing on a real sentence from today's text; close Unit A (Body & Health) with a short synthesis task comparing two Unit A texts.
**New today:** Text structure — Problem/solution (TEACH). Strategy — Clarifying/inference (TEACH). Syntax — Pronoun reference tracing (TEACH).
**Review:** Description (L6.01), Sequence (L6.02), Cause/effect (L6.03); question generation, summarizing, monitoring/fix-up.
**Reciprocal-teaching stage:** GUIDED — learner leads 3 of the 4 roles, tutor coaches and only steps in when needed.
**Time:** 30–45 min
**Materials:** This lesson file, pencil/paper.

## 1. Retrieval warm-up (2–3 min)

1. "What signal words tell us a text is using cause/effect structure?" (because, as a result, therefore, due to, so, consequently, leads to)
2. "What does 'monitoring/fix-up' mean when you're reading?" (noticing when you've lost understanding, and having a plan to fix it — reread, read on, or check a word's parts)
3. "Name one organ or system we've learned about so far in Unit A." (any answer from L6.01–L6.03)

## 2. Word work (5–7 min)

**Word 1: sanitation**
- Friendly definition: systems and practices — like clean toilets, sewers, and waste disposal — that keep water and surroundings free from germs and dirt.
- Example 1: "Good sanitation, like proper sewer systems, keeps human waste from contaminating drinking water."
- Example 2: "The town improved its sanitation by building a modern water treatment plant."
- Generative task: "Name one example of good sanitation and one example of poor sanitation, and explain the difference between them."
- Urdu note: no direct loanword — this one needs to be taught fresh. (The everyday Urdu term is صفائی ستھرائی / *safai satharai*, "cleanliness," not a borrowing of the English word "sanitation.")

**Word 2: contaminate**
- Friendly definition: to make something dirty, polluted, or unsafe by adding something harmful to it.
- Example 1: "Sewage leaking into a river can contaminate the drinking water downstream."
- Example 2: "Unwashed hands can contaminate food before it's eaten."
- Generative task: "Which is more likely to contaminate a water supply, a well far from any sewage, or a well right next to an open drain? Explain your reasoning."
- Urdu note: no direct loanword — this one needs to be taught fresh. (The everyday Urdu term is آلودہ کرنا / *aaluda karna*, "to pollute," not a borrowing of the English word "contaminate.")

*Morphology note:* "contaminate" and "contamination" share the same base — noticing that the noun form just adds "-ion" is a useful, low-effort spelling/vocabulary connection; no need to expand into a full suffix lesson today.

## 3. Fluency or close reading (5–8 min) — Syntax focus: Pronoun reference tracing

Today's syntax slot is pronoun reference tracing. Here is a deliberately longer, constructed
sentence on today's exact topic — the same idea today's text tells in three shorter sentences,
combined into one so there's a genuine multi-pronoun chain to trace:

> "When a community builds a water treatment system, it removes harmful bacteria and parasites before they ever reach a household tap, which means fewer people fall ill and fewer children miss school because of it."

**How to trace it:** For each pronoun, ask "what noun does this stand in for?" and draw an arrow back to it.

- "**it** removes harmful bacteria" → *it* = the water treatment system
- "before **they** ever reach a household tap" → *they* = the harmful bacteria and parasites
- "because of **it**" → *it* = (trickier) refers back to the whole earlier idea — building a water treatment system reducing contamination — not a single noun. Point out that pronouns sometimes refer to a whole idea, not just one word, and that's worth pausing on.

Ask the learner to reread the sentence aloud, replacing each pronoun with what it stands for, to confirm the sentence still makes sense.

## 3.5 Prime the topic (3–5 min)

Before reading, briefly build the picture: "Picture the water pump or tap your own family uses at home, or a water tanker delivering to your street — now imagine tracing that water all the way back to where it came from, and asking what might have gotten into it along the way."

Key facts, stated plainly:
1. Water can carry disease-causing germs if it isn't treated or is allowed near waste.
2. Simple, low-cost fixes — boiling water, purification tablets, proper toilets — can break the cycle from contamination to illness.
3. Clean water and good sanitation work together; neither one alone fully solves the problem.

**Orienting question:** "Does your family boil drinking water, use a filter, or trust the tap directly? Why do you think that choice matters?"

## 4. Knowledge text + discussion (15–20 min)

### Naming the strategy and structure

> "Today's structure is **problem/solution** — it names a problem clearly, then explains one or more solutions. Watch for signal words like **problem, issue, challenge, solution, solve, address, one answer,** and **in response**."

> "Today's strategy is **clarifying/inference** — this means using clues in the text, plus what you already know, to figure out something the text doesn't say directly. It's a step further than just noticing confusion (that was monitoring/fix-up) — here we actually work out the answer using inference."

> "For reciprocal teaching today we move to **GUIDED**: you lead three of the four roles, and I only step in to coach if you get stuck."

### The Text: Clean Water, Fewer Diseases

> Access to clean water is one of the oldest problems in public health. It's also one of the most solvable. Yet it remains a serious **challenge** in many parts of the world. The **problem** is straightforward to state: contaminated water can carry disease-causing germs. Human or animal waste can get into the water. That water may then carry bacteria that cause cholera and typhoid, or parasites that cause other serious illnesses. Those germs go directly into the mouths of anyone who drinks the water, cooks with it, or washes food in it.

> **Stop and check (Literal):** What two kinds of germs can contaminated water carry, according to the text? *Answer: Bacteria (which can cause cholera and typhoid) and parasites (which cause other serious illnesses).*

> Contamination usually happens when sanitation is poor. Sewage or waste from toilets, farms, or open drains sometimes isn't properly contained. It can then seep into rivers, wells, and underground water sources. Communities rely on those same sources for drinking water. In places without safe sanitation, this creates a repeating cycle. Contaminated water causes illness. Illness — especially diarrheal disease in young children — can itself cause dehydration, malnutrition, and even death, if left untreated.

> **Stop and check (Inferential):** Why does the text call this a "repeating cycle"? *Answer: Because contaminated water causes illness, and that illness — especially in young children — can itself lead to further harm, so poor sanitation and illness keep reinforcing each other unless something breaks the cycle.*

> **One answer** to this problem is water treatment, well established in public health research. When a community builds a water treatment system, it removes harmful bacteria and parasites. This happens before those germs ever reach a household tap. **As a result**, fewer people fall ill, and fewer children miss school because of it. Treatment methods range widely. In cities, this often means large-scale filtration and chlorination plants. In places without full infrastructure, simple household solutions work too — boiling water, or using approved water-purification tablets.

> **A second solution addresses** the sanitation side of the problem directly. It means building proper toilets and sewage systems, so waste never reaches the water supply in the first place. This is often paired with basic hygiene education. Even clean water can become contaminated again, if it's stored, handled, or poured with unwashed hands. Public health research has repeatedly linked improved sanitation and handwashing habits to real drops in diarrheal disease and other water-related illness.

> **Stop and check (Literal):** What does the text say basic hygiene education is often paired with? *Answer: Building proper toilets and sewage systems (the sanitation solution) — because even clean water can be recontaminated by unwashed hands.*

> **In response to** this well-understood problem, governments, international organizations, and local communities have spent decades building wells, treatment plants, and sanitation systems. Their shared goal is breaking the contamination-to-illness cycle. Progress has been real and measurable in many regions. Still, the **challenge** is far from fully solved worldwide — millions of people still lack reliable access to safely managed drinking water. That's why clean water and sanitation remain a central, ongoing global health priority. Remember from L6.02 how blood carries oxygen through the body in a continuous loop? Clean water works on a similar idea, at a community scale. Instead of a pump moving oxygen through blood vessels, a treatment system moves safe water through pipes. Either loop breaking down causes serious harm downstream.
*(≈460 words · ASL 12.8 words/sentence · FK grade 9.4 · max sentence 22 words)*

### Reciprocal teaching — GUIDED

Read paragraph 1 together as a warm-up, tutor modeling briefly if needed, then hand off:

> "From here, you lead. Choose which role to use after each paragraph — Predictor, Questioner, Clarifier, or Summarizer — just make sure all three roles besides mine get used somewhere across the text. I'll only jump in if you get stuck."

Suggested coaching prompts if the learner stalls (offer only if needed, don't lead by default):
- Predictor stuck: "What do you expect 'one answer' in the next paragraph to be, based on the problem just described?"
- Questioner stuck: "What's something in that paragraph you'd want the writer to explain more?"
- Clarifier stuck: "Is there a word or phrase here that isn't fully clear yet? What clues around it might help?"
- Summarizer stuck: "If you could only keep one sentence from that paragraph, which would it be, and how would you shorten it?"

Reserve one role for the tutor (e.g., Clarifier on paragraph 4's phrase "even clean water can become contaminated again") only if the learner doesn't reach it naturally.

### Clarifying/inference, applied live

After paragraph 2, ask an inference question the text doesn't answer directly: "The text doesn't say exactly how many people worldwide lack clean water — but based on the last paragraph's mention of 'millions of people,' what can you infer about how big this problem still is globally?" Guide the learner to combine the text clue with reasoning, not guessing.

**Gate before advancing:** If the learner still needs heavy prompting on 2+ of the 4 roles, repeat this lesson's guided stage with a new short passage of similar structure before moving to L6.05's guided-to-independent handoff.

## 5. Write-to-read (5–8 min) — Unit A synthesis task

This closes Unit A (Body & Health), so today's writing task is a short **synthesis** comparing two texts from this unit, not just today's text alone.

**Task:** In 4–6 sentences, compare **L6.03 (Why We Get Sick)** and **L6.04 (Clean Water, Fewer Diseases)**. Both texts are about disease, but one focuses on how the body reacts to germs already inside it, and the other focuses on stopping germs from getting in in the first place. Explain this difference in your own words, and name one signal word or phrase from each text that helped you notice its structure.

**Model answer:** "L6.03 explains what happens after a germ has already entered the body — it uses cause/effect signal words like 'because' and 'as a result' to show how the immune system reacts and causes symptoms like fever. L6.04 explains how to stop germs from reaching the body in the first place, through clean water and sanitation — it uses problem/solution language like 'the problem' and 'one answer' to lay out the challenge and its fixes. Together, the two texts show two different lines of defense against disease: the body's internal response, and public health prevention outside the body. Both agree that germs are the underlying cause of illness, just at different points in the story."

## 6. Check (3–5 min)

1. **(Literal)** What two types of germs does the text say contaminated water can carry?
2. **(Literal)** Name the two main solutions the text describes for the clean water problem.
3. **(Inferential)** Why does the text pair water treatment with sanitation improvements, rather than presenting only one as "the" solution?
4. **(Inferential)** What does the phrase "even clean water can become contaminated again" imply about handwashing's role in this problem?
5. **(Evaluative)** The text says clean water access is "one of the oldest and most solvable problems in public health," yet still unsolved for millions. Do you find that framing convincing, or does it feel contradictory? Explain your view.

**Answer key:**
1. Bacteria (that cause cholera and typhoid) and parasites.
2. Water treatment (filtration, chlorination, boiling, purification tablets) and sanitation infrastructure (toilets, sewage systems, paired with hygiene education).
3. Because contamination can happen at more than one point — in the water source itself, or later through unsafe handling — so both solutions are needed to fully break the cycle.
4. It implies that treating water once isn't enough on its own; unwashed hands or unsafe storage can reintroduce germs afterward, so handwashing is a necessary complement to clean water, not a separate issue.
5. Either view is acceptable if justified — e.g., "convincing, because it distinguishes between the problem being technically solvable and it actually being solved everywhere due to resources/infrastructure gaps" or "it feels a little contradictory at first, since 'most solvable' sits oddly next to 'millions still lack access,' though the text does explain this is about implementation, not scientific difficulty."

**Pass rule:** 4/5 or more correct → move to L6.05 and Unit B. Below that → reread the water treatment and sanitation paragraphs, then retry questions 3 and 4.

**Support:** If the learner scores below 4/5, reread the water treatment and sanitation paragraphs together, having them point to the sentence that names each of the two solutions before retrying questions 3 and 4.
**Challenge:** If the learner scores 5/5 well under time, ask them to propose a third possible solution to the clean-water problem (not named in the text) and justify which of the two named solutions it's closer to.

---

**Tutor notes:**
- Common error: learner may treat "one answer" as the *only* solution and miss that the text presents a second, complementary one — point to "a second solution addresses" as the structural marker.
- On pronoun tracing: if the learner struggles with "because of it" referring to a whole idea rather than one noun, don't force a single-word answer — confirm that pronouns can point back to an entire earlier clause, and that's normal.
- On an unfamiliar word: sound it out / chunk it, don't guess from the first letter — "contaminate" chunks as con-tam-i-nate; connect to "contamination" if the learner already knows that word.
- In the GUIDED stage, resist jumping in at the first pause — count to five and let the learner attempt the role before coaching.

**Kids-track note:** If the learner is a younger strong reader (roughly 8–12), it can help to frame the water-treatment paragraph concretely: "imagine a filter, like the one in a fish tank, that catches the germs before the water reaches your cup" — the biology and public-health content stays identical.
