# L6.07 — Climate Change: One Cause, Many Effects

**Goal:** Read a cause/effect text about climate change, and learn a **hard-sentence
scaffold** — a set of four moves for tackling a single sentence that is genuinely harder than
your comfortable reading level.
**New today:** The hard-sentence scaffold (glossing / pre-teach / chunk / reread) — a one-time
technique applied to one dense sentence, occupying today's syntax-work slot.
**Review:** Text structure — **Cause/effect** (first taught in L6.03, "Why We Get Sick"). All 6
strategies folded in.
**Time:** 30–45 min.
**Materials:** This lesson only; pencil/paper.

## 2. Word work (5–7 min)

**trap** — *verb* (as used here, not the noun meaning a device to catch an animal) — to hold
something in place so it cannot escape or pass through.
- Example: "A thick blanket traps your body heat, which is why you feel warmer under it."
- Example: "Certain gases in the atmosphere trap heat from the sun, keeping the planet warm."
- **Your turn:** Name one everyday object (other than a blanket) that traps heat, and explain
  how.

*Urdu note:* no direct everyday Urdu loanword for "trap" in this sense — teach fresh.

**consensus** — *noun* — general agreement among a large group of people, especially experts,
after looking at the same evidence.
- Example: "There is scientific consensus that smoking causes lung cancer — almost no
  qualified researcher disputes it."
- Example: "The committee reached a consensus after hours of discussion."
- **Your turn:** Name one topic where you think there's strong scientific consensus, and one
  where you think experts still genuinely disagree. What's the difference between the two
  situations?

*Urdu note:* no direct everyday loanword — Urdu speech uses "اتفاق رائے" (ittifaq-e-raay,
"agreement of opinion"), not a borrowed form of "consensus" — teach fresh.

## 3. Fluency or close reading (5–8 min): A hard-sentence scaffold

Today's text is deliberately a little harder than a comfortable read — real climate-science
writing uses longer, more layered sentences than a simple news report. This is a one-time,
single-sentence scaffold, not the sustained, multi-week "stretch text" practice research/04 §10
describes (IES 2022 guide, Recommendation 4 — genuinely above-level texts worked 2–3 times a
week for 6–10 weeks, with pre-selected group-discussion stop points, moderate evidence). It
borrows that same general idea — meet a genuinely harder passage *with* scaffolding, never cold
— on a much smaller, one-lesson scale: four steps (glossing, pre-teaching, chunking, guided
rereading) applied to the single hardest sentence in today's text.

**The sentence:**

> "Because certain gases — mainly carbon dioxide, released in large quantities whenever fossil
> fuels like coal, oil, or gas are burned — trap extra heat in the atmosphere that would
> otherwise escape into space, the planet's average temperature has been rising, which in turn
> is shifting rainfall patterns, melting ice, and raising sea levels around the world."

**Step 1 — Gloss (define the hard words before touching the sentence).** Give quick, plain
definitions up front so the learner isn't decoding vocabulary and syntax at the same time:
*fossil fuels* = coal, oil, and natural gas, formed underground over millions of years from
buried plants and creatures, burned for energy; *atmosphere* = the layer of air surrounding
Earth; *average temperature* = a single number that represents the overall warmth of the whole
planet over time, not any one place's weather on any one day.

**Step 2 — Pre-teach the key idea before reading.** Say the core idea in one plain sentence
before the learner meets the real sentence: "Burning fuel releases a gas that acts like a
blanket around the Earth, and that blanket is making the whole planet warmer, which changes
weather everywhere." Only after this does the learner tackle the actual sentence.

**Step 3 — Chunk it.** Break the long sentence into meaning-sized pieces and read them one at a
time, pausing between chunks:
- "Because certain gases — mainly carbon dioxide, released in large quantities whenever fossil
  fuels like coal, oil, or gas are burned —"
- "trap extra heat in the atmosphere that would otherwise escape into space,"
- "the planet's average temperature has been rising,"
- "which in turn is shifting rainfall patterns, melting ice, and raising sea levels around the
  world."

**Step 4 — Reread for meaning, whole.** Now read the full sentence again, start to finish,
without stopping. Ask the learner to explain it back in their own words, chunk by chunk if
needed, then as one whole idea. This last full reread is what makes the scaffolding pay off —
skipping it and stopping at Step 3 leaves the pieces separate instead of reconnected.

## 3.5 Prime the topic (3–5 min)

Picture a greenhouse with glass walls, built to keep plants warm — sunlight comes in easily, but
heat has a harder time getting back out, so the air inside stays warmer than the air outside.
Earth's atmosphere works in a similar way: certain gases act like an invisible version of that
glass, letting sunlight in but slowing down how fast heat escapes back into space.

Three facts to hold onto before reading: (1) for more than 150 years, burning coal, oil, and gas
for energy has been adding extra amounts of these heat-trapping gases to the atmosphere; (2)
more trapped heat means Earth's average temperature — not just one place's weather on one day —
has been climbing; (3) that warming doesn't stay in one lane — it shifts rainfall patterns,
melts ice, and raises sea levels all at once.

**Orienting question:** Have you heard news reports about glaciers melting in the mountains of
northern Pakistan, or about unusually severe heatwaves in cities like Jacobabad or Karachi —
what have people around you said is causing that?

## 4. Knowledge text + discussion (15–20 min)

### Text structure review: Cause/effect

Remind the learner: "Watch for the signal words again: **because, as a result, therefore, due
to, so, consequently, leads to, causes.**" Light review, no reteach.

### The text

> Weather changes every day — a hot week, a rainy week, an unusually cold spring. Climate is
> different. It's the long-term pattern behind all that daily weather, measured over decades,
> not days. There is an overwhelming scientific **consensus** on what's happening to that
> long-term pattern. It's represented by organizations like the Intergovernmental Panel on
> Climate Change (IPCC), which reviews research from thousands of scientists worldwide. Earth's
> climate is changing, and human activity is the dominant driver of that change.

> **Stop and check (Literal):** According to the text, what organization represents the scientific consensus on climate change? *Answer: The Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change (IPCC).*

> Here is the basic mechanism. Sunlight passes through Earth's atmosphere and warms the
> surface. Normally, much of that heat radiates back out toward space. But certain gases in the atmosphere trap some of that outgoing heat instead. These are carbon dioxide, methane, and a few others, often called greenhouse gases, and they **trap** heat much like a blanket holds in body heat. This "greenhouse effect" isn't itself a problem — it's the reason Earth is warm enough
> to support life at all. The problem is the amount. People have been burning huge quantities of fossil fuels — coal, oil, and natural gas — since the 1800s. **Because** of this, the concentration of these gases in the atmosphere has risen sharply. That rise is **causing** more heat to be
> trapped than before.

> **Stop and check (Inferential):** Why does the text say the greenhouse effect itself "is not itself a problem"? *Answer: Because trapping some heat is natural and is the reason Earth is warm enough to support life — the problem is the extra AMOUNT of heat now being trapped because of human-caused emissions.*

> **As a result**, the planet's average surface temperature has been climbing. This single
> cause is producing effects across many different systems, not just one. Warmer average
> temperatures are shifting rainfall patterns. Some regions are seeing longer, more intense
> droughts. Others are seeing heavier, more damaging rainstorms and flooding. **Due to** rising
> temperatures, glaciers and polar ice are melting faster than they are being replenished. That
> melting contributes to rising sea levels, which threaten low-lying coastal cities and small
> island nations. Warmer oceans are also linked to more powerful storms in some regions, because
> storms draw energy from ocean heat. Ecosystems are affected too. Some plants and animals are
> shifting where they live, in search of the temperatures they're adapted to. Some — especially
> those that can't move or adapt quickly — are struggling to survive at all.

> **Stop and check (Literal):** Name two different effects of rising temperatures mentioned in this paragraph. *Answer: Any two of — shifting rainfall patterns (droughts/flooding), melting glaciers and ice (rising sea levels), more powerful storms, or ecosystems/species shifting or struggling.*

> None of these effects happens in isolation. **Consequently**, climate change is best understood not as one single event. It's one underlying cause — extra trapped heat — working its way through weather, water, ice, oceans, and ecosystems all at once. The way it shows up differs from region to region. That's also why the next lesson, on floods and droughts, isn't
> really a separate topic. It's a closer look at two of the effects this text has already
> introduced. Remember from L6.06 how the water cycle constantly moves water between ocean, air,
> and land? A warmer atmosphere changes how fast, and how unevenly, that movement happens —
> which is exactly why rainfall patterns are shifting.

*(≈472 words · ASL 13.1 words/sentence · FK grade 7.9 · max sentence 25 words)*

### Reciprocal teaching — INDEPENDENT

Learner runs all four roles alone. Given the harder text, allow one extra beat of thinking time
per paragraph before expecting a Questioner or Clarifier response — this is still independent,
just paced for the added difficulty.

## 5. Write-to-read (5–8 min)

**Task:** In 3–4 sentences, explain the cause-and-effect chain in this text: what is the one
underlying cause, and name at least two different effects it produces. Use at least one
cause/effect signal word.

**Model answer:** "The underlying cause is that burning fossil fuels has increased greenhouse
gases in the atmosphere, which traps extra heat. As a result, average temperatures are rising.
This single cause leads to several different effects: shifting rainfall patterns that cause
both worse droughts and worse floods, melting ice that raises sea levels, and ecosystems
struggling to adapt to new conditions."

---

## 6. Check (3–5 min)

1. **(Literal)** What are greenhouse gases, and name one example given in the text.
2. **(Literal)** According to the text, since when have humans been burning fossil fuels in
   large quantities?
3. **(Inferential)** The text says the greenhouse effect itself "is not a problem" — so what
   exactly is the problem, according to the text?
4. **(Inferential)** Why does the text say climate change should be understood as "one
   underlying cause" rather than several unrelated problems?
5. **(Evaluative)** The hard sentence in Section 3 was harder than most sentences in this
   course so far. Which of the four scaffold steps (gloss, pre-teach, chunk, reread) helped you
   most, and why?

**Answer key:**
1. Gases in the atmosphere that trap heat, keeping the planet warm — carbon dioxide (or
   methane) as the named example.
2. Since the 1800s.
3. The problem is the *amount* of greenhouse gas — human activity has increased the
   concentration of these gases, so more heat is being trapped than would be trapped naturally,
   which is what's driving the temperature rise.
4. Because all the effects described (droughts, floods, melting ice, sea-level rise, shifting
   ecosystems) trace back to the same root cause — extra trapped heat — rather than each having
   its own separate, unrelated origin.
5. No single correct answer — accept any reasoned response naming a specific step and why (e.g.,
   "chunking helped most because the sentence was too long to hold in my head all at once").

**Pass rule:** 4/5 or more, move to L6.08. Below that, reread paragraphs 2 and 3 (the mechanism
and its effects) together, walking through the hard-sentence scaffold again on one more sentence
from those paragraphs before retrying questions 3 and 4.

**Support:** If the learner scores below 4/5, don't just reread — run the four-step scaffold
(gloss, pre-teach, chunk, reread) out loud on one more sentence from paragraph 2 or 3 with the
tutor doing Step 1 (gloss) and Step 2 (pre-teach) for them this time, before they retry Steps 3
and 4 themselves and then attempt questions 3 and 4 again.

**Challenge:** If the learner scores 5/5 quickly, have them pick one effect from paragraph 3
(shifting rainfall, melting ice, storms, or ecosystem shifts) and explain, without looking back
at the text, how it traces back through the full chain to the one underlying cause — trapped
heat — in their own words, out loud, with no notes.

## Tutor notes

- Common error: learners think "greenhouse effect" and "climate change" mean the same problem.
  Correction: "The greenhouse effect is natural and necessary — re-read the sentence that says
  so. What word does the text use to describe what's actually gone wrong?"
- Common error: skipping straight to reading the hard sentence without the gloss/pre-teach
  steps, then giving up on it as "too hard." Correction: "Don't read it cold. Do steps 1 and 2
  first — what do you already know after just those two steps, before you've even read the full
  sentence?"
- Common error: treating "consensus" as meaning "some scientists agree." Correction: "Consensus
  means near-total agreement after looking at the same evidence — not a majority vote, and not
  one side of a live debate."
- Do not invent a specific temperature-rise number, a specific sea-level figure, or a specific
  year-by-year date for any of these effects — the text deliberately states the mechanism and
  the direction of change (warmer, higher, more frequent) as settled scientific consensus,
  without citing a precise statistic that would need separate verification.
- Fact-check flag: none of the claims in this text should need softening beyond what's already
  written — human-driven climate change and the basic greenhouse mechanism are standard,
  textbook-safe science, stated here as consensus without invented numbers, matching the
  spec's fact-accuracy guidance.
