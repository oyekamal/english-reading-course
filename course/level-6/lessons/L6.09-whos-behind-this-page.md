# L6.09 — Who's Behind This Page? Learning to Read Sideways

**Goal:** Learn **lateral reading** — the practice of leaving a page to check who's behind it,
before deciding whether to trust it — through a worked example and a guided practice scenario.
This is the course's mandatory standalone critical-reading module (research/06 §1.9).
**New today:** Lateral reading / source-evaluation protocol. This lesson's "extra" slot is the
protocol itself — there is no separate syntax focus today.
**Review:** Text structure — **Description**, applied here to evaluating a web page rather than
a science topic. All 6 strategies folded in.
**Time:** 30–45 min.
**Materials:** This lesson only; pencil/paper. (If the learner has real internet access, this
lesson works even better done live, alongside a real browser — see Tutor notes.)

---

## 1. Retrieval warm-up (2–3 min)

2. What does "consensus" mean, and why does the climate-change text describe human-driven

3. When you read something online that seems confident and well-written, does that alone tell

## 2. Word work (5–7 min)

**credible** — *adjective* — believable, worthy of trust, because of good evidence or a
trustworthy source.
- Example: "A claim from a well-known hospital's research team is generally more credible than
  an anonymous post with no sources."
- Example: "Just because a website looks professional doesn't automatically make it credible."
- **Your turn:** Name one source you'd consider highly credible for medical information, and
  explain what makes it credible — not just "it looks nice," but something about who's behind
  it or how they got their information.

*Urdu note:* no direct everyday loanword for "credible" itself — teach fresh. (The related word
"credit" is a genuine, very common loanword in spoken Urdu — "credit card," "credit pe lena" —
but it carries a financial meaning, not "believable," so don't lean on it as a cognate.)

**sponsored** — *adjective* — paid for by a company or organization, usually to promote a
product, service, or point of view, even when it's designed to look like ordinary content.
- Example: "The article looked like regular news, but a small label at the top said
  'Sponsored,' meaning a company had paid to have it written and placed there."
- Example: "Sponsored posts on social media are still ads, even when they don't look like
  ads."
- **Your turn:** Have you ever seen something online that turned out to be sponsored content?
  What was the giveaway — was it a label, or did you have to look closely to notice?

*Urdu note:* genuine everyday loanword — "sponsor" / "sponsored" is used as-is in spoken
Pakistani Urdu (TV programs, cricket matches, and events are routinely described as "sponsored
by" a company), so this word likely needs little fresh teaching beyond the specific
online-content meaning here.

## 3. Fluency or close reading (5–8 min): Phrase-cued fluency passage

No new syntax focus today — instead, use this slot for a phrase-cued fluency read (research/05
fluency strand). Read the passage aloud, pausing naturally at each marked break (/), then have
the learner read it back the same way, aiming for smooth, confident prosody rather than speed.

> Before you believe / anything you read online, / ask one simple question: / who wrote this, /
> and why? / A page can look calm, / confident, / and completely sure of itself / and still be
> wrong — / or worse, / deliberately misleading. / The words on the page / will not tell you /
> who's behind it. / You have to go looking / for that answer / yourself.

## 3.5 Prime the topic (3–5 min)

Picture someone scrolling through a WhatsApp forward or a website late at night: the message
sounds urgent and certain, the page looks clean and professional, and nothing on the screen
tells them who actually wrote it or why. That missing information — not the tone or the design
— is exactly what today's lesson is about.

Three facts to hold onto before reading: (1) a page can look calm, confident, and completely
sure of itself and still be wrong, or even deliberately misleading; (2) the words on a page will
never tell you, by themselves, who is behind it or what they might gain from you believing it;
(3) the fix is to leave the page and check independently — this is called "lateral reading."

**Orienting question:** Think of a message or forward you've received on WhatsApp that sounded
certain and urgent — did you check who actually wrote it or where it came from before believing
or sharing it?

## 4. Knowledge text + discussion (15–20 min)

### The text: what lateral reading is, and a worked example

> Imagine you're shown a short, grainy video online. It shows people at what looks like a
> polling station. The caption claims it proves an election was stolen. The video looks real —
> nothing about the footage itself seems faked. Most people, faced with this, judge the video by
> staying on the page. They watch it closely, look for signs of editing, and decide based on how
> convincing it feels. Researchers who study how people evaluate information online have a name for this: **vertical reading**. It means reading up and down the same page, digging deeper into the same source, without ever leaving it.

> The problem is that vertical reading can't catch the most important kind of lie. A video can
> be completely genuine, but still show something totally different from what the caption
> claims. In a real national study of thousands of students, over half judged a video like this
> "strong evidence." They had no way to know that the footage was filmed in an entirely different country, showing an entirely unrelated event. Nothing on the page told them.

> **Stop and check (Inferential):** Why couldn't "vertical reading" alone catch the problem with the polling-station video? *Answer: Because the video footage itself wasn't faked — the deception was in the caption and context, and only checking outside sources (lateral reading) could reveal that.*

> **Lateral reading** is the fix. Instead of staying on the page, you open a new tab. You search
> for information about the page or the claim, from other sources entirely. Fact-checkers and
> professional researchers do this automatically. It's exactly what trained students in the same
> study learned to do instead. They'd hit a suspicious claim, immediately open a new tab, and
> search the organization's name or a few words from the claim. Then they'd check what
> independent sources said about it — often within seconds, before reading any further on the
> original page.

> Three specific habits make up lateral reading:
>
> 1. **Search "who is [name]."** Before trusting a claim, search the name of the author,
>    organization, or website in a new tab. Look for what independent sources — not the site's
>    own "About" page — say about who runs it and what their track record is.
> 2. **Read "About" pages skeptically.** A site's own "About Us" page is written by the site
>    itself, to make itself look good — it's a starting point, not proof. The same 2019 national study found something else. Most students who did check an "About" page simply trusted whatever it said, without checking that description against outside sources.
> 3. **Watch for sponsored or labeled content.** Some content is clearly marked as paid, promotional, or sponsored. But the same study found that roughly two-thirds of students could not reliably tell the two apart. When a real news story and sponsored content were placed side by side, most students couldn't tell which was which.

> **Stop and check (Literal):** Name the three lateral-reading habits described in the text. *Answer: (1) Search "who is [name]," (2) read "About" pages skeptically, (3) watch for sponsored or labeled content.*

> One more finding from that study is worth sitting with directly. Researchers at the
> **Stanford History Education Group (2019)** showed students a website that looked like an
> independent source on a hot topic. In reality, it was funded behind the scenes by a company with a direct financial stake in that topic. That connection was never disclosed on the page itself.
> **96% of students did not factor that hidden financial tie into their judgment of whether to
> trust the site.** A confident tone and professional design tell you almost nothing on their
> own. Neither does a page simply *looking* independent. What tells you something is what you find when you leave the page and look sideways. That includes who is quietly paying for what you're reading. (Keep this in mind — you'll meet exactly this kind of hidden tie again in a
> few minutes, in the practice scenario below.)

> **Stop and check (Literal):** According to the Stanford History Education Group's 2019 study, what percentage of students failed to factor in a site's hidden financial tie? *Answer: 96%.*

*(≈595 words · ASL 13.2 words/sentence · FK grade 7.3 · max sentence 24 words)*

## 5. Write-to-read (10–12 min): Practice scenario with model answer

**The scenario:** You find a website called "PureFlow Water Relief." Its homepage has a
photo of children drinking from a well, a headline that reads *"Every $20 you give brings clean
water to a family in need,"* and a donate button. The "About" page says: *"We are a
passionate team dedicated to solving the global water crisis, working tirelessly on the
ground to bring life-saving water access to communities that need it most."* There is no list
of board members, no address, and no link to any financial reports. A small line at the very
bottom of the homepage, in gray text, reads: *"In partnership with BrightWell Bottled Water
Co."*

**Task:** Using the three lateral-reading habits from today's text, write 4–6 sentences
explaining what you would do *before* deciding whether to trust or donate to this site — not
what you think of the site emotionally, but the concrete steps you'd take.

**Model answer:** "First, I would open a new tab and search 'PureFlow Water Relief' plus a word
like 'reviews' or 'complaints,' to see what independent sources — not the charity's own site —
say about it. Second, I would notice that the 'About' page describes itself in very positive,
vague language ('passionate,' 'tirelessly') without naming any real people, an address, or a
financial report, which is a gap worth checking rather than trusting on its face. Third, I
would pay close attention to that small gray line about 'BrightWell Bottled Water Co.' — a
bottled water company partnering with a water charity could be a genuine cause, or it could be
a company using charity branding to sell its product, and I wouldn't know which without
searching for BrightWell separately too. Overall, I wouldn't decide anything — good or bad —
based only on what's written on PureFlow's own page; I'd look sideways first, at what
independent, non-affiliated sources say about both PureFlow and BrightWell before donating
anything."

---

## 6. Check (3–5 min)

1. **(Literal)** What is the difference between "vertical reading" and "lateral reading"?
2. **(Literal)** In the Stanford History Education Group's 2019 study described in the text,
   what percentage of students did not factor a site's hidden financial tie into their trust
   judgment?
3. **(Inferential)** Why is a website's own "About" page not, by itself, good evidence that the
   site is trustworthy?
4. **(Inferential)** In the PureFlow scenario, why is the small line about "BrightWell Bottled
   Water Co." worth investigating rather than ignoring?
5. **(Evaluative)** A friend says, "This site looks really professional and sincere, so I trust
   it." Using today's text, explain why that reasoning is risky, and what they should do
   instead.

**Answer key:**
1. Vertical reading stays on the same page, reading deeper into the same source; lateral
   reading means leaving the page and opening other sources in new tabs to check who's behind
   it and what independent sources say.
2. 96%.
3. Because it's written by the site itself to present itself favorably — it's a starting point
   for what the site *claims* about itself, not independent proof of who really runs it or how
   it actually operates; the text notes students who checked "About" pages tended to simply
   trust them without verifying elsewhere.
4. Because it reveals an undisclosed (or barely disclosed) commercial partnership that could
   mean the "charity" is connected to a company with a financial interest in its own image or
   sales — that relationship should be checked with independent sources before deciding whether
   donations genuinely go toward water relief.
5. Accept reasoned answers. Strong answer: a confident, professional, sincere-sounding tone is
   easy to produce regardless of whether a source is honest, and the text's own findings show
   that "looking credible" and "being credible" are not reliably connected — the friend should
   open a new tab and search for independent information about the site before trusting it,
   rather than judging by tone and design alone.

**Pass rule:** 4/5 or more, complete Unit B and move to Unit C (L6.10). Below that, reread the
three-habit list together and retry the PureFlow write-to-read task with the tutor coaching one
round before continuing.

**Support:** If the learner scores below 4/5, walk through the PureFlow scenario again line by
line, having the learner say out loud, specifically, what exact words they would type into a
search bar and what they would look for in the results — not a general feeling — before
retrying the write-to-read task with the tutor coaching one round.

**Challenge:** If the learner scores 5/5 quickly, have them find (or recall) a real website,
news story, or WhatsApp forward they've actually seen, and apply all three lateral-reading
habits to it live — reporting back what they found, or didn't find, about who's really behind
it.

## Tutor notes

- Common error: learners treat lateral reading as "just Google it," without the specific target
  of searching *who* is behind a claim rather than searching the claim's content itself.
  Correction: "What exact words would you type into that new tab? Say the search out loud."
- Common error: on the PureFlow scenario, learners jump straight to "this is definitely a scam"
  or "this is definitely fine" without describing the actual verification steps. Correction:
  "I'm not asking what you feel about it. I'm asking: what would you actually go and check,
  step by step?"
- Common error: assuming a lack of information (no board members listed, no financial reports)
  automatically proves wrongdoing. Correction: "Missing information is a reason to check
  further, not proof of guilt on its own — what would checking further actually involve?"
- If the learner has real internet access, do this lesson live: pick a real, unfamiliar
  cause-related website together and actually open new tabs to search "who is [organization],"
  check independent sources, and look for a sponsorship disclosure — this is far more powerful
  than the paper scenario alone, and is exactly how SHEG-trained interventions work in practice.
- This lesson bridges toward Unit C (Government & Money) — L6.10 will apply this same
  lateral-reading habit to a "get rich quick" financial claim, so keep the three-habit list
  visible or written down for quick reuse there.

## Kids-track note

For a younger strong reader (roughly 8–12), swap the PureFlow scenario framing slightly:
instead of "deciding whether to donate," frame it as "deciding whether to trust and share a
website with a parent or teacher." The three lateral-reading habits and the model answer's
reasoning stay exactly the same.
