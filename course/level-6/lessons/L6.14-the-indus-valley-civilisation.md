# L6.14 — The Indus Valley Civilisation: A City Before Its Time

**Goal:** Read and discuss an original text on the Indus Valley Civilisation, independently
running the full reciprocal-teaching routine, while reviewing description/sequence text
structure and choosing your own syntax focus.
**New today:** Unit D begins (History Thread) — no new strategy or structure; this lesson is a
full review/consolidation lesson.
**Review:** Description structure, sequence structure, all 6 comprehension strategies,
reciprocal teaching (independent stage), long-sentence chunking / pronoun tracing / connectives
/ passive voice (learner picks which to apply).

## 2. Word work (5–7 min)

**Word 1: civilisation** (also spelled *civilization*) — a large, organized society with
cities, government, writing or record-keeping, and shared culture, usually lasting many
generations.
- Example: "Ancient Egypt is often called one of the world's first civilisations."
- Example: "A civilisation needs more than a village — it needs cities, trade, and rules
  everyone follows."
- Generative task: Name one thing a group of people needs before historians would call it a
  "civilisation" rather than just "a village." Explain your choice.
- Urdu-cognate note: no direct loanword — teach fresh. Urdu's everyday word for this idea,
  "تہذیب" (*tehzeeb*), comes from a different Arabic root entirely (unrelated to the Latin
  *civis* root behind the English word), so there's no shared-sound shortcut here.

**Word 2: excavate** — to carefully dig up buried remains (buildings, tools, bones) so
archaeologists can study how people lived long ago.
- Example: "Archaeologists excavated the old city slowly, brushing away dirt layer by layer."
- Example: "It can take decades to fully excavate a large ancient site."
- Generative task: Why do you think archaeologists dig slowly and carefully instead of quickly?
  What might be lost if they rushed?
- Morphology note: **ex-** (out) + a Latin root related to digging out — the prefix **ex-**
  also appears in *exit*, *exhale*, *expand*. If a new word starts with ex-, try "out of/from"
  as a first guess at its meaning.
- Urdu-cognate note: no direct loanword — teach fresh. This is a Latin-rooted word with no
  parallel in Urdu's everyday vocabulary for digging/uncovering.

## 3.5 Prime the topic (3–5 min)

Picture an ancient city with straight streets crossing at neat right angles, covered drains
running under the roads, and a bathing area in almost every home — built roughly 5,000 years
ago, long before most of the "ancient" civilisations we usually hear about.

Key facts before we read: (1) this city-building culture grew up along the Indus River, in
what is today Pakistan and northwest India; (2) its two most famous cities are Harappa and
Mohenjo-daro — Mohenjo-daro is now a UNESCO World Heritage site in Sindh, Pakistan; (3) we can
see exactly how they built and organized their cities, but their writing has never been
deciphered, so we still don't know what they said in their own words.

Orienting question: Have you ever visited, or heard a grandparent or great-grandparent talk
about, an old fort, tomb, or ruin near where you live — and did anyone ever tell you roughly
how old it actually was?

## 4. Knowledge text + discussion (15–20 min)

**Text structure:** This text is mainly **Description** (REVIEW), with a **Sequence** thread
(REVIEW) running through the "what happened to it" section near the end. Signal words for
description are bolded; signal words for sequence are in *italics*.

### The Text: The Indus Valley Civilisation: A City Before Its Time

> Around 5,000 years ago — roughly the same era as ancient Egypt and ancient Mesopotamia — a
> civilisation grew up along the banks of the Indus River, in what is today Pakistan and
> northwest India. Historians call it the Indus Valley Civilisation, or sometimes the Harappan
> Civilisation, after one of its major cities, Harappa. Its other great city, Mohenjo-daro, is
> now a UNESCO World Heritage site in Sindh, Pakistan.

> **Stop and check (Literal):** Along which river did this civilisation grow up, and what are its
> two major cities called? *Answer: The Indus River; its two major cities are Harappa and
> Mohenjo-daro.*

> What made this civilisation remarkable was not just its size, but its planning. **Its
> cities**, unlike many ancient cities that grew messily over time, **were laid out on a
> grid**, with straight main streets crossing at right angles. **For example**, Mohenjo-daro
> had wide roads running north-south and east-west, dividing the city into neat blocks. **Such
> characteristics** were unusual for a city this old — many cities built thousands of years
> later show far less planning.

> **In fact**, the most famous feature of Indus Valley cities is their drainage system. **For
> instance**, most houses had their own bathing area connected to a covered drain running under
> the street, carrying wastewater away from homes. Few ancient civilisations — and not even
> every modern city today — managed sanitation this well. Archaeologists who excavate these
> sites often note that the drains were covered with bricks that could be lifted for cleaning,
> a detail that suggests real planning for long-term use, not just a quick fix.

> **Stop and check (Inferential):** Why might the detail that drain covers "could be lifted for
> cleaning" matter to archaeologists studying how well-planned this city was? *Answer: It
> suggests the drains were built for long-term maintenance and reuse, not just a one-time fix —
> a sign of real forward planning, not luck.*

> The people of the Indus Valley also had a system of weights and measures that was remarkably
> consistent across the region, suggesting organized trade. **Characteristics** of their trade
> network include small stone weights, found from one end of the civilisation to the other, that
> follow the same scale — a sign that merchants across a huge area agreed on how to measure
> goods. They also had a writing system, seen on small stone seals, but historians have not yet
> agreed on how to read it — the Indus script remains undeciphered to this day, which means we
> know a great deal about how these people built and organized their world, but very little
> about what they believed, wrote, or said in their own words.

> So what happened to this civilisation? Historians describe a gradual decline rather than a
> single dramatic event. ***First***, evidence suggests the Indus River system changed course
> over time, and some rivers the cities depended on dried up or shifted. ***Then***, trade
> networks that had connected the cities to distant regions seem to have weakened. ***After
> that***, the great cities were gradually abandoned, and the population is thought to have
> spread out into smaller farming settlements across the region — not vanished, but dispersed.
> ***Finally***, over centuries, the memory of these cities faded from view entirely, buried
> under soil, until archaeologists began excavating Harappa and Mohenjo-daro in the 1920s and
> uncovered a civilisation nobody in the modern world had known existed.

> Remember from L6.02 how blood carries oxygen through a network of vessels reaching every part
> of the body? The Indus Valley's covered drains did something similar for a whole city —
> carrying waste away from every home through a single connected network, keeping the city
> healthier as a result.

*(≈645 words · ASL 23.0 words/sentence · FK grade 11.4 · max sentence 61 words — L6.10-16 intentionally run higher, post-syntax-toolkit ramp per Round 2's ruling)*

**Fact check note:** Dates for the Indus Valley Civilisation are usually given as roughly
3300–1300 BCE, with its "mature phase" around 2600–1900 BCE — this text uses the softer
"around 5,000 years ago" deliberately rather than a precise date range, since exact boundaries
are debated among historians. The script being undeciphered is well established and safe to
state directly.

### Reciprocal teaching — INDEPENDENT stage

The learner runs all four roles alone (or with a peer/tutor as a second reader who only
listens and reads their own gist at the end for comparison). Use these role-prompt cards —
no scripted dialogue this lesson:

> **Predictor card:** Before reading the next paragraph, glance at its opening sentence or
> topic. What do you think it will tell you?
>
> **Questioner card:** After reading a paragraph, ask yourself one real question about it —
> not a question you already know the answer to. Write it down.
>
> **Clarifier card:** Find one word or sentence in the paragraph that felt confusing on first
> read. Reread it. What does it mean now?
>
> **Summarizer card:** In one sentence, what was that paragraph mainly about?

Run this over each of the six paragraphs above. Tutor's job this lesson: observe only — step
in only if the learner is stuck for more than 30 seconds on one role.

## 5. Write-to-read (5–8 min)

**Task:** In 3–5 sentences, explain what made Indus Valley cities unusual for their time, and
give at least one piece of evidence from the text.

**Model answer:** "Indus Valley cities were unusual because they were carefully planned instead
of growing messily, as most ancient cities did. Evidence for this includes their grid-pattern
streets, which crossed at right angles, and their covered drainage system, which carried
wastewater away from homes through the streets. They also used a consistent system of weights
across a wide area, which suggests organized trade. Even so, we still can't read their writing,
so a lot about their daily beliefs and language remains unknown."

---

## 6. Check (3–5 min)

1. **(Literal)** What are the names of the two major cities of the Indus Valley Civilisation
   mentioned in the text?
2. **(Literal)** What feature of Indus Valley houses connected to a covered street drain?
3. **(Inferential)** Why might the consistent weights found across the region suggest organized
   trade, rather than each town inventing its own system separately?
4. **(Inferential)** The text says the Indus script is "undeciphered." What does this mean we
   do and don't know about the Indus Valley people?
5. **(Evaluative)** The text calls the drainage system "remarkable... not even every modern
   city today" manages sanitation this well. Do you think this is a fair comparison to make
   across 5,000 years? Why or why not?

**Answer key:**
1. Harappa and Mohenjo-daro.
2. A bathing area, connected to a covered drain running under the street.
3. Because if towns across a huge area were all using weights on the same scale independently,
   that would be an unlikely coincidence — a shared, agreed system points to trade contact and
   communication between towns.
4. We know a lot about how they built and organized their cities (from physical remains), but
   we don't know what they believed, said, or wrote in their own words, because nobody can read
   their writing system yet.
5. Accept reasoned answers either way — e.g., "fair in the sense that basic sanitation is still
   a real problem in many places today, so noting it existed 5,000 years ago is genuinely
   notable," or "a little unfair, since it compares one planned ancient city to entire modern
   cities with far more people and different challenges."

**Pass rule:** 4/5 or more → move on to L6.15. Below that → reread the description paragraphs
(bolded signal words), retry questions 3 and 4, then continue.

**Support:** If the learner scores below 4/5, reread the drainage-system paragraph together
aloud, pointing at each bolded signal word ("In fact," "For instance") as you go, before
retrying questions 3 and 4.

**Challenge:** If the learner passes quickly and easily, ask them to name one modern city or
town they know of that still struggles with basic sanitation, and write 2–3 sentences comparing
it directly to Mohenjo-daro's drainage system 5,000 years ago.

## Tutor notes

- Learners sometimes read "undeciphered" and assume it means "unimportant" or "fake" — clarify
  it means "not yet solved," not "not real." Corrective language: "Undeciphered means nobody has
  found the key to reading it yet — it doesn't mean it isn't real writing."
- If the learner struggles to choose a syntax focus for this lesson (it's their choice), suggest
  they pick the drainage-system sentence ("Few ancient civilisations... managed sanitation this
  well") for pronoun/connective work, since it has a clear contrast structure.
- Some learners may confuse "Indus Valley Civilisation" with "India" as a modern country — clarify
  gently: the Indus River runs mostly through modern-day Pakistan, and the civilisation predates
  both modern India and modern Pakistan by thousands of years; it belongs to the shared ancient
  history of the whole region.
- Watch for the word "civilisation" itself tripping up decoding at speed (multisyllabic, soft
  c) — if needed, have the learner chunk it: ci-vil-i-sa-tion.

**Kids-track note:** For a younger strong reader (8–12), lean the discussion toward "detective"
language — archaeologists as detectives figuring out an old mystery from clues in the ground —
rather than dwelling on the abstract "what is a civilisation" definition.
