# L6.05 — Salt Water and Fresh Water: Earth's Water Budget

**Goal:** Read a compare/contrast text about how Earth's water is divided between salt and fresh
water, and take full ownership of the reciprocal-teaching routine for the first time.
**New today:** Text structure — **Compare/contrast** (last of the 5 structures). Strategy —
**Predicting** (last of the 6 strategies). Syntax focus — connectives (although / however /
therefore).
**Review:** Description, sequence, cause/effect, problem/solution (structures); question
generation, summarizing, monitoring/fix-up, clarifying/inference (strategies); pronoun reference
tracing (syntax).
**Time:** 30–45 min.
**Materials:** This lesson only; pencil/paper; optional audio recorder for the fluency step.

---

## 1. Retrieval warm-up (2–3 min)

Ask the learner, out loud, no notes:

1. In L6.04, what was the *problem* in "Clean Water, Fewer Diseases," and what was the
   *solution*?
2. What signal words tell you a text is using **problem/solution** structure? (Give at least
   two: *problem, solution, solve, address, in response...*)
3. Quick recall: what do the four reciprocal-teaching roles do — Predictor, Questioner,
   Clarifier, Summarizer? (One phrase each is enough.)

---

## 2. Word work (5–7 min)

Two words from today's text.

**vast** — *adjective* — extremely large in area or amount.
- Example: "The Sahara Desert is a vast stretch of sand that takes days to cross."
- Example: "Earth's oceans are vast — they cover most of the planet's surface."
- **Your turn:** Name one thing you would call vast, and one thing you would NOT call vast
  even though it's big. Explain the difference in one sentence.

*Urdu note:* no direct everyday loanword — Urdu has its own native word, "وسیع" (wasee) —
teach fresh.

**accessible** — *adjective* — able to be reached, used, or gotten to without much difficulty.
- Example: "The library is accessible by bus, so you don't need a car to get there."
- Example: "Clean drinking water is not accessible to everyone in the world."
- **Your turn:** Which is more accessible where you live — fresh water from a tap, or fresh
  water from a river? Why?

*Morphology note (optional):* **accessible** breaks down as **access** (the base — a way in or
a way to reach something) + **-ible** ("able to be"). If the word "inaccessible" ever comes up,
the prefix **in-** (research/05 §5f, one of the top-4 highest-yield prefixes) flips it to "NOT
able to be reached."

*Urdu note:* no direct everyday loanword for "accessible" itself — teach fresh, though the tutor
can point out that urban/educated Urdu speech does sometimes borrow the related noun "access"
as-is in tech or bureaucratic contexts (e.g. "access nahi mil raha" — "I can't get access"), so
the word family isn't entirely foreign-sounding.

## 3. Fluency or close reading (5–8 min)

**Close reading — connectives.** Pull this sentence from today's text:

> "Most of the planet is covered in salt water, but only a small share is fresh; **however**,
> even most of that fresh share is not something a person can easily drink, because it is
> locked away as ice."

Work through it with the learner, out loud:

1. Find the connective: **however**. What relationship does it signal? (A contrast — it's
   flagging that what comes next pushes back against, or complicates, what came before.)
2. Before "however": "only a small share is fresh" — sounds like good news for fresh water.
3. After "however": that small share isn't even mostly usable. The connective is doing the
   work of saying "wait — don't celebrate yet."
4. Ask: "If I deleted the word 'however' and just put a period there, would the sentence still
   make sense?" (Grammatically yes, but the reader would lose the signal that a complication is
   coming — they'd have to figure that out from content alone, which is slower and less
   reliable.)
5. Have the learner find one more connective in today's text (any of *although, however,
   therefore, unlike, whereas, in contrast, on the other hand*) and explain in their own words
   what relationship it signals — contrast, or cause/result.

## 3.5 Prime the topic (3–5 min)

Picture a photo of Earth taken from space: it looks almost entirely blue, because oceans cover
most of the surface. But nearly all of that blue is salt water — water people, farm animals, and
most machines can't drink or use. Only a small share of Earth's water is fresh, and even most of
that fresh share is frozen solid or buried underground, not sitting in an easy-to-reach river or
lake.

Three facts to hold onto before reading: (1) about 97 of every 100 drops of water on Earth is
salt water; (2) most of what's left over — the fresh water — is locked away in ice, not flowing
in a river; (3) the fresh water people actually use every day is a very small slice of the
planet's total water.

**Orienting question:** Where does the fresh water in your own home or neighbourhood actually
come from — a hand-pump or well, a canal or river, or a water tanker — and have you ever seen
that source run low or dry up during a hot season?

## 4. Knowledge text + discussion (15–20 min)

### Text structure lesson: Compare/contrast (TEACH — last of the 5 structures)

Tell the learner directly: "Today's text uses **compare/contrast** structure. This means it
puts two things side by side and shows how they are alike and how they are different. Writers
signal this with words like: **however, on the other hand, similarly, unlike, in contrast,
both, whereas.**" Point out that this is the fifth and final structure in the course — they now
know all five: description, sequence, cause/effect, problem/solution, and compare/contrast.

### Strategy lesson: Predicting (TEACH — last of the 6 strategies)

Tell the learner: "The last of our six strategies is **predicting** — using the title and
headings to guess what a text will cover *before* you read it, then checking your guess as you
go." Model it once, out loud, on the title below: "The title is 'Salt Water and Fresh Water:
Earth's Water Budget.' The word 'budget' usually means a limited amount of something that has to
be divided up. I predict this text will tell me how much of Earth's water is salty, how much is
fresh, and that the fresh amount is probably the smaller, more limited share — because that's
usually why people call something a 'budget.'" Have the learner make their own one-sentence
prediction before reading, then check it against the text afterward.

### The text

> Look at a photo of Earth from space. It is easy to see why people call it "the blue
> planet." Oceans stretch across most of the globe. It can seem like water is everywhere, in
> **vast**, endless supply. But that first impression is misleading. Almost all of that
> water — roughly 97 out of every 100 drops — is salt water, locked in the oceans. People,
> farm animals, and most crops cannot drink salt water. Most machines cannot use it either.

> **Stop and check (Literal):** About how many drops out of every 100 on Earth are salt
> water? *Answer: about 97 drops out of every 100.*

> That leaves only about 3% of Earth's water as fresh water — the kind living things
> actually need. **However**, even that 3% is not evenly spread out or evenly usable. The
> ocean is one connected body sitting on the surface, where anyone can reach it. **Unlike**
> the ocean, most fresh water is hidden away or hard to reach. The largest single share of
> the world's fresh water is frozen solid. It is locked into glaciers and the ice caps at the
> North and South Poles. It is fresh, but it is not **accessible** — nobody is going to melt
> a glacier to fill a cup.
>
> A smaller share of fresh water sits underground, in layers of rock and soil that hold
> water the way a sponge holds liquid. This is called groundwater, and people reach it by
> digging wells. **In contrast** to the frozen ice caps, groundwater is at least reachable
> with the right tools. Still, it can take deep drilling and real effort to get to it. Only a
> very small sliver of all the fresh water on Earth exists as water people can walk up to
> directly: rivers, lakes, and streams. That sliver is well under 1% of the total water budget.
> Still, this tiny sliver is the water most towns, farms, and cities depend on for daily life.

> **Stop and check (Inferential):** Why is groundwater, reached by drilling a well, more
> useful day to day than a glacier, even though both are technically "fresh water"? *Answer:
> a well can be dug and used with real tools and effort. But nobody can practically melt a
> glacier to get a drink. So groundwater is genuinely reachable, while glacier ice is not.*

> So there is a real gap between how much water Earth has and how much is genuinely usable
> day to day. Salt water and fresh water are **similarly** made of the same basic molecule,
> H₂O. But they behave completely differently once people try to use them. Salt water
> corrodes pipes and machinery over time. It will slowly kill most plants and freshwater
> animals if it mixes in. **Whereas** salt water is common and hard to use, fresh water is
> rare and easy to use. That is exactly why fresh water has to be protected and shared
> carefully, the way any limited resource does.

> **Stop and check (Literal):** According to the text, what basic molecule are salt water and
> fresh water both made of? *Answer: H₂O.*

> This is also why the water cycle matters so much — the next lesson covers it in detail.
> The water cycle keeps moving a share of the ocean's salt water up into the air as fresh
> water again. This refills rivers and lakes that would otherwise slowly run dry. Remember
> from L6.04 how a shortage of *clean* water spreads disease. This lesson is about a related
> but different shortage: not water that's dirty, but water that's simply salty, frozen, or
> out of reach.

*(≈562 words · ASL 13.1 words/sentence · FK grade 6.2 · max sentence 25 words)*

### Reciprocal teaching — GUIDED → INDEPENDENT transition

This is the handoff lesson. The learner runs **all four roles alone**, paragraph by paragraph.
The tutor's job today is to **observe only** — no coaching mid-paragraph, no prompting. Use
these role-prompt cards; hand them to the learner (or read them aloud once, then step back):

> **PREDICTOR CARD** — Before you read a paragraph, glance at its first sentence. Say out loud
> what you think it will tell you, in one sentence.
>
> **QUESTIONER CARD** — After you read a paragraph, ask one real question about it — not a
> question you already know the answer to. A good question usually starts with *why, how,* or
> *what would happen if.*
>
> **CLARIFIER CARD** — Find one word or one sentence in the paragraph that felt confusing or
> unclear. Say what confused you, then work out what it means using the words around it.
>
> **SUMMARIZER CARD** — Say the paragraph's main point in one sentence, in your own words.

After each paragraph, the tutor only marks (on paper, silently) whether the learner attempted
all four roles — not whether every answer was perfect. Discuss the misses together only *after*
the full text is finished, not mid-reading. This matters: independence means the learner
carries the routine through friction and confusion without being rescued paragraph by
paragraph.

**Gate before advancing:** If the learner cannot run all 4 roles with only light tutor coaching
(not heavy prompting) by the end of this lesson's passage, repeat this lesson's guided-to-
independent handoff with a new passage before treating L6.06 onward as fully independent.

## 5. Write-to-read (5–8 min)

**Task:** In 3–4 sentences, compare salt water and fresh water using at least one signal word
from today's list (*however, unlike, in contrast, similarly, whereas, both*).

**Model answer:** "Both salt water and fresh water are made of the same basic molecule.
However, salt water makes up about 97% of Earth's water, while fresh water is only about 3%.
Unlike the ocean, which sits on the surface where it's easy to reach, most fresh water is
locked in ice or hidden underground. This is why fresh water has to be protected carefully,
even though there is far more water on Earth overall."

---

## 6. Check (3–5 min)

1. **(Literal)** About what percentage of Earth's water is salt water?
2. **(Literal)** Where is the largest single share of Earth's fresh water located?
3. **(Inferential)** Why does the text say salt water is not "accessible" in the same way
   groundwater is, even though both are technically reachable with effort?
4. **(Inferential)** Based on the text, why might a town build wells rather than simply wait
   for more rain to fill its rivers?
5. **(Evaluative)** Is it fair to call fresh water "rare," given that nearly three-quarters of
   Earth's surface is covered in water? Explain your reasoning using something from the text.

**Answer key:**
1. About 97% (roughly 97 out of every 100 drops).
2. Frozen in glaciers and the polar ice caps.
3. Salt water sits right on the surface and is technically "reachable" by walking to the
   ocean, but it can't actually be used (drunk, farmed with, run through most machines)
   without expensive treatment — so "accessible" in this text means usable, not just
   physically nearby. Groundwater, once reached by a well, is directly usable.
4. Rivers depend on rainfall and the water cycle, which is unreliable and seasonal; wells tap
   into a more stable underground store that doesn't dry up as fast, so they're a more
   dependable backup source.
5. Accept reasoned answers either way. Strong answer: yes, it's fair — "fresh water" doesn't
   mean "surface water"; only a tiny accessible sliver (rivers/lakes, under 1% of the total
   water budget) is what people actually depend on, so even though Earth looks like a water
   world, usable fresh water really is scarce relative to demand.

**Pass rule:** 4/5 or more, move to L6.06. Below that, reread paragraph 2 (the fresh vs. salt
water split) and paragraph 3 (accessibility) together, then retry questions 3 and 5 before
continuing.

**Support:** If the learner scores below 4/5, draw a simple two-column table with the tutor —
"Where is it?" and "Can you actually use it right now?" — and fill it in together for salt
water, ice caps, groundwater, and rivers/lakes before retrying questions 3 and 4. Seeing all
four side by side usually fixes the "reachable ≠ usable" confusion faster than rereading alone.

**Challenge:** If the learner scores 5/5 quickly, ask them to name one method people use to turn
salt water into fresh water (for example, desalination) and explain, using ideas from the text,
what problem it would solve and what new cost or difficulty it would likely introduce — even
without the text mentioning desalination directly.

## Tutor notes

- Common error: learners confuse "compare/contrast" with just listing two facts side by side
  without naming a relationship. Correction: "You told me two facts. Now tell me the
  *relationship* — are they alike, or different, and how do you know? Point to the connective
  word that told you."
- Common error: treating "however" as just meaning "but," without noticing it's flagging a shift
  in what the reader should expect next. Correction: "Read the sentence before 'however' and the
  sentence after it separately. Does the second one confirm the first, or push back on it?"
- On the reciprocal-teaching handoff: resist the urge to jump in when the learner struggles with
  Clarifier or stalls on Questioner. Let there be a pause. If they're genuinely stuck after 15–20
  seconds, the only allowed prompt is: "Try the card again — what does it ask you to do?" Never
  supply the answer.
- Predicting is a light strategy — don't let this turn into a long guessing game. One sentence,
  a few seconds, then move on and read.

## Kids-track note

For a younger strong reader (roughly 8–12), swap "water budget" for a simpler framing before
reading: "A budget is like having a fixed amount of pocket money to spend — you can't spend
more than you have. Earth has a water budget the same way: only so much is fresh, so it has to
be shared out carefully." The text itself needs no rewriting.
