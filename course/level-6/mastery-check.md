# Level 6 Mastery Check — "Reading to Learn"

This is the level-exit check for Level 6. It tests the six things this level built: text-
structure identification, independent reciprocal-teaching application, syntax-as-comprehension
tools, lateral reading, cross-unit knowledge, and synthesis writing. Run it in one sitting if
possible (45–60 min), or split into two sessions at the natural section breaks.

---

## Part 1 — Text-structure identification (5 short passages, one per structure)

For each passage: (a) name the text structure, (b) underline or list its signal words.

**Passage 1**
> The common cold and the flu are both caused by viruses, but they have different
> characteristics. A cold usually comes on gradually, with a runny nose and mild tiredness. The
> flu, on the other hand, often comes on suddenly, with a high fever and body aches. Both are
> contagious, but the flu is generally more severe.

**Passage 2**
> To safely cross a busy road, first stop at the curb and look both ways. Next, listen for
> traffic you might not see. Then, if it's clear, begin crossing at a steady pace — never run.
> Finally, keep checking both directions until you've reached the other side.

**Passage 3**
> Heavy rain fell for three days straight upriver. As a result, the river rose above its banks
> by the fourth day. Because the town's drainage system couldn't handle that much water, streets
> flooded within hours, and several homes had to be evacuated.

**Passage 4**
> A checking account and a savings account both hold your money safely at a bank, but they work
> differently. A checking account is for everyday spending — you can withdraw from it anytime,
> often with no limit. A savings account, in contrast, usually earns a small amount of interest,
> but the bank may limit how often you can withdraw.

**Passage 5**
> Many small farmers can't afford the cost of modern irrigation equipment, so their crops often
> fail during dry spells. One response some governments have tried is a shared-equipment
> program, where several farmers pool resources to buy and share one irrigation system. This
> doesn't solve the problem everywhere, but it addresses the cost barrier for the farmers who
> can access it.

### Answer key — Part 1

1. **Compare/contrast** — signal words: *characteristics, on the other hand, both... but*.
2. **Sequence** — signal words: *first, next, then, finally*.
3. **Cause/effect** — signal words: *as a result, because*.
4. **Compare/contrast** — signal words: *both... but, in contrast*.
5. **Problem/solution** — signal words: *can't afford... so (problem), one response, addresses*.

## Part 2 — Reciprocal teaching, independent application

Read this short passage once:

> Bees are not the only pollinators — moths, bats, and even some birds carry pollen between
> flowers too. Bats, for instance, pollinate certain cactus and agave plants at night, when bees
> are inactive. Without a range of different pollinators working at different times of day,
> many plants that depend on them would struggle to reproduce.

**Task:** Write one of each — a Predictor question, a Questioner question, a Clarifier note, and
a Summarizer sentence — as if you were reading this for the first time.

**Model answer:**
- **Predictor:** "Given the title mentions pollinators beyond bees, I'd guess the next part will
  describe another specific pollinator and what it does."
- **Questioner:** "Why do bats pollinate specifically at night — is it because that's when
  they're awake, or because the flowers they visit only open at night?"
- **Clarifier:** "'Pollinators' was the word I paused on — it means any creature that carries
  pollen from one flower to another, helping plants reproduce; bees are just the most famous
  example."
- **Summarizer:** "Several different animals, not just bees, pollinate plants, often at
  different times of day, and losing that variety would hurt plant reproduction."

*(Grade by fit, not exact wording — a Questioner question that's already answered in the text,
or a Summarizer sentence that just repeats one detail instead of the gist, should be reworked.)*

## Part 3 — Syntax

**3a. Chunking.** Mark chunk breaks with `/` in this sentence, then read it aloud at a natural
pace:

> "Because many coastal cities depend on shipping for trade, and because rising sea levels
> threaten the ports those cities were built around generations ago, several governments have
> begun planning, at considerable expense, to raise or relocate critical harbour infrastructure
> before it floods permanently."

**Model chunking:** "Because many coastal cities depend on shipping for trade, / and because
rising sea levels threaten the ports / those cities were built around generations ago, / several
governments have begun planning, / at considerable expense, / to raise or relocate critical
harbour infrastructure / before it floods permanently."

**3b. Pronoun reference tracing.** Read this passage and trace each bolded pronoun back to what
it refers to:

> A city's water engineers monitor river levels every day. **They** compare current readings
> against historical averages to spot early warning signs. If a flood risk is found, **it** is
> reported immediately to emergency planners, who then decide whether **it** justifies an
> evacuation warning.

- **They** → the city's water engineers.
- **it** (first) → a flood risk (the thing found).
- **it** (second) → the same flood risk — NOT "an evacuation warning," which comes after it in
  the sentence; this is a common tracing error (matching to the nearest noun instead of the
  logical one).

**3c. Connectives.** Explain what job each connective is doing in this sentence: *"The bridge was
closed for repairs; **however**, traffic was rerouted smoothly, **so** commute times barely
changed."*

- **however** → signals a contrast between what you'd expect (a closure causing problems) and
  what actually happened (smooth rerouting).
- **so** → signals a result — because the rerouting worked well, the effect was that commute
  times stayed about the same.

**3d. Passive voice.** Rewrite this sentence in the active voice: *"The new library was funded
by a combination of city taxes and private donations."*

**Model answer:** "A combination of city taxes and private donations funded the new library."

## Part 4 — Lateral reading mini-task

**Scenario:** You find a website called "TrueHealthFactsNow.org" claiming that a common
vitamin, taken in very high doses, "cures" a serious illness that doctors supposedly don't want
you to know about. The site has no author name listed, just a logo, and its "About" page says
only "We are dedicated to bringing you the truth other sites won't tell you."

**Task:** What would you check before trusting or sharing this claim? List at least 3 checks.

**Model answer:**
1. Open a new tab and search the site's name and domain — who actually owns/runs it, and do
   independent sources describe it as reliable or as a known misinformation source?
2. Search the specific claim itself (the vitamin + illness) alongside terms like "evidence" or
   "study" — do independent medical organizations or peer-reviewed sources say the same thing,
   or contradict it?
3. Notice the persuasion pattern — "doctors don't want you to know" and "the truth other sites
   won't tell you" are classic distrust-everyone-else framing, a red flag independent of the
   specific topic.
4. Check whether the "About" page names real people, credentials, or institutions you can
   verify — a vague mission statement with no accountable author is a warning sign, not proof
   of anything either way.

## Part 5 — Knowledge check across the 4 units

1. **(Unit A)** Name one thing the human immune system does when the body is fighting off a
   disease.
2. **(Unit A)** Why does handwashing reduce the spread of many diseases?
3. **(Unit B)** What are the three main stages of the water cycle mentioned across Unit B's
   lessons (evaporation, condensation, and what third stage)?
4. **(Unit B)** What is one human activity that scientific consensus says is a major driver of
   climate change?
5. **(Unit C)** Why do governments collect taxes, in general terms?
6. **(Unit C)** What happens to a family's purchasing power when inflation rises?
7. **(Unit D)** What made Indus Valley cities unusual for their time?
8. **(Unit D)** What event in 1947 created India and Pakistan as separate independent countries?

### Answer key — Part 5

1. Accept reasonable answers — e.g., it identifies and attacks the specific germ causing the
   illness, produces antibodies, or (for accept-either) develops memory of that germ to fight it
   faster if encountered again.
2. Because handwashing physically removes many disease-causing germs from the hands before they
   can be transferred to the mouth, eyes, food, or other people.
3. Precipitation (rain, snow, etc. falling back to Earth) — accept "collection" if the learner's
   lessons used that fourth-stage term instead.
4. Human burning of fossil fuels (releasing greenhouse gases that trap heat) — accept any answer
   naming greenhouse gas emissions from human activity.
5. To pay for public goods and services everyone benefits from — roads, schools, hospitals,
   defense, etc.
6. Purchasing power falls — the same amount of money buys less than it used to, because general
   prices have risen.
7. Their careful city planning — grid-pattern streets and an advanced covered drainage/sanitation
   system, plus consistent weights suggesting organized trade.
8. Partition — the end of British colonial rule over the Indian subcontinent, dividing it into
   two independent countries.

## Part 6 — Short synthesis writing task

**Task:** In one paragraph (5–8 sentences), pick two texts from ANY two different units covered
in Level 6 and explain one genuine connection between them — a shared structure, a shared kind
of cause-and-effect, or a shared human problem. (This mirrors the Unit B, Unit C, and L6.16
capstone synthesis tasks — if the learner already wrote a strong synthesis paragraph in L6.16,
they may reuse and adapt it here rather than starting from scratch.)

**Grading guidance:** A passing paragraph names both specific texts/units, states a real
(non-generic) connection, and supports it with at least one concrete detail from each text —
not just "they're both interesting" or "they're both about problems."

---

## Pass rule for the whole Level 6 Mastery Check

- **Part 1:** 4/5 or more correct (structure + at least one correct signal word each).
- **Part 2:** all 4 roles attempted and each one plausibly fits its role (grade by fit, not
  exact wording).
- **Part 3:** 3/4 or more correct.
- **Part 4:** at least 3 genuine, non-generic checks listed.
- **Part 5:** 6/8 or more correct.
- **Part 6:** paragraph meets the grading guidance above.

**Overall: pass all 6 parts at their individual thresholds → ready for Level 7.**

If one or two parts fall short, don't restart the whole level — go back to the specific
lesson(s) tied to the weak part before retrying just that part:

| Weak part | Reread this lesson first |
|---|---|
| Part 1 (text structure) | Whichever structure was missed: L6.01 (description) · L6.02/L6.06 (sequence) · L6.03/L6.07/L6.13 (cause/effect) · L6.05 (compare/contrast) · L6.04/L6.08/L6.12 (problem/solution) |
| Part 2 (reciprocal teaching) | L6.06 (first fully independent-stage lesson) |
| Part 3 (syntax) | L6.03 (chunking) · L6.04 (pronouns) · L6.05 (connectives) · L6.06 (passive) · or L6.15 (comprehensive review, all four) |
| Part 4 (lateral reading) | L6.09 (the lateral-reading module) and L6.10 (applied practice) |
| Part 5 (knowledge) | Whichever unit's questions were missed — reread that unit's texts |
| Part 6 (synthesis) | L6.16 (the capstone synthesis task) |

If three or more parts fall below threshold, treat this as a signal to slow down rather than
patch individual gaps: work back through the second half of Level 6 (L6.09 onward) more
carefully before retaking the full Mastery Check.
