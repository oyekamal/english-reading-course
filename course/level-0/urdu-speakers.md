# A Guide for Urdu, Punjabi, Pashto, and Other South-Asian-Language Speakers

## Read this first: you are not starting from zero

If you can already read Urdu (or Punjabi, Pashto, Sindhi), you already have real skills that
transfer to English. Research on Urdu-English bilinguals found that phonological awareness —
the ability to break words into sounds — transfers between the two languages; it does not have
to be rebuilt from scratch (`research/04` §1, "Urdu-L1 → English-L2 script/orthography
research," and the Cummins "linguistic interdependence" finding). The same research also found
no difference in reading-intervention outcomes between English learners and native English
speakers, when both get the same explicit, systematic instruction (`research/03` §1 item 9,
Toronto/Brock finding) — so you'll follow the exact same Level 1–7 sequence as a native
speaker. This guide tells you where to expect extra difficulty and why, and gives you specific
practice, not a different course.

## The four sounds that will need real, deliberate practice

These are documented, specific gaps — not a vague "accent" — so the practice below targets the
exact mismatch, not general pronunciation drilling (`research/04` §6).

### 1. "th" — /θ/ and /ð/ (as in "think" and "this")

Urdu has no sound made by putting your tongue between your teeth. Urdu speakers typically
substitute the closest sound they do have: an aspirated "t" (تھ) for "think," and a plain "d"
for "this" (`research/04` §1, source: acoustic analysis of Pakistani English).

**What to do:** put the tip of your tongue lightly between your front teeth and blow air out
gently — you should feel air, not a stopped sound. Practice these minimal pairs (words that
differ by only this one sound) aloud, listening for the difference:

| /θ/ or /ð/ word | Minimal-pair partner |
|---|---|
| think | tink |
| thin | tin |
| thank | tank |
| this | dis |
| that | dat |
| bath | bat |
| path | pat |
| mouth | mout |

### 2. /v/ vs /w/ (as in "vet" and "wet")

Urdu's و (waw) covers the territory of both English /v/ and /w/, so these two English sounds
collapse into one for many Urdu-L1 speakers — again, a precise, documented gap, not a general
accent issue (`research/04` §1, source: pronunciation comparative study).

**What to do:** for /v/, your top teeth touch your bottom lip and you feel a buzz/vibration.
For /w/, your lips round into an "oo" shape and no teeth touch. Practice these pairs aloud:

| /v/ word | /w/ word |
|---|---|
| vet | wet |
| vine | wine |
| van | wan |
| vest | west |
| vine | wine |
| verse | worse |
| vary | wary |
| veil | wail |

### 3. Short vowels (the a/e/i/o/u distinctions)

English has more short-vowel contrasts than most Pakistani-language vowel systems, so words
that differ only by a short vowel sound (bit/bet/bat) are genuinely harder to hear apart at
first, not just harder to spell (`research/04` §1, Pashto-speaker vowel study, generalized
directionally to Urdu/Punjabi/Sindhi speakers).

**What to do:** practice these short-vowel minimal-pair sets aloud, slowly at first:

| Set |  |  |
|---|---|---|
| bit / bet / bat | pin / pen / pan | sit / set / sat |
| full / fool | (short /ʊ/ vs long /uː/) | pull / pool |
| cup / cap | bed / bad | hit / hut |

### 4. Consonant clusters — no added vowel sound

Urdu (like several South Asian languages) tends not to allow certain consonant clusters at the
start of a word without an inserted vowel sound, so "school" can become "iskool" and "sport"
can become "isport." English requires the cluster spoken with nothing inserted
(`research/04` §1, general cross-linguistic finding, cited in the course design implication).

**What to do:** practice starting these words with the consonant sound immediately, no
lead-in vowel: school, spot, stop, sport, small, snow, sleep, star, sky, speak. Say each one
slowly, then at normal speed, checking there is no "ih" or "uh" sound before the first
consonant.

## What Urdu-script habits mean for reading English print

- **Direction:** Urdu (Nastaliq) is written right-to-left; English is left-to-right. If you're
  a beginning reader, this is a real, if temporary, adjustment — practice tracking left to
  right with your finger under the line for the first few lessons if it helps.
- **Vowels are always written in English, often not in everyday Urdu.** Everyday printed Urdu
  usually omits short-vowel diacritics — fluent Urdu readers are used to inferring vowels from
  context and vocabulary rather than decoding every one. English works the opposite way:
  every vowel sound is represented by a letter (or letters) you must decode, every time. This
  is a genuine mindset shift, not just a new alphabet (`research/04` §6). Expect to
  deliberately sound out every vowel in a word at first, even words you can already say.
- **Letter shape confusions:** b/d/p/q. These four letters share the same basic shape rotated
  or mirrored, which trips up many beginning readers regardless of native language — but it is
  worth naming explicitly (this is covered directly in Level 1's review lesson, 1.13).
- **What transfers positively:** if you are already a confident Urdu reader, you already know
  how to track print left-to-right within a line once you adjust the overall direction, how to
  sound out a written symbol system, and how phonemes work as units of sound (your Urdu
  phonemic awareness genuinely transfers — you are not learning "what a sound is" for the
  first time, only new sound-to-letter mappings) (`research/04` §1, §6).

## If you are also new to reading in Urdu

If you've had little or no reading instruction in Urdu either, that's fine — this course does
not require Urdu literacy as a prerequisite. Weak first-language literacy is common, not rare,
among the population this course is built for (`research/04` §6 cites ASER Pakistan 2023: only
about half of surveyed children could read a grade-2-level Urdu story). Start at Level 1 exactly
as a never-taught native English speaker would — the oral phonological-awareness on-ramp in
1.1 works the same way regardless of what, if any, script you already read.

## How this guide fits into your lessons

This guide does not replace any lesson — it supplements it. Whenever a lesson introduces one
of the four trouble spots above (th in Level 2.3, v/w in Level 1.11/2.6, short vowels
throughout Level 1, consonant blends in Level 2.6–2.8), the lesson itself will note "Urdu-
speaker tip: see `urdu-speakers.md`." Come back to this page whenever that note appears, and
run the relevant minimal-pair drill a few extra times — a few minutes of deliberate contrastive
practice on these four specific points is worth more than generic repetition.
