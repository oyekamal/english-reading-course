# Guide for Tutors and Parents

You do not need a teaching qualification to run these lessons. They are scripted so you can
follow them directly. This guide covers the exact language to use, what to do when a learner
is stuck, how to keep an adult's dignity and a child's engagement, and how to track progress.
Read this once, fully, before your first lesson.

## The one rule that matters most: never cue

"Cueing" means prompting a learner to guess a word from anything other than its letters — "look
at the picture," "what would make sense here," "start again and guess." This is banned in this
course, full stop (`DESIGN.md` rule 1). It feels helpful in the moment and actively damages
reading development, because it teaches the learner that guessing is an acceptable substitute
for decoding (`research/02`, on why "balanced literacy"/three-cueing was rejected as a method
after failing controlled comparisons against systematic phonics).

**Banned phrases:** "Guess." "What do you think it says?" "Look at the picture." "What word
would make sense there?" "Just skip it and keep going."

**Correct phrases, in order, when a learner is stuck on a word:**
1. "Point to each sound." (Let them physically point under each letter or letter-group.)
2. "Say the sounds." (Each one, left to right.)
3. "Now blend them together." (Say the sounds fast, running into each other.)
4. If they blend it wrong: "Try that again, a little faster." (Often the error is a slow,
   separated blend, not a wrong sound.)
5. If a sound itself is wrong: go back to step 2 and correct only that sound — "That letter
   says /x/, not /y/. Try again." — then re-blend from step 3.
6. Only *after* a word is correctly decoded, you may ask: "Does that make sense in the
   sentence?" — this is allowed as a final check, never as a way to solve the word
   (`DESIGN.md` rule 1).

## Error-correction routine (use every time, same words)

1. Learner reads a word wrong.
2. You say: "Let's check that." (Neutral, not "no" or "wrong" — no judgment in your tone.)
3. Point to the specific letter/letters that caused the error.
4. "What sound does that make?" (Let them try to self-correct first — give 3 seconds.)
5. If they get it: "Yes. Now blend the whole word again."
6. If they don't: tell them the sound directly, have them repeat it, then re-blend the whole
   word from the start — never just the part they got wrong; whole-word reblending builds the
   habit of blending as a unit.
7. Move on. Don't dwell, don't repeat the correction more than once per word in a single
   pass — if the same error recurs across many words, that's the reteach signal below, not a
   discipline issue.

## Dictation / spelling routine (used in every lesson's "Spell it" step)

1. Say the sound (not the letter name): "/s/ — write the letter that makes that sound."
2. Say the whole word slowly, sound by sound: "/k/ /a/ /t/ — cat. Segment it with your fingers
   first, then write it."
3. Check the writing immediately, letter by letter — don't wait until the end of a sentence.
4. For heart words: "This word has a regular part and a 'heart' part you just have to know.
   The regular part is ___. The heart part is ___." Mark (underline or highlight) only the
   heart part — never the whole word — so the learner sees how much of it is actually
   decodable (`DESIGN.md` rule 5).

## What to do when a learner is stuck (beyond a single word)

- **Same error across several words in a lesson:** stop, go back to the "Meet it" step for
  that specific sound/pattern, re-teach it explicitly, then re-attempt the word list from the
  start. Don't push forward hoping it "clicks" later — the mastery-gate rule exists precisely
  so you don't (`DESIGN.md` rule 4).
- **Below 90% on the lesson's Check step:** don't advance. Re-teach the specific
  sound(s)/pattern(s) that were missed (look at *which* words were wrong, not just the score),
  then re-run the Check with a fresh set of words at the same level of difficulty — you can
  build a fresh check by swapping in different real/pseudowords using the same letters/pattern
  from the lesson's word list.
- **A learner freezes or goes quiet on a word for more than ~5 seconds:** that's your cue to
  start the correction routine above at step 3 (point to letters) — don't let a long silent
  struggle continue; it isn't building anything useful and it's often the moment dignity
  starts to erode for an adult learner.
- **A learner is clearly guessing from context rather than decoding** (reads a wrong word that
  fits the sentence meaning but doesn't match the letters): this is the moment to be most
  firm about the no-cueing rule — say "Let's check that word specifically" and run the full
  point/say/blend routine, even though the guess "made sense."

## Keeping an adult learner's dignity

- Never use child-coded material, images, or examples with an adult or teen learner — always
  use Track B texts (`DESIGN.md` rule 6). If you're building any extra practice materials
  yourself, keep them in adult-relevant contexts: work, money, family, health, the phone, the
  market, the bus, the news.
- Never call the practice words "baby words" or compare pace to children's typical timelines.
  Say "made-up words" or "practice words," not "nonsense," which can land as mocking.
- Never express surprise ("You don't know this?!") at a gap, however basic. Assume nothing
  about what an adult was or wasn't taught.
- Give adults control over pace and session length explicitly: "We can stop here and pick up
  next time, whenever works for you" — reinforce the pause-safe rule out loud, don't just rely
  on them reading it in `start-here.md`.
- If an adult wants to skip straight to a mastery check because they suspect they already know
  the content: let them. That's exactly what the "skip if you know it" rule is for
  (`DESIGN.md` rule 10) — it isn't disrespectful of the course to ask for it, and refusing
  would be disrespectful of them.

## Keeping a child engaged

- Keep sessions to the stated time (20–35 min) — don't extend "just a bit more" even if a
  child seems willing. This is general good practice for keeping attention and momentum, not
  something the research base specifically measured. The evidence-backed lever for older
  struggling readers is **group size**, not session length — Wanzek et al. (2013) found small
  group size (1:1 or very small group) was the significant moderator of outcomes, not
  frequency or duration of sessions (`research/03` §5) — and a single tutor with one learner
  already has that advantage, whatever the session length.
- Multisensory add-ons (tapping out sounds on fingers, tracing letters in the air or in sand,
  saying a sound while writing it) are genuinely optional — use them if a child enjoys them or
  needs the extra engagement, but never present them as required for the method to work, because
  the evidence doesn't support that claim (`DESIGN.md` rule 11; `research/03` §2–3 on
  multisensory instruction having no isolated evidence of added value beyond explicit,
  systematic teaching).
- Praise the *process* ("You pointed to every sound before you blended — that's exactly right")
  more than the outcome ("Good job!") — it reinforces the actual decoding habit you want
  automatic.
- If a child is bored with a word list, let them do the Listen & Talk read-aloud step early or
  add extra time there — that step is designed to be the enjoyable, lower-pressure part of
  each lesson.

## Progress tracking sheet

Track **decoding** and **comprehension** as two separate lines — never combine them into one
score (`DESIGN.md` rule 8). Use one row per lesson.

```
Learner: _______________            Level: _____

| Lesson | Date | Decoding check (X/10) | ≥90%? | Comprehension Qs (X/Y) | Notes / reteach needed |
|--------|------|------------------------|-------|--------------------------|------------------------|
| 1.1    |      |                        |       |                          |                        |
| 1.2    |      |                        |       |                          |                        |
| 1.3    |      |                        |       |                          |                        |
| ...    |      |                        |       |                          |                        |
```

- **Decoding metric:** the lesson's Check step score (real + pseudowords + 1 dictated word),
  out of however many items that lesson's check has (each lesson template specifies this).
- **Comprehension metric:** the "Read it" and "Listen & Talk" question scores from that
  lesson, tracked separately from decoding — a learner can be decoding well but comprehending
  poorly, or vice versa, and conflating the two metrics hides that.
- **Notes column:** write down *which* sound/pattern needed a reteach, not just "struggled" —
  this is what tells you (or the next tutor) exactly where to focus next session.
- Re-administer the placement test's relevant Stage 3 tier every 4–6 weeks even for a learner
  progressing normally, to catch anyone who has accelerated past a gap or is ready to skip
  ahead (`research/04` §5, rule 8: diagnostic checkpoints, not just at entry).
