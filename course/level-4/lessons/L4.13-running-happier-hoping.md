# L4.13 — Running, Happier, Hoping

**Goal:** Learn three spelling rules for adding suffixes: the **1-1-1 doubling rule**, the
**change-y-to-i rule**, and a review of the **drop-e rule** — so spelling stays correct as
words grow longer.
**New today:** 1-1-1 doubling rule, change-y-to-i rule; drop-e reviewed
**Review:** syllable division + schwa, and all of Level 4's earlier patterns
**Heart words:** none new
**Time:** 30 min
**Materials:** paper, pencil

## 1. Warm-up review (2–3 min)
Read 5 review words: *napkin, spider, cabin, money, people.* Name the three ways to split a
VCV-pattern word.

## 2. Hear it (2 min)
Say *run* then *running*. Ask: "What happened to the letters when -ing was added?" (The n
doubled.) Say *happy* then *happier*. "What changed?" (The y became i.) Say *hope* then
*hoping*. "What happened?" (The e disappeared.) Three different spelling changes — today's
job is to know which change happens when.

## 3. Meet it (3 min)
- **1-1-1 doubling rule:** if a word has **1 syllable, 1 vowel, 1 final consonant**, double
  that final consonant before adding a vowel suffix (-ing, -ed, -er, -est): *run→running,
  hop→hopped, big→bigger, sit→sitting.* Why? Doubling keeps the vowel sound **short** — without
  it, *hoping* (one p) would look like it should rhyme with *hope*, not *hop*.
- **Change-y-to-i rule:** if a word ends in a consonant + y, change the y to i before most
  suffixes: *happy→happier, happiest; carry→carried; baby→babies.* **Exception: keep the y
  before -ing** (so it doesn't create "ii"): *cry→crying, carry→carrying, study→studying.*
- **Drop-e rule (review from Level 3):** if a word ends in silent e, drop the e before a
  vowel suffix: *hope→hoping, make→making, nice→nicer.* Keep the e before a consonant suffix:
  *hope→hopeful, nice→nicely.*
Three different jobs, three different reasons — the goal is spelling that shows the correct
sound, not spelling for its own sake. **Urdu-speaker tip:** these rules protect a **sound**
(short vs. long vowel, or avoiding an awkward letter run), not just a written pattern — say
the word aloud before and after each rule to hear why the spelling must change.

## 4. Blend it (5 min)
1-1-1 doubling: running, hopped, bigger, sitting, swimming, stopped, planning, dropped,
sadder, wetter, hottest, thinner.
Change-y-to-i: happier, happiest, carried, babies, funnier, tries, families, studied.
Keep-y-before-ing (contrast): crying, carrying, studying, trying.
Drop-e review: hoping, making, nicer, using, caring, largest.

Pseudowords (apply the rule): *vop → vopping* (double), *plopy → plopier* (y to i), *snape →
snaping* (drop e), *ferr → ferrer* (double), *dass → dassied* (y to i, base treated as if it
ended in consonant+y for practice).

## 5. Spell it (4 min)
1. Say the base and the rule needed: *run + ing* → *running* (double). *happy + er* →
   *happier* (y to i). *hope + ing* → *hoping* (drop e).
2. Words (learner applies the right rule from just hearing the word): *stopped, carried,
   using.*
3. Sentence: **"She was hoping the funnier, bigger clown was carrying the babies' toys."**
   (deliberately stacks all three rules in one sentence for practice)

## 6. Heart word(s) (2 min)
No new heart word today. Quick cumulative check: spell *money* and *people*, saying the
schwa syllable correctly (from 4.12).

## 7. Read it (5–8 min)

### Track A — "The Bigger, Funnier Puppy"
> Iman's puppy, Buttons, was getting bigger every week. "He's the funniest, wobbliest puppy on
> the street," Iman's dad said, laughing as Buttons went running and slipping across the
> kitchen floor. Iman had been hoping for a puppy for months, and now she was caring for
> Buttons every day — feeding him, walking him, and studying every trick in her puppy training
> book. "He's not the smartest puppy," Iman admitted, "but he's trying." One evening, Buttons
> started chasing his tail, spinning and tripping until he flopped down, dizzy. Iman and her
> family were all crying with laughter. "That was the funniest thing I've seen all week," her
> mom said, still wiping tears from laughing so hard.

*Questions:* (1) What has Iman been hoping for, and what is she doing now? (2) What does
Buttons do that makes the family laugh? (3) Why do you think the story calls Buttons "the
funniest, wobbliest puppy"?

### Track B — "Studying for a Bigger Job"
> Sarah has been studying at night, hoping for a bigger role at her company. She has been
> carrying a heavy load — working her regular shift, then studying for two hours before bed.
> "It's the hardest I've ever tried at anything," she tells her sister, tapping her pencil,
> stopping only to stretch her tired hands. Her manager, impressed by how she's been
> performing, mentions she is planning to recommend Sarah for the new position. "You've been
> working harder than anyone else on the team," her manager says. Sarah feels her effort
> paying off — she keeps studying, knowing the extra work is worth it for a chance at
> something bigger.

*Questions:* (1) What has Sarah been doing at night? (2) What does her manager say about her
work? (3) Why does Sarah keep studying even though it's hard?

## Fluency check (2–3 min, every lesson)
Have the learner read Track A or Track B aloud once, cold (no rehearsal first). Time it and
count **WCPM** (words correct per minute): count total words read in one minute, subtract
errors not self-corrected. Note one **prosody** observation too — did they pause at commas
and periods, use a questioning tone on "?", change their voice for dialogue in quotes? Log
both numbers in the learner's own tracker. At this level, texts are short and heavily
controlled, so WCPM will stay fairly flat lesson to lesson — that is expected. The target is
smooth, accurate, expressive reading, never raw speed (assisted-practice model, not a race).

## 8. Listen & Talk (5 min)
Read aloud: "People who keep working toward a goal, even slowly and through setbacks, show
**perseverance** — refusing to give up, even when progress feels slow." **Tier-2 word:
perseverance** — friendly definition: "continuing to try, even when something is hard or
takes a long time." Example 1: "Learning to read takes perseverance." Example 2: "Her
perseverance paid off when she finally passed the test." Your turn: "Can you think of
something that took you a long time and a lot of perseverance to learn?"
*Discuss:* What helps you keep going when something feels slow or hard?

## 9. Check (2 min)
Real words: *running, happier, hoping, carried, stopped.*
Pseudowords: *vopping (1-1-1 doubling), plopier (y to i), snaping (drop e), ferrer (1-1-1
doubling), dassied (y to i).*
Dictate: *"studying."*
**≥90%? → 4.14. Below 90%?** Reteach one rule at a time: give 5 base words needing only the
doubling rule, check, then 5 needing only y-to-i, check, then mix all three.

## Tutor notes
- **Common error:** doubling the consonant on words that don't qualify (e.g., writing
  "rainning" for *raining* — two vowels before the consonant, so no doubling needed).
  **Correction:** always say the 1-1-1 checklist aloud before applying: "one syllable? one
  vowel? one final consonant?" — if any answer is no, don't double.
- **Common error:** changing y to i before -ing (writing "studing"). **Correction:** the
  keep-y-before-ing exception is worth its own drill — contrast *carried* vs. *carrying* side
  by side until it's automatic.
- **For adults:** Track B's workplace-studying scenario reflects a real and common adult
  situation (studying while working) — validate that this is genuinely hard, not just a
  school-kid problem.
- **For young kids:** the puppy story's humor is a good moment to let the learner retell the
  funniest part in their own words before moving to Listen & Talk.
- **Never say:** "just memorize the spelling" — always route back to the specific rule (1-1-1,
  y-to-i, or drop-e) and the sound reason behind it.
