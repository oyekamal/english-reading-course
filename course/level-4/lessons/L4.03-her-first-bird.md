# L4.3 — Her First Bird

**Goal:** Read and spell words with **er, ir, ur** — three spellings, one sound: /er/.
**New today:** er, ir, ur
**Review:** ar, or, ore
**Heart words:** none new — review *were, there*
**Time:** 25 min
**Materials:** paper, pencil

## 1. Warm-up review (2–3 min)
Read 5 review words: *fork, store, north, before, harvest.* Flash sounds: ar, or, ore, ee, oa.

## 2. Hear it (2 min)
Say *her*. Isolate: /h/ /er/. Say *bird*: /b/ /er/ /d/. Say *fur*: /f/ /er/. Ask: "Do these
three words — her, bird, fur — end in the same sound even though they're spelled
differently?" Let the learner notice: yes, one sound, three spellings.

## 3. Meet it (3 min)
Show all three: **er, ir, ur**. "All three teams say the exact same sound: /er/. There's no
rule for which one to use in a new word — you just have to learn each word's spelling as you
meet it, the same way you learned which vowel team a word uses." **Urdu-speaker tip:** this
/er/ sound (a "schwa + r" or vowel-r blend) has no single Urdu match; keep lips relaxed, not
rounded (that's /or/) and not open (that's /ar/) — a small, neutral, pulled-back sound.
Practice: *her, bird, fur* side by side.

## 4. Blend it (5 min)
Real words — er: her, term, herd, fern, perch, person, under, winter, summer, sister.
ir: bird, girl, dirt, first, third, shirt, stir, chirp, birth, thirst, skirt, whirl.
ur: fur, hurt, turn, burn, curl, church, nurse, purse, curb, surf.

Pseudowords: dirn, sturf, ferb, zirt, thur.

## 5. Spell it (4 min)
1. Sounds: /b/ /er/ /d/ → *bird*. /t/ /er/ /n/ → *turn*. /p/ /er/ /s/ /ə/ /n/ → *person*
   (tutor: help segment the second syllable loosely; don't demand perfect schwa spelling yet).
2. Words: *first, church, winter.*
3. Sentence: **"Her first bird was a small brown bird."**

## 6. Heart word(s) (2 min)
Review **were**: /w/ regular, "ere" is the heart part (spells /er/ irregularly — compare to
today's *er*, which the learner now knows, so point out *were*'s spelling is close but not
exactly a match). Review **there**: same heart part, different meaning — a good moment to
contrast *there/were* with *her/fur* so learners don't confuse look-alikes.

## 7. Read it (5–8 min)

### Track A — "Her First Bird"
> It was winter. Nadia's dad set up a bird perch by the porch. Nadia sat still, waiting for
> her first bird to land. First a big black bird sat on the fence. Then a tiny bird with a
> red chest came to perch. "Look! Its chirp is so soft," Nadia said. Her sister ran to see too.
> "Don't stir, or it will fly off," Nadia told her. The bird did not fly off — it stayed and
> had a drink, then flew off with a quick turn. Nadia and her sister sat under the tree until
> the sun went down.

*Questions:* (1) What season is it? (2) What does Nadia's sister almost do wrong? (3) Why do
you think the bird flew away at the end?

### Track B — "A Shift at the Nurse's Desk"
> Bilal is a nurse. His shift starts at sunup. First, he checks each person's
> chart. Then he helps a girl with a hurt arm — she fell by the curb and needs a splint.
> "Try not to stir it too much," Bilal tells her. Her mother sits with her, holding her purse.
> Later, an older man comes in with a bad thirst and a fever. Bilal checks his term of illness
> and rings the doctor. By the end of his shift, Bilal is tired, but glad he could help.
> "Nursing is hard work," he tells his sister that night, "but I would not turn back."

*Questions:* (1) What is Bilal's job? (2) What two patients does he help? (3) Why do you
think Bilal says he "would not turn back"?

## Fluency check (2–3 min, every lesson)
Have the learner read Track A or Track B aloud once, cold (no rehearsal first). Time it and
count **WCPM** (words correct per minute): count total words read in one minute, subtract
errors not self-corrected. Note one **prosody** observation too — did they pause at commas
and periods, use a questioning tone on "?", change their voice for dialogue in quotes? Log
both numbers in the learner's own tracker. At this level, texts are short and heavily
controlled, so WCPM will stay fairly flat lesson to lesson — that is expected. The target is
smooth, accurate, expressive reading, never raw speed (assisted-practice model, not a race).

## 8. Listen & Talk (5 min)
Read aloud: "People who care for the sick and injured, easing pain and helping bodies heal,
often say the work feels like a **calling** — something they feel deeply pulled to do, not
just a job." **Tier-2 word: calling** — friendly definition: "a job or purpose a person
feels strongly drawn to, more than just work for money." Example 1: "Teaching was her
calling — she loved it even on hard days." Example 2: "Some people feel farming is their
calling." Your turn: "Do you know anyone who talks about their job as a calling?"
*Discuss:* What jobs help people directly, like a nurse does? Would you want a job like that?

## 9. Check (2 min)
Real words: *bird, turn, first, church, winter.*
Pseudowords: *ferl, dirst, thurb, zern, sturk.*
Dictate: *"her."*
**≥90%? → 4.4. Below 90%?** Reteach step 2 with just *her, bird, fur* — have the learner say
all three, eyes closed, confirming they sound identical before re-showing the spellings.

## Tutor notes
- **Common error:** trying to "sound out" er/ir/ur differently from each other because they
  look different. **Correction:** always say "same sound, new spelling" — never let the
  learner invent three different vowel-r sounds.
- **Common error:** confusing /ar/, /or/, /er/ mid-word (e.g., reading *turn* as *torn*).
  **Correction:** three-way choice check — say all three sounds, ask which one this word has,
  before blending.
- **For adults:** Track B's Urdu-cognate-free vocabulary (*nurse, shift, splint, fever*) is
  everyday workplace English — a good moment to affirm the learner already knows these words
  aloud; today's lesson only adds the spelling.
- **For young kids:** the bird-watching narrative supports a real "wait quietly" discussion —
  useful for building patience alongside phonics.
- **Never say:** "just remember it" for er/ir/ur choice in a *new* word being met for the
  first time — that's fine for a *known* word, but for truly new vocabulary, tell the learner
  directly which spelling it uses; don't make them guess.
