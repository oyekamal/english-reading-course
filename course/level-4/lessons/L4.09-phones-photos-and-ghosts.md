# L4.9 — Phones, Photos, and Ghosts

**Goal:** Read and spell words with **ph** (/f/), **ch** saying /k/ or /sh/ (not just its
usual /ch/), and **gh** (as /g/ in *ghost*, and as /f/ in *rough, tough, enough, cough*).
**New today:** ph, ch (/k/, /sh/), gh
**Review:** ea(/ĕ/), kn, wr, gn, mb, and all earlier vowel-r/diphthong/aw teams
**Heart words:** none new — review *laugh* (already known — now explainable!)
**Time:** 30 min
**Materials:** paper, pencil

## 1. Warm-up review (2–3 min)
Read 5 review words: *bread, know, write, sign, comb.* Flash sounds: ea(/ĕ/), kn, wr, mb.

## 2. Hear it (2 min)
Say *phone*. Ask: "What's the first sound?" (/f/.) Show it's spelled **ph**, not f. Say
*school*: "What's the middle sound?" (/k/, not the usual /ch/.) Say *chef*: "What's the first
sound?" (/sh/, not /ch/ or /k/.) Say *ghost*: "What's the first sound?" (/g/ — the h is
silent.) Say *rough*: "What's the last sound?" (/f/ — a totally different gh job.)

## 3. Meet it (3 min)
Four things to hold at once, so go slowly:
- **ph = /f/** — mostly in words from Greek origin (*phone, photo, graph, phrase*).
- **ch = /k/** — also often Greek-origin words (*school, echo, chorus, ache, stomach*).
- **ch = /sh/** — rarer, mostly French-origin words; treat these as a short heart-word-style
  list to learn by sight (*chef, machine, chute*).
- **gh = /g/** at the start of a word (*ghost, ghastly*) or **gh = /f/** at the end of some
  words (*rough, tough, enough, cough*) or **silent** in others (*though, through* — flag
  these as heart-word-style exceptions, not a rule to over-apply).
**Urdu-speaker tip:** none of these four behaviors map neatly onto Urdu spelling-sound rules;
frame this explicitly as "English borrowed spellings from other languages and kept their
old sound-patterns" — a historical fact, not a puzzle to solve logically.

## 4. Blend it (5 min)
ph (/f/): phone, photo, graph, phrase, dolphin, trophy.
ch (/k/): school, echo, chorus, stomach, ache.
ch (/sh/) — heart-word-style: chef, machine, chute.
gh (/g/): ghost, ghastly.
gh (/f/): rough, tough, enough, cough.
gh (silent, heart-word-style): though, through.

Pseudowords: phin (/f/), chask (/k/), ghab (/g/), snough (/f/ ending, alien word), phord.

## 5. Spell it (4 min)
1. Sounds: /f/ /oa/ /n/ → *phone* (tutor: point out ph choice — a spelling fact to remember
   for this word family, since /f/ is usually spelled with f). /s/ /k/ /oo/ /l/ → *school.*
2. Words: *photo, ache, tough.*
3. Sentence: **"I took a photo of the old ghost story at school."**

## 6. Heart word(s) (2 min)
Review **laugh**: mark it fully now — /l/ /a/ (regular, short a) + **augh saying /af/**
(heart part, an unusual combination of today's au and gh-as-f patterns fused together).

## 7. Read it (5–8 min)

### Track A — "The Ghost Story at School"
> At school, Miss Amna told a ghost story before lunch. "This school used to be a farmhouse,"
> she said, "and some say a ghost still walks the halls at night." Hamza's stomach did a
> quick flip. "Is it true?" he asked. "Maybe," Miss Amna said with a grin, "or maybe it's just
> an old tale." At recess, Hamza and his friends took a photo of the old, dusty hallway,
> hoping to catch something strange in the photo. They didn't see a ghost, but they did find
> an old graph pinned to a wall, showing how many students went to the school long ago. It was
> a rough, faded page, but Hamza's teacher said it was a real piece of history. Hamza laughed,
> his stomach finally calm again.

*Questions:* (1) What story does Miss Amna tell? (2) What do Hamza and his friends do at
recess? (3) What do they find instead of a ghost?

### Track B — "A Tough Day at the Clinic"
> Dr. Farid works at a small clinic. Today has been a tough day — the phone has not stopped
> ringing. A patient calls to ask if her cough is bad enough to come in. "It sounds rough,"
> Dr. Farid says, "come in and I will check your chest with a stethoscope." Later, a machine
> in the clinic breaks down, and Dr. Farid must call for a repair. He takes a photo of the
> broken part to send to the repair shop. By the end of his shift, his stomach aches from
> stress, but he still has enough energy to smile at his last patient. "Tough day," he tells
> his wife that night, "but a good one."

*Questions:* (1) Why has Dr. Farid's day been tough? (2) What does he do about the broken
machine? (3) How does he feel by the end of his shift?

## Fluency check (2–3 min, every lesson)
Have the learner read Track A or Track B aloud once, cold (no rehearsal first). Time it and
count **WCPM** (words correct per minute): count total words read in one minute, subtract
errors not self-corrected. Note one **prosody** observation too — did they pause at commas
and periods, use a questioning tone on "?", change their voice for dialogue in quotes? Log
both numbers in the learner's own tracker. At this level, texts are short and heavily
controlled, so WCPM will stay fairly flat lesson to lesson — that is expected. The target is
smooth, accurate, expressive reading, never raw speed (assisted-practice model, not a race).

## 8. Listen & Talk (5 min)
Read aloud: "A doctor or nurse who stays warm and patient with every person they see, even on
an exhausting day, shows real **compassion** — caring about someone else's pain as if it were
your own." **Tier-2 word: compassion** — friendly definition: "caring deeply about someone
else's suffering and wanting to help." Example 1: "The nurse treated every patient with
compassion." Example 2: "Compassion means noticing when someone is struggling and offering
help." Your turn: "Can you think of someone who has shown you compassion?"
*Discuss:* Why might it be hard to stay compassionate on a tough, tiring day? What helps
people keep going anyway?

## 9. Check (2 min)
Real words: *phone, school, ghost, rough, laugh.*
Pseudowords: *phin, chask, ghab, snough, phord.*
Dictate: *"photo."*
**≥90%? → 4.10. Below 90%?** This lesson has four sub-patterns — isolate which one is
missed (ph? ch? gh-as-g? gh-as-f?) and redo only that mini-set of 5 words before re-checking
the whole list.

## Tutor notes
- **Common error:** reading ph as two sounds (/p/ + /h/) instead of one /f/. **Correction:**
  cover the letters and just say "this spelling always means /f/ — no exceptions in this
  lesson's word set."
- **Common error:** applying /k/ or /sh/ ch-readings to ordinary /ch/ words (like reading
  *chair* with a /k/ sound). **Correction:** frame ch=/k/ and ch=/sh/ words as a short,
  specific list to build up over time, not a new default — the regular /ch/ sound (as in
  *chair, church*) is still by far the most common.
- **For adults:** Track B's clinic setting normalizes seeking medical help and names real
  workplace vocabulary (*stethoscope, patient, shift*) an adult learner is likely to encounter
  or use.
- **For young kids:** the ghost-story frame is genuinely engaging — keep the tone
  light/curious rather than scary, matching the story's own "maybe it's just an old tale."
- **Never say:** "just memorize it" without also giving the pattern name (ph, ch/k/, ch/sh/,
  gh/g/, gh/f/) — naming the pattern helps the learner file it correctly for next time.
