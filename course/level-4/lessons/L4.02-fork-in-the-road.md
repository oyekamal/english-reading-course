# L4.2 — The Fork in the Road (or/ore extended + the war/wor quirk)

**Goal:** Review **or** and **ore** (/or/ as in *for* and *more*, taught in Level 3.09),
extend to multisyllabic or/ore words, and meet today's genuinely new content: the **war/wor
quirk** — when w sits right before or, the vowel sound shifts (*war* → /wor/, *work* →
/wer/), even though the spelling still uses the familiar "or."
**New today:** the war/wor quirk (warm, war, work, word, world)
**Review:** or, ore (Level 3.09); ar (Level 3.08); closed syllables, VCe, open syllables
**Heart words:** none new — review *four, your, they*
**Time:** 25 min
**Materials:** paper, pencil

## 1. Warm-up review (2–3 min)
Read 5 review words with **or/ore**: *for, corn, storm, more, store.* Flash sounds: ee, oa,
igh, ar, or.

## 2. Hear it (2 min)
Say *for*: /f/ /or/. Now say *war*: /w/ /??/ — ask "does this sound like /or/ in *for*, or
something else?" (Something else — a rounder, deeper sound, /wor/.) Say *work*: /w/ /??/ /k/
— again, not quite /or/, more like /wer/. Point out: same "or" spelling both times, but the
w right before it is bending the vowel sound.

## 3. Meet it (3 min)
Quick review only: "or says /or/, ore says /or/ with a silent e — same as always." Then the
real new content: "**When w comes right before or, watch out — the vowel often shifts.**
**War** says /wor/, like in *war, warm, wart, ward, warn* — a rounder sound than plain
/or/. **Wor** says /wer/, like in *work, word, world, worm, worth, worst* — this one
actually sounds like the er/ir/ur family from 4.3, not like /or/ at all! The spelling stays
"or" both times — only the sound changes, and only because of the w. This is a genuine
spelling-vs-sound mismatch, not a mistake you're making." **Urdu-speaker tip:** because the
spelling looks identical to the or/ore already learned, the instinct will be to read *war*
and *work* with the regular /or/ sound — that's a reasonable first guess, but flag it
explicitly as one of the small set of words where you must override the spelling-based guess
with the sound you actually recognize from speech.

## 4. Blend it (5 min)
Single-syllable or/ore review: for, fork, corn, horn, torn, sport, short, storm, form, fort,
north, more, sore, wore, core, store, shore, snore, score.

Multisyllabic or/ore words (today's extension): before, story, morning, forget, corner,
explore, restore, ignore.

war/wor (today's new quirk): war, warm, wart, ward, warn, toward, work, word, world, worm,
worth, worst.

Pseudowords: dorl, plor, storg, morst, worz (worz — say it the "wor" (/wer/) way, not plain
/or/, since it starts with w).

## 5. Spell it (4 min)
1. Sounds: /f/ /or/ /k/ → *fork*. /s/ /t/ /or/ /m/ → *storm*. /m/ /ore/ → *more.*
2. Words: *corn, short, store, before.*
3. Sentence: **"The corn is short before the storm."**

## 6. Heart word(s) (2 min)
Review **four**: mark it — /f/ is regular, "our" is the heart part (spells /or/ in an
irregular way). Review **your**: /y/ regular, "our" the heart part again.

## 7. Read it (5–8 min)

### Track A — "The Storm at the Farm"
> It is harvest time on the farm. Dad and Sam load the cart before the storm starts. Sam picks
> corn from the yard. His dog Storm (yes, that is her name!) runs to the barn. "A storm is
> coming, Storm!" Sam yells. The wind is strong, and rain starts to come down fast. Dad
> and Sam sort the corn and put it in the fort — a tiny shed by the barn. Storm the dog
> barks at the wind. Soon the storm is gone, and the sun shows itself again from the north.
> "That was a short storm," Sam says. "More corn for us to sort tomorrow."

*Questions:* (1) What does the dog's name mean, and why is that funny? (2) What do Dad and
Sam do before the storm starts? (3) Why do you think Storm the dog barks at the wind?

### Track B — "A Short Trip North"
> Amina drives north for work. Before she starts the car, she checks the horn and the fuel.
> The road has a fork — one way goes north, one goes to the port. Amina picks the north road.
> A storm starts, and rain comes down hard and hits the window. Amina slows the car and clicks
> on the lights. At last, she spots the store where she must stop for parts for the fort's old
> gate. She picks four parts, pays, and starts the drive back. "That was a short but hard
> trip," she tells her boss. "I will need more time for the next one."

*Questions:* (1) What does Amina check before she starts driving? (2) What does the fork in
the road mean here? (3) Why might Amina need more time next time?

## Fluency check (2–3 min, every lesson)
Have the learner read Track A or Track B aloud once, cold (no rehearsal first). Time it and
count **WCPM** (words correct per minute): count total words read in one minute, subtract
errors not self-corrected. Note one **prosody** observation too — did they pause at commas
and periods, use a questioning tone on "?", change their voice for dialogue in quotes? Log
both numbers in the learner's own tracker. At this level, texts are short and heavily
controlled, so WCPM will stay fairly flat lesson to lesson — that is expected. The target is
smooth, accurate, expressive reading, never raw speed (assisted-practice model, not a race).

## 8. Listen & Talk (5 min)
Read aloud: "Long before cars, people used horses to travel. A **caravan** was a group of
travelers — often with camels or horses — who journeyed together across long, hard roads for
safety." **Tier-2 word: caravan** — friendly definition: "a group of people traveling
together, usually a long way, for safety and company." Example 1: "A caravan crossed the
desert to trade goods." Example 2: "The family joined a caravan of cars driving to the
wedding." Your turn: "Why might people prefer to travel together instead of alone?"
*Discuss:* Have you ever traveled somewhere far with a group? What might go wrong if you
traveled that far alone?

## 9. Check (2 min)
Real words: *fork, store, before, work, world* (the last two must be read with the war/wor
shift, not plain /or/).
Pseudowords: *dorl, plort, storf, forn, worz.*
Dictate: *"more."*
**≥90%? → 4.3. Below 90%?** If plain or/ore words are the problem, reteach step 2 with just
*for, more, corn*, exaggerating lip rounding, then redo the single-syllable list in step 4.
If or/ore is solid but *work/world*-type words are read with the wrong (plain /or/) sound,
the war/wor quirk itself hasn't landed — redo step 3's explicit contrast (*for* vs. *war* vs.
*work*, said aloud side by side) before re-checking.

## Tutor notes
- **Common error:** mixing up /ar/ and /or/ mid-word (reading *fork* as *fark*). **Correction:**
  hold up two fingers, say "which one — /ar/ (mouth open) or /or/ (lips round)?" before
  re-blending, rather than telling the answer.
- **Common error:** treating **ore** as a brand-new sound instead of recognizing it's the
  same /or/ with a VCe pattern they already know. **Correction:** point back to *hope→hoping*
  logic — same silent-e job, just paired with a new vowel-team-like base.
- **For adults:** Track B "caravan" links to widely known Urdu/South Asian usage (کارواں) —
  point out the English word is a direct cognate, which builds confidence rather than
  treating vocabulary as entirely foreign.
- **For young kids:** the dog-named-Storm joke is a good moment to check literal
  comprehension ("Wait — is a real storm coming, or is that the dog's name?").
- **Never say:** "it just sounds right" — always route back to the letters.
