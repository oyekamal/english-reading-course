# L4.5 — The Noisy Boy

**Goal:** Read and spell words with the diphthong **oi** and **oy** (one sound, two spellings).
**New today:** oi, oy
**Review:** ar, or, ore, er, ir, ur, air, are, ear, eer, war, wor
**Heart words:** none new — review *once, boy(not a heart word — decodable now, see note)*
**Time:** 25 min
**Materials:** paper, pencil

## 1. Warm-up review (2–3 min)
Read 5 review words: *fair, care, deer, work, world.* Flash sounds: air, eer, war, wor.

## 2. Hear it (2 min)
Say *oil*. Isolate the vowel sound: /oi/ — starts round, glides to /i/. Say *boy*: /b/ /oi/.
Ask: "Same sound, or different?" (Same.) This is a **diphthong** — a vowel sound that glides
from one mouth shape to another inside a single sound, like a tiny two-part sound.

## 3. Meet it (3 min)
Show **oi** and **oy**: "Both spell the exact same glide, /oi/. Here's the pattern that
usually holds: **oi** in the middle of a word (*oil, coin*), **oy** at the end of a word
(*boy, toy*)." This is a genuinely reliable positional rule — point it out as one of the few
spelling choices in English that's almost always predictable. **Urdu-speaker tip:** Urdu has
a similar glide sound (as in تیل *tel*'s neighbor sounds, or English loanwords like "toy");
this one is usually easy — the main job is just linking it to the two spellings.

## 4. Blend it (5 min)
oi (mid-word): oil, boil, coin, join, point, joint, spoil, moist, soil, void, noise, choice,
voice.
oy (end of word): boy, toy, joy, soy, enjoy, annoy, royal, destroy.

Pseudowords: oip, floy, moip, scoy, throil.

## 5. Spell it (4 min)
1. Sounds: /oi/ /l/ → *oil*. /p/ /oi/ /n/ /t/ → *point*. /b/ /oi/ → *boy* (tutor: point out
   this is end-of-word, so it's spelled oy, not oi — a spelling choice, not a sound choice).
2. Words: *coin, voice, enjoy.*
3. Sentence: **"The boy made a choice to join in."**

## 6. Heart word(s) (2 min)
No new heart word today. Review **once**: point out its "o" sound is actually a /wuh/ sound
before "nce" — a genuinely irregular heart part, unrelated to today's oi/oy but worth a
quick re-check since it often gets stale in memory.

## 7. Read it (5–8 min)

### Track A — "The Noisy Boy"
> Zayn is a boy who makes a lot of noise. In class, his choice seat is next to the window, and
> he likes to spot every bird, truck, and train he sees. "Zayn, use a soft voice," his
> teacher says. At recess, Zayn and his friends toss a coin to pick a game. "Heads, we play tag.
> Tails, we play with the toy trucks," Zayn says. The coin lands on tails. Zayn grins big
> and runs to get the toy box. His friend Roy joins in, and soon the whole class
> is enjoying the game. By the end of recess, Zayn's voice is quite tired from cheering.

*Questions:* (1) What does Zayn's teacher ask him to do? (2) How do Zayn and his friends
choose a game? (3) Why might Zayn's voice be tired at the end?

### Track B — "A Choice at Work"
> Tariq works at a shop that fixes engines. Today, he must make a choice: fix an old engine's
> oil leak, or help a customer who needs a new coin battery for her car key. His boss, Mr. Roy,
> tells him to help the customer first. Tariq checks the car key, finds the right coin battery,
> and fits it in. "That should not annoy you again," he tells her. Then Tariq turns to the oil
> leak — it takes him a good while to find the moist patch under the engine and seal the
> joint. By the end of the day, Tariq feels the joy of a job done well, even when his voice
> is tired from talking to so many customers.

*Questions:* (1) What choice does Tariq have to make? (2) What does he fix for the customer
first? (3) Why does Tariq feel joy at the end of the day?

## Fluency check (2–3 min, every lesson)
Have the learner read Track A or Track B aloud once, cold (no rehearsal first). Time it and
count **WCPM** (words correct per minute): count total words read in one minute, subtract
errors not self-corrected. Note one **prosody** observation too — did they pause at commas
and periods, use a questioning tone on "?", change their voice for dialogue in quotes? Log
both numbers in the learner's own tracker. At this level, texts are short and heavily
controlled, so WCPM will stay fairly flat lesson to lesson — that is expected. The target is
smooth, accurate, expressive reading, never raw speed (assisted-practice model, not a race).

## 8. Listen & Talk (5 min)
Read aloud: "When people work well together and things go smoothly, we sometimes say the day
felt **harmonious** — everything and everyone fitting together without friction." **Tier-2
word: harmonious** — friendly definition: "peaceful and working well together, without
fighting or friction." Example 1: "The classroom was harmonious once everyone knew the
rules." Example 2: "A harmonious team gets more done with less stress." Your turn: "What
would make your classroom or workplace feel more harmonious?"
*Discuss:* Think of a day that felt harmonious for you. What made it that way?

## 9. Check (2 min)
Real words: *oil, point, coin, boy, enjoy.*
Pseudowords: *floip, scoy, moin, throy, zoint.*
Dictate: *"join."*
**≥90%? → 4.6. Below 90%?** Reteach step 3's positional rule with a sorting task: give 6
words (3 oi, 3 oy) cut into strips, have the learner sort them by "middle of word" vs. "end
of word" before re-blending.

## Tutor notes
- **Common error:** spelling an end-of-word /oi/ sound as "oi" instead of "oy" (writing "toi"
  for *toy*). **Correction:** always ask "is this sound in the middle or at the end?" before
  choosing a spelling during the Spell it step.
- **Common error:** blending oi/oy as two separate sounds instead of one glide. **Correction:**
  model sliding your voice smoothly through the sound rather than stopping between "o" and
  "i."
- **For adults:** Track B keeps the register workplace-appropriate (engines, coin batteries,
  customers) — swap in whatever trade is locally relevant (tailor, electrician, mechanic).
- **For young kids:** the tag/toy-truck game is a natural moment for a real coin-toss demo if
  a coin is available — optional multisensory tie-in, not required.
- **Never say:** "it just sounds like toy" without pointing back to the position-in-word rule
  — the goal is a transferable rule, not memorizing single words.
