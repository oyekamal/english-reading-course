# L4.14 — The Word-Attack Routine

**Goal:** Put every syllable-type, syllable-division, and morphology skill from Level 4
together into one repeatable routine for decoding ANY long, unfamiliar word — real or made-up.
**New today:** the five-step word-attack routine (mark → peel → chunk → blend → flex)
**Review:** all six syllable types, syllable division + schwa, suffix spelling rules, and
-tion/-sion/-ture/-ous/-cial-tial
**Heart words:** none new
**Time:** 30–35 min — this lesson is the payoff of the whole level; give it room
**Materials:** paper, pencil; optional highlighter for marking vowels

## 1. Warm-up review (2–3 min)
Read 5 review words: *running, happier, hoping, carried, stopped.* Name the six syllable
types and the three ways a VCV word can split.

## 2. Hear it (2 min)
Show the learner a genuinely long word cold, with no support: **unfriendliness**. Ask: "What
would you do if you saw this in a book and nobody was there to help?" Let them try, then say:
"By the end of today, you'll have an exact five-step plan for exactly this moment."

## 3. Meet it (3 min) — The Word-Attack Routine
Teach these five steps, in order, every time:

1. **Mark the vowels.** Underline or circle every vowel letter (including teams like ea, oa)
   in the word — this shows roughly how many syllables it has.
2. **Peel off prefixes and suffixes.** Look for a recognizable chunk at the front (*un-, re-,
   dis-, mis-, non-, pre-*) or the end (*-ing, -ed, -er, -ly, -ful, -tion, -ous*, etc.) and
   set it aside first. What's left in the middle is the base word — often much shorter and
   more familiar.
3. **Chunk the rest by syllable type.** Use the six syllable types and the VC/CV, V/CV, VC/V
   division patterns from 4.12 to break the base word into readable pieces.
4. **Blend the chunks together**, left to right.
5. **Flex it: adjust for stress and schwa, then check it's a real word (or a name/place you
   recognize).** Say it a couple of ways if the first attempt doesn't sound like a real word —
   try shifting which syllable is stressed, or softening an unstressed vowel to schwa. **If
   that still doesn't land, try the all-schwa read-through as a last resort:** keep your best
   guess at which syllable is stressed, but flatten every *other* (unstressed) vowel in the
   word to a lazy "uh," ignoring its "textbook" sound entirely, and listen for whether the
   resulting sound-shape matches a word you already know *by ear* (this is often faster than
   re-guessing individual vowels one at a time, because it matches how the word actually
   sounds in fast, natural speech — fluent speakers genuinely reduce unstressed vowels to
   schwa; they don't fully pronounce every vowel's "textbook" sound). Worked example:
   **animal**, chunked and blended carefully as an-i-mal, might come out stiff on a first
   pass — read it through instead with only the first syllable stressed and every other vowel
   flattened: "AN-uh-muhl" — and the familiar spoken word "animal" jumps right out, because
   that really is how a fluent speaker says it (/ˈæn.ə.məl/ — the second and third syllables
   are genuinely schwa in natural speech, unlike a word such as *complicate*, whose final
   syllable keeps a full, unreduced vowel — /ˈkɒm.plɪ.keɪt/ — so flattening it would produce a
   sound-shape a fluent speaker would never actually make).

Model the full routine once, out loud, on **unfriendliness**. First, mark the vowels: **u n f
r i e n d l i n e s s** — five vowel letters (u, i, e, i, e), giving a rough sense of the
word's shape before any splitting happens. Peel **un-** (prefix) and **-ness** (suffix) →
left with **friendli**. Recognize **friend** inside it — this is already a taught heart word
(Level 2), so its "ie" spelling for the short-e sound doesn't need decoding, just
recognition — plus the **y-to-i** change before a suffix (4.13's rule: *friendly → friendli-*
before adding *-ness*). Chunk: **friend** (heart word) + **li** (open syllable). Blend:
friend-li-ness. Flex: stress falls on "friend," the rest stays light. Check: real word, "the
state of not being friendly." **Urdu-speaker tip:** this routine works the same regardless of
L1 — the steps are about English's spelling structure, not about English fluency, so trust
the process even on totally unfamiliar words.

## 4. Blend it (5 min)
Apply all five steps to each word, saying the steps aloud the first few times:

Real words: reporting, unfriendly, misplaced, nonstop, rewriting, unbuttoned, disconnected,
reconsider, unhappiest, restarting.

Pseudowords (the real test — no dictionary can rescue you here, only the routine):
contramble, fenscription, unpladding, disflorted, remistion.

## 5. Spell it (4 min)
1. Say a long word slowly, syllable by syllable, having the learner write each chunk:
   *disconnected* → dis / con / nect / ed.
2. Words: *reporting, unhappiest.*
3. Sentence: **"She was reconsidering the unfriendly, nonstop noise from the disconnected
   speaker."**

## 6. Heart word(s) (2 min)
No new heart word today. Use the time to run the word-attack routine on one heart word that
is now mostly decodable: *because* (be + cause — "cause" uses the au pattern from 4.7! Mostly
regular now, with only the "be" vs. expected spelling as a tiny quirk).

## 7. Read it (5–8 min)

### Track A — "The Unhappiest Robot"
> In her story, Amina wrote about a robot named Blip who was the unhappiest robot in the whole
> factory. Blip kept restarting himself, hoping a reboot would fix his mood, but nothing
> changed. One day, an engineer noticed Blip's wiring was disconnected in one small spot. After
> reconnecting it, Blip stopped feeling unfriendly toward the other robots and started
> reporting for his shift with a smile. "Sometimes the unhappiest robot just needs the right
> person to notice the disconnection," Amina's teacher said when she read the story aloud.
> Amina's classmates loved it, especially the part where Blip went from unfriendly to the
> friendliest robot in the whole story.

*Questions:* (1) Why was Blip the unhappiest robot? (2) What was actually wrong with him?
(3) How did Blip change after the engineer fixed him?

### Track B — "Reconnecting the System"
> At the office, the internet has been nonstop unreliable all morning. Farah, the IT support
> worker, starts by reconsidering the whole network setup instead of just restarting each
> computer one by one. She finds that a cable was quietly disconnected behind a desk — someone
> had bumped it while unpacking a delivery. After reconnecting it, she spends an hour
> retesting every computer, reporting her findings to her manager as she goes. "This was more
> than a quick fix," she tells her manager, "it needed real troubleshooting, not just
> restarting things and hoping." Her manager thanks her for being so thorough, and by the
> afternoon, the whole office is back online, no longer disconnected.

*Questions:* (1) What problem does Farah investigate? (2) What does she find caused it?
(3) What does Farah say was needed instead of "just restarting things and hoping"?

## Fluency check (2–3 min, every lesson)
Have the learner read Track A or Track B aloud once, cold (no rehearsal first). Time it and
count **WCPM** (words correct per minute): count total words read in one minute, subtract
errors not self-corrected. Note one **prosody** observation too — did they pause at commas
and periods, use a questioning tone on "?", change their voice for dialogue in quotes? Log
both numbers in the learner's own tracker. At this level, texts are short and heavily
controlled, so WCPM will stay fairly flat lesson to lesson — that is expected. The target is
smooth, accurate, expressive reading, never raw speed (assisted-practice model, not a race).

## 8. Listen & Talk (5 min)
Read aloud: "When someone works through a problem methodically instead of guessing, checking
each possible cause one at a time, we call that **troubleshooting** — a careful, step-by-step
way of finding and fixing what's wrong." **Tier-2 word: troubleshooting** — friendly
definition: "solving a problem by checking possible causes one at a time, in order, instead
of guessing." Example 1: "Troubleshooting the car took an hour, but he found the real
problem." Example 2: "Good troubleshooting means not skipping steps." Your turn: "Does the
word-attack routine you learned today remind you of troubleshooting? How?"
*Discuss:* Why might "guess and hope" usually take longer than a real troubleshooting method,
even though it feels faster at first?

## 9. Check (2 min)
Real words: *reporting, unfriendly, disconnected, reconsider, unhappiest* (learner must name
at least the peel and chunk steps aloud for two of these).
Pseudowords: *contramble, fenscription, unpladding, disflorted, remistion.*
Dictate: *"restarting."*
**≥90%? → 4.15. Below 90%?** Slow the routine down to one step at a time across a whole
session: do only "mark vowels" on 10 words one day, only "peel affixes" on 10 words the next,
before recombining the full five-step routine.

## Tutor notes
- **Common error:** skipping straight to blending without peeling affixes first, leading to
  a much longer, harder chunk to sound out. **Correction:** physically cover the prefix and
  suffix with fingers or sticky notes so only the base word shows, forcing the peel step.
- **Common error:** accepting a made-up "close enough" reading without the final flex/check
  step. **Correction:** always ask explicitly, "is that a real word, or a name/place you
  know? If not, what could you adjust?"
- **For adults:** the troubleshooting metaphor in Listen & Talk is deliberately chosen to
  connect directly to workplace problem-solving — invite the learner to compare the reading
  routine to a real troubleshooting task they've done at work.
- **For young kids:** the robot story keeps the tone playful, but hold the full five-step
  routine seriously — this is not a moment to let a child guess-and-move-on; every long word
  gets the routine.
- **Never say:** "just try to remember what the word looks like" — the whole point of Level 4
  is that no word, however long, needs to be memorized whole; the routine always works.

## Decodability audit (Track A + Track B texts)
Graphemes/patterns used beyond today's **word-attack routine** (a skill, not new graphemes):
all single letters; sh, ch, th, ng; blends (bl, cl, dr, cr, gr, sp, st, tr, sk, sw); -s/-es,
-ed, -ing; VCe (i_e as in *wire→wiring, like→not used*); open syllables; ee (*need→not used,
week→not used*); consonant-le (*little→not used, whole→VCe not le, check: "whole" is w+h+o_e,
VCe with silent h, an irregular spelling — flagged below*); -tion/-ous (*disconnection→not
used directly, reporting uses -tion family root "report"*); syllable division + schwa
(pervasive, per this lesson's routine); prefixes **un-, re-, dis-, non-** (new functional
vocabulary for the peel step, not formally taught as a full list until Level 5's 20 prefixes,
but used here as practical peel targets). Heart words used: *the, is, to, and, was, her,
said, do, you, they, are, have, because* (Level 1–3, previously taught; *because* revisited
above). Content words: *Amina, wrote, story, robot, named, Blip, whole, factory, kept, fix,
mood, nothing, changed, one, day, engineer, noticed, wiring, small, spot, after, stopped,
feeling, other, robots, started, shift, smile, sometimes, needs, right, person, notice,
