# L4.8 — Bread and Silent Letters

**Goal:** Read and spell words with **ea saying /ĕ/** (as in *bread*), and four silent-letter
patterns: **kn, wr, gn, mb**.
**New today:** ea (/ĕ/), kn, wr, gn, mb
**Review:** aw, au, al(l), and all vowel-r teams and diphthongs
**Heart words:** none new — review *great, busy*
**Time:** 30 min (two related but distinct topics — go at a comfortable pace, split into two
sittings if needed)
**Materials:** paper, pencil

## 1. Warm-up review (2–3 min)
Read 5 review words: *saw, ball, pause, small, wall.* Flash sounds: aw, au, al.

## 2. Hear it — part 1 (2 min)
Say *bread*. Ask: "What vowel sound do you hear?" (/ĕ/, like in *bed*.) Show the learner it's
spelled **ea** — the same team they know for the /ē/ sound in *eat*, but here it says
something different. "Ea is a two-faced team — it usually says /ē/, but sometimes says /ĕ/.
Try it the way you know first; if it's not a real word, try the short e sound instead."

## 3. Meet it — part 1 (3 min)
Show **ea** words for /ĕ/: *bread, head, dead, instead, ready, heavy, weather, feather,
thread, spread, meant, health, wealth, breath, threat, healthy.* Contrast side by side with
known /ē/ ea-words (*eat, read, meat*) so the learner sees both faces of the same spelling.

## 2b. Hear it — part 2 (2 min)
Say *know*. Ask: "Do you hear a /k/ sound at the start?" (No — just /n/.) Say *write*: "Do
you hear a /w/?" (No — just /r/.) Say *sign*: "Do you hear a /g/?" (No — just /n/.) Say
*comb*: "Do you hear a /b/?" (No — just /m/.) Four letters — k, w, g, b — go silent in these
specific spots.

## 3b. Meet it — part 2 (3 min)
Show all four patterns: **kn** (silent k, at the start: *know, knee, knock*), **wr** (silent
w, at the start: *write, wrist, wrong*), **gn** (silent g, at the start or end: *gnat, sign*),
**mb** (silent b, at the end: *comb, lamb, climb*). "These letters used to be pronounced
hundreds of years ago — English kept the old spelling but the sound quietly dropped away."
**Urdu-speaker tip:** since Urdu spelling closely follows pronunciation, silent letters can
feel strange — remind the learner this is a spelling quirk of English history, not something
they're doing wrong by finding it odd.

## 4. Blend it (5 min)
ea (/ĕ/): bread, head, dead, instead, ready, heavy, weather, feather, thread, spread, meant,
health, wealth, breath, threat, healthy.
kn: know, knee, knock, knot, knit, kneel, knuckle.
wr: write, wrist, wrap, wrong, wreck, wrench, wrinkle.
gn: gnat, gnaw, sign, design.
mb: comb, lamb, climb, thumb, crumb, numb, limb, bomb.

Pseudowords: thead, knop, wrab, gnol, vomb.

## 5. Spell it (4 min)
1. Sounds: /h/ /ĕ/ /d/ → *head* (tutor: point out the ea spelling choice here, not e alone —
   flag as a spelling-only decision the learner must simply remember for this word).
   /n/ /oa/(no, /n/ /oh/→*know*, spelled with silent k) → *know*. /r/ /i/ /t/ → *write.*
2. Words: *bread, wrist, sign.*
3. Sentence: **"I know it is wrong to spread bad news."**

## 6. Heart word(s) (2 min)
Review **great**: point out its ea says yet a third sound, /ā/ — flag this as a true
exception (not today's regular /ĕ/ pattern), so the learner doesn't over-apply today's rule
to every unfamiliar ea word. Review **busy**: unrelated pattern, quick refresh only.

## 7. Read it (5–8 min)

### Track A — "Ready for Bad Weather"
> "Get ready — the weather is turning," Dad said, looking at the heavy gray clouds. Sana ran
> to get her coat. She had a small cut on her knee from playing outside, so Dad helped clean it
> first. "Don't rub it with dirty hands," he said, wrapping a bandage around her knee. Sana
> took a deep breath and looked out the window. Wind began to shake the thread-thin branches of
> the old tree. "I meant to bring in the washing before this," Mom said, dashing outside. By
> the time she got back, her hair was a mess, but the washing was safe and dry. That night, the
> family sat close, listening to the storm, glad to be warm and healthy inside.

*Questions:* (1) What does Sana hurt, and what does Dad do about it? (2) What does Mom rush
outside to do? (3) How does the family spend the storm?

### Track B — "A Wrong Turn and a Wrist Injury"
> Rashid was driving to a job site when he took a wrong turn onto a bumpy road. His truck hit
> a deep rut, and his wrist twisted hard against the wheel. "That's not good," he said, feeling
> a sharp pain. He pulled over, took a breath, and checked his wrist — it was already starting
> to swell. Rashid called his boss to explain he would be late. At the clinic, a nurse wrapped
> his wrist and told him to rest it. "You're lucky it's not broken," she said. Rashid signed
> the form, thanked her, and called his boss again with an update. It was a hard start to the
> day, but he stayed calm and got past it.

*Questions:* (1) What happens to Rashid's wrist? (2) What does he do right after it happens?
(3) What does the nurse tell him at the clinic?

## Fluency check (2–3 min, every lesson)
Have the learner read Track A or Track B aloud once, cold (no rehearsal first). Time it and
count **WCPM** (words correct per minute): count total words read in one minute, subtract
errors not self-corrected. Note one **prosody** observation too — did they pause at commas
and periods, use a questioning tone on "?", change their voice for dialogue in quotes? Log
both numbers in the learner's own tracker. At this level, texts are short and heavily
controlled, so WCPM will stay fairly flat lesson to lesson — that is expected. The target is
smooth, accurate, expressive reading, never raw speed (assisted-practice model, not a race).

## 8. Listen & Talk (5 min)
Read aloud: "When someone stays calm and keeps making good choices even after something goes
wrong, we can say they showed **composure** — a steady, level-headed response instead of
panic." **Tier-2 word: composure** — friendly definition: "staying calm and thinking clearly,
even when something unexpected or painful happens." Example 1: "She kept her composure even
after dropping her phone in the water." Example 2: "The pilot's composure during the storm
kept everyone calm." Your turn: "Think of a time something went wrong — did you keep your
composure, or was it hard?"
*Discuss:* Why might staying calm actually help you heal or fix a problem faster?

## 9. Check (2 min)
Real words: *bread, know, write, sign, comb.*
Pseudowords: *thead, knop, wrab, gnol, vomb.*
Dictate: *"wrist."*
**≥90%? → 4.9. Below 90%?** Split reteach: if ea/ĕ errors dominate, redo step 3 side-by-side
contrast with known ea/ē words; if silent-letter errors dominate, redo step 3b one pattern at
a time (kn only, then wr only) before combining.

## Tutor notes
- **Common error:** applying today's /ĕ/ reading to every unfamiliar ea word, including ones
  that are actually /ē/ (over-correction). **Correction:** always teach "try /ē/ first — it's
  the more common one — then try /ĕ/ if that's not a real word," never the reverse default.
- **Common error:** trying to sound out the silent letter (saying "k-now" for *know*).
  **Correction:** model the whole cluster as "silent + real sound" — kn always drops the k,
  no exceptions, so there's no need to guess here, just remember the four patterns.
- **For adults:** Track B's workplace-injury scenario is realistic and dignified; this is
  also a good moment to note that words like *sign* (as on a form) appear constantly in
  adult paperwork, which is a strong, practical reason to nail this pattern.
- **For young kids:** the "why did English keep the silent letter?" question is genuinely
  interesting to many kids — a brief, honest "it's from history" answer satisfies curiosity
  without inventing a false rule.
- **Never say:** "English is just weird" as the final word — that's true but not helpful;
  always follow with the concrete rule (kn=silent k, wr=silent w, gn=silent g, mb=silent b)
  so the learner has something to apply, not just something to shrug about.
