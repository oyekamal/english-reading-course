# L4.12 — Breaking Words Apart

**Goal:** Learn to divide two-syllable words into chunks using three patterns — **VC/CV**,
**V/CV**, **VC/V** — as a flexible first-attempt strategy (not a fixed rule), and meet
**schwa**, the "uh" sound that shows up in unstressed syllables.
**New today:** syllable division (VC/CV, V/CV, VC/V — the "flex rule"), schwa
**Review:** all six syllable types (closed, open, VCe, vowel team, r-controlled,
consonant-le) and today's building blocks: -tion/-sion/-ture/-ous/-cial-tial
**Heart words:** **money** becomes decodable-with-schwa; **people** re-explained
**Time:** 30 min — this is the hinge lesson of Level 4; do not rush it
**Materials:** paper, pencil; optional index cards to physically split words

## 1. Warm-up review (2–3 min)
Read 5 review words: *nation, vision, picture, famous, special.* Name the six syllable
types from memory if the learner can.

## 2. Hear it (2 min)
Say *napkin*. Clap it: nap-kin. Say *tiger*. Clap it: ti-ger. Say *robin*. Clap it: rob-in
(short o) — then say *cabin*: cab-in (also short a, but looks the same on paper as *robin*
before you check it). Ask: "These three words all have a vowel-consonant-vowel pattern in
the middle, but do they split the same way?" (No — this is today's whole point.)

## 3. Meet it (3 min)

### The physical marking procedure (do this on paper, every time, before dividing anything)
Before you try to split a word, **mark it first** — this gives you a repeatable, visual
routine instead of guessing where to break it:
1. Write a small **v** above every vowel *sound* — a vowel team (ai, ea, oa, igh...) counts
   as **one** v with a small arc/bridge drawn under the two letters, since it's one sound.
2. Write a small **c** above every consonant letter between the vowels.
3. Now *look* at the marked pattern — VCCV, VCV, VCCCV, or two v's with **no** bridge between
   them — and use the matching case below to decide where the syllable break goes.
Example, marked: **n a p k i n** → v-c-c-v (nap|kin, VC/CV — split between the two
consonants). **t i g e r** → v-c-v (ti|ger, V/CV — one consonant, try open first).

### Four cases (not three — two more the marking procedure reveals)
- **VC/CV** — two consonants marked between the vowels: split between them. First syllable
  closed, short vowel. *nap-kin, rab-bit, mag-net.* This one is the most reliable.
- **V/CV** — one consonant marked, first syllable **open** (long vowel), try this first:
  *ti-ger, pa-per, si-lent, ro-bot.*
- **VC/V** — same one-consonant pattern, but the first syllable is **closed** (short vowel)
  instead: *cab-in, riv-er, sev-en, wag-on.*
- **VCCCV — three consonants marked.** Two of them are almost always a taught blend or
  digraph that stays glued together as a single unit; split off the third. Compounds are the
  clearest version of this: mark **rainbow** as v-c-v-c-c-v — but don't split blindly by the
  marks alone here, split at the **compound boundary** you can already hear: rain-bow,
  hand-spring, sun-set. For a non-compound VCCCV word like **instruct**, the "str" blend
  (already taught, L2.8) stays together: in-struct.
- **VV — two vowel letters side by side with NO bridge between them, because they are NOT a
  taught vowel team.** Each vowel keeps its own full sound in its own syllable: **di-et,
  po-em, li-on, cre-ate.** This is the case that catches learners who over-apply "two vowels
  together = one team" (true for ai/ea/oa, false here) — always check: *is this spelling one
  of my taught vowel teams? If not, and I can hear two separate vowel sounds, there's no
  bridge — split right between them.*
Say clearly: **"When there's just one consonant in the middle (V/CV or VC/V), you can't tell
which way it splits just by looking. Try the open way first. If that's not a real or
recognizable word, try the closed way. Whichever one makes a real word — or a name you
recognize — is the answer."** This is a flexible first-attempt strategy, not a guaranteed
rule — real research shows this exact pattern predicts the split correctly only about half
the time, so "try both, check which is real" is the actual skill, not memorizing "VCV always
splits this way."
Then introduce **schwa**: "In many two- or three-syllable words, one syllable isn't
stressed — it gets said quickly and lazily, and almost any vowel letter in that spot ends up
sounding like 'uh.' That 'uh' sound is called schwa." Show *banana*: /bə-NA-nə/ — first and
last syllables are schwa, only the middle is stressed and clear. Show *pencil*: /PEN-səl/ —
second syllable is schwa.

## 4. Blend it (5 min)
VC/CV: napkin, rabbit, sudden, magnet, picnic, muffin, kitten, basket, helmet, mitten.
V/CV (try open first): music, robot, tiger, paper, moment, spider, silent, pilot, focus,
human, even, item.
VC/V (open fails, so flex to closed): cabin, river, seven, shiver, wagon, closet, planet,
punish, finish, visit, credit, **robin — THE flex example**: try open first (ro-bin with long
o, to rhyme with *robot*) — that is not a real word, so flex to closed (rob-in with short o,
"ROB-in," rhyming with *bobbin*) — that IS the real word. Contrast directly with **robot**,
which looks like the exact same v-c-v shape but stays open (ro-bot, long o): same letters,
same marked pattern, two different real answers — this is the single clearest proof in the
whole lesson that the open/closed choice can't be read off the spelling alone and must be
checked against a real word every time.
VCCCV / compound boundary: rainbow, sunset, handspring, instruct, complain (com-plain: "pl"
blend stays together, not a compound but the same "keep the blend, split off the rest" move).
VV (no team, two full vowel sounds): diet, poem, lion, create, quiet, science, giant.
Schwa practice (notice the "uh"): banana, pencil, camera, gallon, method, problem.

Pseudowords: naptin, tazil (try open: /tay-zil/? then closed: /taz-il/ — either counts as
correct if the learner explains their flex reasoning), gorlin, plemat, thindstruck (VCCCV —
practice keeping a taught blend together and splitting off the rest), zoem (VV — no team,
say both vowels separately: /zoh-em/).

## 5. Spell it (4 min)
1. Clap and mark: *napkin* → nap / kin (VC/CV). *paper* → pa / per (V/CV). *cabin* → cab / in
   (VC/V). *rainbow* → rain / bow (compound boundary). *poem* → po / em (VV, no bridge).
2. Words: *rabbit, spider, seven, diet.*
3. Sentence: **"The spider hid in the small basket."**

## 6. Heart word(s) (2 min)
Revisit **money**: /m/ /ʌ/ /n/ /ee/ — the first syllable is stressed ("MUH-nee"), so its
vowel isn't schwa (schwa is specifically the reduced sound in an *unstressed* syllable) — it's
a short "uh" sound (the same vowel as in *cup, come, love*) spelled with "o," which is simply
an irregular spelling to remember, same family of quirk as schwa but not schwa itself. Revisit
**people**: /p/ /ee/ /p/ /ə/ (schwa) /l/ — the unstressed second syllable's vowel genuinely is
schwa this time. Both words are now fully explainable, not random exceptions — but only
*people* is a true schwa example; *money*'s irregular vowel is a separate, simpler kind of
spelling quirk.

## 7. Read it (5–8 min)

### Track A — "The Spider in the Basket"
> Layla found a small spider in her picnic basket. "Don't be scared," her mother said, "it's
> just a garden spider — it won't hurt you." Layla watched the spider a moment, then gently
> tipped the basket so it could climb onto a napkin and off into the garden. "That spider has
> seven legs left — maybe it lost one," Layla noticed. Her mother explained that spiders often
> have eight legs, but this one must have had an accident. At the picnic, Layla ate a muffin
> and told everyone about the seven-legged spider. "People at school won't believe this," she
> laughed, already planning how she'd tell the story tomorrow.

*Questions:* (1) Where does Layla find the spider? (2) What does she notice about its legs?
(3) Why does Layla think people at school won't believe her story?

### Track B — "A Visit to the River Cabin"
> Rukhsana and her husband rent a small cabin by the river for the weekend. It is a simple
> place — one room, a basket for firewood, and a helmet hanging by the door for the rental
> kayak. On the first evening, they spot a robin building a nest near the window. "That's a
> good sign," Rukhsana's husband says. "A robin near your cabin means good luck, my mother used
> to say." They spend the visit reading, cooking simple meals, and watching the river. Before
> they leave, Rukhsana notices the robin has finished its nest. "I hope it's safe here," she
> says, already planning to visit again — maybe with enough money saved for a full week next
> time.

*Questions:* (1) Where do Rukhsana and her husband stay? (2) What does the husband's mother
say about a robin near a cabin? (3) What does Rukhsana hope for the robin?

## Fluency check (2–3 min, every lesson)
Have the learner read Track A or Track B aloud once, cold (no rehearsal first). Time it and
count **WCPM** (words correct per minute): count total words read in one minute, subtract
errors not self-corrected. Note one **prosody** observation too — did they pause at commas
and periods, use a questioning tone on "?", change their voice for dialogue in quotes? Log
both numbers in the learner's own tracker. At this level, texts are short and heavily
controlled, so WCPM will stay fairly flat lesson to lesson — that is expected. The target is
smooth, accurate, expressive reading, never raw speed (assisted-practice model, not a race).

## 8. Listen & Talk (5 min)
Read aloud: "Some people believe small signs from nature — a bird building a nest, a certain
season's first rain — carry meaning. Scientists call this kind of belief **folklore**:
traditional stories and beliefs passed down between people, not proven by evidence but often
carrying real wisdom about nature." **Tier-2 word: folklore** — friendly definition:
"traditional stories, beliefs, and sayings passed down through a community, often about
nature or luck." Example 1: "It's folklore that a robin's nest brings good luck." Example 2:
"Every culture has its own folklore about the weather." Your turn: "Do you know any folklore
from your own family or community?"
*Discuss:* Why might people keep believing folklore even without scientific proof? Is that a
bad thing?

## 9. Check (2 min)
Real words: *napkin, spider, cabin, rainbow, diet* (say the schwa syllable correctly in
*napkin*'s second syllable if the learner reaches for it).
Pseudowords: *naptin, tazil, gorlin, plemat, zoem* (VV — accept either flex reading with
correct reasoning stated aloud for tazil; zoem must be read as two separate vowel sounds,
not a team).
Dictate: *"basket."*
**≥90%? → 4.13. Below 90%?** This is the hinge lesson — if the flex concept itself (not just
individual words) isn't landing, slow all the way down: use 8 word cards (2 VC/CV, 2 V/CV, 2
VC/V, 1 compound/VCCCV, 1 VV), physically mark vowels/consonants and split each with scissors
or a drawn line, and have the learner say both possible readings out loud before picking the
real one, for every card that has a genuine open/closed choice (VC/CV, compound, and VV cases
only have one correct split — don't ask the learner to "flex" those, only the true V/CV vs.
VC/V ambiguity).

## Tutor notes
- **Common error:** trying to apply one rule to every word instead of flexing. **Correction:**
  the exact phrase to repeat is "try it, check it, flex it" — never present syllable division
  as something with one right first guess. This applies only to the true V/CV vs. VC/V
  ambiguity — VC/CV, VCCCV/compound, and VV all have one correct split once marked correctly,
  so don't invite unnecessary flexing on those.
- **Common error:** over-applying "two vowels together = one vowel team" from Level 3 and
  mis-splitting every VV word (e.g., trying to read *diet* as one long vowel sound, or *lion*
  as rhyming with a vowel-team word). **Correction:** this is exactly why the physical
  marking procedure matters — if the learner can't name the two letters as one of their
  *taught* vowel teams (ai, ea, oa, ee, igh, ie, ue, ew, ui, oo), there is no bridge, and each
  vowel gets its own syllable. Note for the record: Level 4's very first lesson (4.01)
  originally used the word *quiet* in a story before this exception was taught — it was
  removed there and the concept is taught properly here instead (GAUNTLET.md Round 1 critic).
- **Common error:** skipping the physical marking step and trying to split "in your head."
  **Correction:** for at least the first 10 words, insist on writing the v's and c's out —
  the routine is the point, not just the final answer; UFLI-style mark-then-divide gives a
  learner something concrete to fall back on when a word doesn't come apart easily by ear.
- **Common error:** ignoring schwa and trying to sound out an unstressed syllable with its
  "textbook" vowel sound (e.g., saying *pencil* as "PEN-SIL" with a hard i). **Correction:**
  model the fast, lazy "uh" explicitly, and note that spelling still matters even when the
  sound blurs to schwa — this connects forward to spelling rules in 4.13.
- **For adults:** Track B's cabin/folklore framing works well for adult learners with rural or
  family folklore backgrounds — invite the learner to share their own family's sayings, which
  builds both engagement and real oral vocabulary.
- **For young kids:** the "does it lose points if I said it the 'wrong' way first?" worry is
  common — reassure explicitly that trying both readings and checking is the whole skill, not
  a mistake to avoid.
- **Never say:** "you should have known" if a flex-guess doesn't land on the first try —
  the entire point of this lesson is that even skilled readers try both and check.
