# L4.7 — Paw, Pause, and the Mall

**Goal:** Read and spell words with **aw, au, al(l)** — all spelling the same /aw/ sound.
**New today:** aw, au, al(l)
**Review:** ou, ow(/ow/), oi, oy, and all vowel-r teams
**Heart words:** none new — review *walk, talk*
**Time:** 25 min
**Materials:** paper, pencil

## 1. Warm-up review (2–3 min)
Read 5 review words: *loud, town, house, cow, sound.* Flash sounds: ou, ow(/ow/), oi, oy.

## 2. Hear it (2 min)
Say *paw*. Isolate: /p/ /aw/ — mouth wide open, jaw dropped, no glide (unlike oi/ou — this is
a single steady vowel sound, not a diphthong). Say *ball*: /b/ /aw/ /l/. Same vowel sound.

## 3. Meet it (3 min)
Show all three spellings: **aw** (*saw, paw, draw*), **au** (*haul, sauce, pause*), and **al**
followed by a double l or another consonant (*ball, all, walk*). Rule of thumb: **aw** often
ends a word or comes before n (*lawn*); **au** is usually in the middle; **al** appears when
the /aw/ sound is followed by "l." Already-known heart words *walk* and *talk* fit this al
pattern too — point out they are no longer fully irregular now that al is taught, only the
silent "l" pronunciation quirk in *walk/talk* (the l is silent) remains the true heart part.
**Urdu-speaker tip:** this /aw/ sound is close to the vowel in "آ" — a steady, open, back
sound; don't let it drift toward the short-o sound in *hot*.

## 4. Blend it (5 min)
aw: saw, paw, jaw, law, raw, claw, draw, straw, lawn, dawn, crawl, yawn, hawk, shawl.
au: haul, fault, sauce, launch, pause, cause.
al(l): all, ball, call, fall, hall, mall, small, tall, wall, stall, also, always, almost.

Pseudowords: fraw, staul, glall, thraw, scall.

## 5. Spell it (4 min)
1. Sounds: /s/ /aw/ → *saw*. /p/ /aw/ /z/ → *pause* (tutor: point out the middle-of-word au
   spelling here). /f/ /aw/ /l/ → *fall.*
2. Words: *lawn, cause, small.*
3. Sentence: **"We saw a small hawk pause on the wall."**

## 6. Heart word(s) (2 min)
Review **walk** and **talk**: mark the silent "l" as the true heart part — the *al* spelling
for /aw/ is now fully regular and explainable; only the missing /l/ sound remains irregular.

## 7. Read it (5–8 min)

### Track A — "The Hawk at Dawn"
> At dawn, Zara and her granddad walk to the small lawn behind their house. A hawk lands on the
> old wall, its claw gripping the stone. "Don't move, or you'll cause it to fly off," her granddad
> whispers. Zara stands tall and still. The hawk turns its head, gives a small yawn, and looks
> right at her. Then it spreads its wings and flies over the mall down the road. "That was
> worth the early start!" Zara says. On the walk back, they talk about hawks and how they
> hunt. Zara makes a quick drawing of the hawk in her notebook before her meal.

*Questions:* (1) Where do Zara and her granddad go at dawn? (2) What does the hawk do while
they watch? (3) What does Zara do before her meal?

### Track B — "A Pause at the Mall"
> Mariam works at a small shop inside the mall. Business is slow, so she and her coworker take
> a pause to talk. "I saw a hawk on my walk to work this morning," Mariam says. "That's rare
> for downtown," her coworker replies. At noon, a small fault in the mall's power system causes
> all the lights to flicker, and a loud alarm sounds. "Don't fall over anyone — stay calm and
> walk to the hall," a staff member calls out. After a short pause, the power comes back, and
> the alarm stops. "Well, that was not part of the plan," Mariam laughs, and they get back to
> work.

*Questions:* (1) What do Mariam and her coworker do during a slow moment? (2) What causes
the alarm to sound? (3) What does the staff member ask everyone to do?

## Fluency check (2–3 min, every lesson)
Have the learner read Track A or Track B aloud once, cold (no rehearsal first). Time it and
count **WCPM** (words correct per minute): count total words read in one minute, subtract
errors not self-corrected. Note one **prosody** observation too — did they pause at commas
and periods, use a questioning tone on "?", change their voice for dialogue in quotes? Log
both numbers in the learner's own tracker. At this level, texts are short and heavily
controlled, so WCPM will stay fairly flat lesson to lesson — that is expected. The target is
smooth, accurate, expressive reading, never raw speed (assisted-practice model, not a race).

## 8. Listen & Talk (5 min)
Read aloud: "In a busy public space, staff are trained to stay **composed** — calm and in
control of their actions and voice — even when something unexpected happens, because panic
spreads fast in a crowd." **Tier-2 word: composed** — friendly definition: "calm and in
control, not panicked, even in a stressful moment." Example 1: "The pilot stayed composed
during the storm." Example 2: "She looked composed even though she was nervous inside." Your
turn: "Can you think of someone who stays composed under pressure?"
*Discuss:* Why might staying composed help everyone else around you stay calm too?

## 9. Check (2 min)
Real words: *saw, ball, pause, small, wall.*
Pseudowords: *fraw, staul, glall, thraw, scall.*
Dictate: *"lawn."*
**≥90%? → 4.8. Below 90%?** Reteach step 3's three-spelling positions with a sort task
(end-of-word aw, middle-of-word au, before-l al), then re-blend the first 10 words of step 4.

## Tutor notes
- **Common error:** spelling middle-of-word /aw/ as "aw" instead of "au" (writing "sawce"
  for *sauce*). **Correction:** the position rule is a helpful heuristic, not a law — remind
  the learner it's a "usually," and some words must simply be learned by sight over time.
- **Common error:** turning the steady /aw/ vowel into a glide, like oi or ou. **Correction:**
  model holding the sound steady: "aaawww," no movement of the tongue partway through.
- **For adults:** Track B's mall/workplace setting keeps the register professional; a
  "power system fault" and "alarm" scenario is realistic and dignified.
- **For young kids:** the hawk-watching scene supports a real nature-observation discussion —
  a good bridge into Listen & Talk's "composed" vocabulary.
- **Never say:** "you'll just know which spelling" — always route back to the position rule
  first, and treat true exceptions as new heart-word-style items to note, not shrug off.
