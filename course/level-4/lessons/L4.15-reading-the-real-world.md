# L4.15 — Reading the Real World

**Goal:** Read the first fully authentic, non-controlled texts in the course — a real notice,
a recipe, and a short news paragraph — and learn the exact, no-guessing protocol for handling
a genuinely unknown word in real text.
**New today:** the off-ramp — authentic text + the "stop, don't guess" unknown-word protocol
**Review:** the full word-attack routine (4.14), all Level 4 patterns
**Heart words:** none new — this lesson uses whatever heart words naturally appear in
authentic text, flagged as met, not pre-taught
**Time:** 30–35 min
**Materials:** paper, pencil

## Why this lesson is different
Every text before this one in the whole course was written to be ≥95% decodable using only
what had been taught so far (DESIGN.md rule 2). That was deliberate — Share's self-teaching
hypothesis (research/01) shows that successful decoding attempts are how a reader builds a
permanent memory for new words, and decodable text maximizes the chance of a successful
attempt. But no learner can stay inside controlled text forever — evidence (Odo 2024) does
not support decodables much past this point, and real text (notices, recipes, news, forms,
messages) simply isn't spelling-controlled. From here on, texts will mix familiar patterns
with genuinely new words. That's not a mistake — it's the point.

## 1. Warm-up review (2–3 min)
Read 5 review words pulled from across Level 4: *chart, world, enjoy, ghost, picture.* Recite
the five-step word-attack routine from memory.

## 2. Hear it / Meet it (5 min) — The unknown-word protocol
Say plainly: "Starting today, you will meet words no one has taught you yet. Here is exactly
what to do — every time, no exceptions:"

1. **Stop.** Don't skip the word and don't guess from the picture, the shape of the word, or
   what "would make sense" in the sentence. (Rule 1, DESIGN.md — this is banned at every level.)
2. **Run the word-attack routine** from 4.14: mark vowels → peel affixes → chunk by syllable
   type → blend → flex for stress/schwa.
3. **Say the word out loud.** Does it sound like a real word you've heard, even if you don't
   know what it means?
4. **If yes** — keep reading. If the *meaning* is still unclear, finish the sentence, then
   decide: does the rest of the sentence make the meaning clear enough to continue, or do you
   need to look it up / ask someone?
5. **If no** — the routine didn't produce a real-sounding word — try it once more, flexing a
   different stress or vowel choice. Still no? It's fine to ask someone or look it up. That is
   not failure; decoding gets you to a pronunciation, not always to instant meaning, especially
   for a genuinely new word.
**The one banned move at every step: never guess a word from context, a picture, or its
first letter alone, and call that "reading" it.** Decode first, always — meaning-checking
only comes after a word is fully sounded out. **Urdu-speaker tip:** authentic English text
(notices, forms) often uses formal, Latinate vocabulary (*required, applicable, eligible*) —
these usually respond very well to the word-attack routine's peel-and-chunk steps, since they
are built from recognizable prefixes/suffixes, even when the base root is unfamiliar.

## 3. Practice the protocol (5 min)
Before reading the full texts, practice the protocol on 5 words in isolation, pulled from
today's texts: *notice, ingredients, temperature, according, residents.* For each: stop → run
the routine → say it aloud → check it sounds real → move on.

## 4. Read it — three real, unedited-in-spirit authentic texts (10–12 min)

### Text 1 — A real notice (Track A + B share this one — a notice is for everyone)
> **NOTICE TO ALL RESIDENTS**
> The water supply to this building will be shut off on Tuesday from 9:00 AM to 2:00 PM for
> scheduled maintenance. Please store enough water in advance. Residents on the top floor may
> experience lower water pressure for the rest of the week. If you have questions, contact
> the building office between 10:00 AM and 4:00 PM. We apologize for any inconvenience.

*Questions:* (1) What day and time will the water be off? (2) What should residents do
before then? (3) Who should you contact with questions, and when?

### Text 2 — A recipe (Track A: baking with a family member / Track B: cooking at home)
> **Simple Vegetable Soup**
> Ingredients: 2 onions, 3 carrots, 2 potatoes, 1 liter of water, salt, and black pepper.
> Chop the onions, carrots, and potatoes into small pieces. Heat a little oil in a large pot
> and cook the onions until soft. Add the carrots and potatoes, then pour in the water. Bring
> to a boil, then lower the heat and let it simmer for 25 minutes, until the vegetables are
> tender. Season with salt and pepper according to taste. Serve hot.

*Questions:* (1) List the five ingredients. (2) What do you do right after adding the water?
(3) How long does the soup simmer?

### Text 3 — A short news paragraph
> Local officials announced that a new health clinic will open in the district next month.
> The clinic will offer free checkups for children under five and will be staffed by two
> doctors and three nurses. Officials say the clinic is part of a wider plan to improve
> access to basic healthcare in areas outside the city center. Residents welcomed the news,
> though some asked when a similar clinic might open in neighboring areas.

*Questions:* (1) What will open next month, and where? (2) Who will the free checkups be for?
(3) What did some residents ask about?

## Fluency check (2–3 min)
Time the learner reading Text 2 (the recipe) aloud, cold. Count WCPM and note one prosody
observation (did they read the ingredient list as a list, pausing at each comma, rather than
running it together?). This is the first fluency check on genuinely authentic (not
fully-decodable-guaranteed) text — expect WCPM to dip slightly compared to Level 4's
controlled texts; that's expected and not a failure, since new, unpracticed vocabulary is
now in the mix.

## 5. Listen & Talk (5 min)
Read aloud: "A notice, a recipe, and a news report are three examples of **functional text**
— writing whose main job is to help you do something (know when the water's off, cook a
meal, understand a local event) rather than to tell a story." **Tier-2 word: functional** —
friendly definition: "designed to do a practical job, not just to entertain or tell a story."
Example 1: "A bus schedule is a functional text — it tells you when to be at the stop."
Example 2: "This recipe is functional: it tells you exactly what to do, step by step." Your
turn: "What other functional texts do you read or hear in a normal week?"
*Discuss:* Why might a notice or recipe use different, more direct language than a story?

## 6. Check (2 min)
Ask the learner to demonstrate the unknown-word protocol out loud on one unfamiliar word from
each text (their choice), narrating each step. **Pass criterion:** correctly narrates all
five steps of the protocol on at least 2 of 3 words, without guessing from context first.
**Below criterion?** Re-run the isolated-word practice (step 3) with 5 new words from a
different real text (a cereal box, a bus ticket, a form) before trying again.

## Tutor notes
- **Common error:** reverting to guessing from context now that text feels "real." **This is
  the single biggest risk in this lesson.** **Correction:** hold the line exactly as hard as
  in Level 1 — "stop, don't guess" applies to every word at every level, including this one.
  Authentic text does not mean context-guessing is now allowed.
- **Common error:** treating "I don't know what it means" as the same problem as "I can't
  decode it." **Correction:** separate the two explicitly — decoding (getting a pronunciation)
  and vocabulary (knowing a meaning) are different skills; today's protocol is about decoding,
  with meaning-checking only as a distinct step 4/5.
- **For adults:** point out plainly that this lesson's three text types (notice, recipe,
  news) are exactly the kind of real-world reading that matters most day to day — this is a
  genuine milestone, not a school exercise.
- **For young kids:** a recipe works well as a hands-on follow-up — actually cooking the soup
  together turns "functional text" into an immediately lived experience.
- **Never say:** "don't worry about the hard words, just get the gist" — the protocol always
  gets run on every unfamiliar word; skipping it defeats the entire purpose of decoding
  instruction.

## Decodability note (not a full audit — these are intentionally authentic, uncontrolled texts)
These three texts are NOT decodability-audited against a taught-inventory, by design — that
is the entire point of the off-ramp (DESIGN.md rule 2 explicitly names this transition point:
"the rest are pre-taught story words, flagged"; from here forward, the flag is the protocol
itself, not a pre-teaching step). Words expected to require the full word-attack routine at
this stage include: *residents, scheduled, maintenance, experience, pressure, apologize,
inconvenience, ingredients, vegetable, simmer, tender, according, officials, announced,
district, staffed, healthcare, neighboring.* All are fully decodable using the 4.14 routine
(they contain only known syllable types once affixes are peeled and syllables are chunked) —
none require whole-word memorization, which is the honest test of whether Level 4's teaching
actually generalizes.
