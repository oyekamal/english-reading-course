# L1.13 — Review: All Letters, Letter Names vs. Sounds, b/d/p/q

**Goal:** Consolidate all 26 letters and their sounds; explicitly separate letter *name* from
letter *sound*; directly address the b/d/p/q mirror-confusion as its own short, dedicated
lesson.
**New today:** no new letter-sounds — this is a consolidation lesson, plus one genuinely new
skill: telling b/d/p/q apart reliably.
**Review:** everything, L1.02–L1.12.
**Heart words:** no, so.
**Time:** 25–30 minutes (slightly longer — this is a full review lesson), single sitting.

**Not split into sittings:** no new sounds are introduced today (see L1.10's note for the same
reasoning) — this is consolidation plus the b/d/p/q discrimination skill, not new-GPC load.

**Fast track (adults/older learners):** if the anchor tricks in §3 click quickly, there's no
need to labor over them — move to the Check once confident.

## 1. Warm-up review (3 min)

Flash all 26 letters in random order (not alphabetical) — for each, ask "what does it *say*?"
not "what is it *called*?" Note any hesitations for targeted practice later in the lesson.

## 2. Hear it (2 min)

No new sounds. Instead: pick five words, one from early in the level and one from late, and
have the learner blend all five back to back — sat, cut, fox, quiz, jazz — to feel how far
things have come since L1.02's three-word universe.

## 3. Meet it — name vs. sound, and b/d/p/q (5 min)

**Letter name vs. letter sound:** every letter has a *name* (what you'd call it in the
alphabet song — "bee," "dee," "pee," "cue") and a *sound* (what it *says* in a word — /b/,
/d/, /p/, /kw/). This course has drilled *sounds* from day one on purpose, since sounds are
what blending needs — but it's worth naming this distinction explicitly now that all 26 are
known: "the letter is called 'bee'; it says /b/." Quiz a few at random both ways.

**b / d / p / q — the mirror-confusion set.** Unlike the v/w mix-up (an *auditory* confusion,
tackled in L1.11), b/d/p/q is a *visual* one: these four shapes are literally mirror-images
and 180° rotations of each other (flip b vertically → get p; flip b horizontally → get d;
rotate d 180° → get q). This is genuinely common at this stage across languages and ages, not
a reading-disability signal on its own.

**Two anchor tricks** (offer both, let the learner pick whichever sticks):
1. **The "bed" trick:** make two fists, thumbs up, held side by side — the left fist's thumb
   and shape look like "b," the right looks like "d" — together they spell "bed," a shape you
   can physically make with your hands.
2. **The clock trick:** b's loop is on the bottom-right (like 4–5 o'clock); d's loop is on the
   bottom-left (like 7–8 o'clock) — say "b's belly points right, d's belly points left."
For **p/q**: p hangs down from the top with its loop on the right at the top; q hangs down
with its tail curling out the bottom-right. Say "q always needs its u right after it" — this
is actually a genuine, reliable extra clue, since q is *never* alone in English (L1.12).

## 4. Blend it (5 min)

**Real words (15, cumulative, deliberately mixing b/d/p pairs):** sat, cut, run, box, jam, bat,
pat, dip, bag, dig, big, pig, dog, doll, mess
**Pseudowords (5):** dax, qov, zeb, wuk, tio

## 5. Spell it (5 min)

1. Dictate three pairs specifically to check b/d/p discrimination in writing: "bat/pat,"
   "dig/big," "bag/dag" (dag is nonsense — checking the letter shape, not word recognition).
2. Full-sentence dictation: **"The dog and the pig sit."** (dictate the whole thing; this
   pulls together review letters, the -s check via "sit" needing no suffix here, and heart
   words "the"/"and.")

## 6. Heart word(s) (2 min)

- **no** — n (✓) is regular. The **o** says its long /oʊ/ sound, same pattern as "go" (L1.12)
  and "do" (L1.10) — **heart part: the "o."** Point out this is now the *third* heart word with
  exactly this same irregular-o pattern; naming the pattern itself is worth doing explicitly.
- **so** — s (✓) is regular. Same long-o pattern again — **heart part: the "o."** By now the
  learner may start predicting this pattern in new words before being told; if so, say so —
  that's exactly the self-teaching mechanism this whole course is built on (`research/01`,
  Share's Self-Teaching Hypothesis).

## 7. Read it

**Track A (child — a race between a dog and a pig):**
> Bob has a big dog. Pat has a big pig. The dog and the pig run. Did the dog win? Did the pig
> win? No, they did not win. It was a big mess.

**Track B (adult — a mislabeled delivery at the dock):**
*(Tutor context, spoken not decoded: "A shipping tag got misread at the dock — the letters
looked alike.")*
> Dan has a big box. Pat has a big box. The tag said "b." Dan said, "d?" No, it was "b." It
> was a big mess at the dock.

**Questions (2 literal, 1 think):**
1. What raced, in Track A, and who won?
2. What letter did Dan misread in Track B, and for which letter?
3. Think: now that you know the b/d/p/q tricks from §3, which one would have helped Dan avoid
   his mistake?

## 8. Listen & Talk (5 min)

> **Why Labels Matter**
> In busy warehouses, a single misread letter on a shipping label can send a package hundreds
> of miles in the wrong direction. That's why professional warehouse workers are trained to
> read labels a specific, careful way: never glance quickly at a whole word and guess, but
> check the exact letters, especially any that could be confused with another — b and d, or
> the number 0 and the letter O. Some warehouses even print certain confusable letters in a
> special typeface designed to make them look as different from each other as possible. It's a
> small design choice built entirely around a very human, very ordinary reading mistake.

**Tier-2 words:**
- **typeface** — a particular style or design of printed letters. "This typeface makes the
  numbers easier to tell apart." "Formal documents often use a plain, clear typeface." Your
  turn: have you noticed a typeface that was especially easy or hard to read?
- **professional** — trained and skilled at a particular job, doing it to a high standard.
  "A professional driver checks mirrors constantly." "She gave a professional, careful answer."
  Your turn: what makes someone act "professionally" at a task?

**Discussion questions:**
1. Why does the passage say warehouse workers are trained not to "glance quickly and guess" at
   labels?
2. What did some warehouses design specifically to solve the b/d mix-up problem?

## 9. Check (2 min)

- **Real (5):** big, dig, pat, bag, dog
- **Pseudo (5):** dax, qov, zeb, wuk, tio
- **Dictated (1):** "pig"

**≥10/11 → move to L1.14 (the Level 1 Mastery Check).** Below that: spend one more short
session purely on the b/d/p/q anchor tricks from §3 with real writing practice (not just
reading) before attempting the mastery check — writing errors on this specific confusable set
are extremely common and usually resolve with a few more days of the anchor tricks, not with
re-teaching any sound.

---

### Tutor notes

- **Common error: any single b/d/p/q mix-up** — expected, not a red flag on its own at this
  stage. Use the anchor tricks from §3 consistently rather than switching strategies lesson to
  lesson.
- **If b/d/p/q confusion persists heavily past the Level 1 mastery check** (see
  `mastery-check.md`'s reteach map): this is the point to start tracking it as a pattern worth
  extra attention, not before.
- **Never** let the learner guess a confusable letter from context — always go back to an
  anchor trick and check the shape directly.
- **For adults:** the warehouse-labeling story is a genuinely useful, dignity-respecting frame
  for adult learners who may feel embarrassed about b/d/p/q mix-ups — this is a documented,
  professionally-addressed problem, not just a "little kid" issue.
- **For young kids:** physically making the "bed" shape with two fists is usually the
  highest-retention trick at this age — do it together, more than once.
