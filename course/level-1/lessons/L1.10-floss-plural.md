# L1.10 — ff, ll, ss, zz (FLOSS) + the -s plural

**Goal:** Learn the doubling spelling rule (FLOSS) and the -s ending for "more than one" and
for matching verbs to "he/she/it" — the first two spelling/grammar *rules* in the course,
not just new sounds.
**New today:** the FLOSS rule (f, l, s, z double at the end of a short word right after one
short vowel: doll, bell, kiss, buzz); the -s ending.
**Review:** b, f, l; words fan, big, bag.
**Heart words:** do, what.
**Time:** 20–25 minutes, single sitting.

**Not split into sittings:** unlike L1.02–L1.09/L1.11–L1.12, this lesson introduces zero new
*sounds* — FLOSS and the -s ending are spelling/grammar rules layered onto sounds already
taught. The one-new-sound-per-sitting pace (see README and any multi-sound lesson) exists to
cap new-GPC load per research/01 §5 rule 2; that constraint doesn't apply here, so this lesson
stays a single session.

## 1. Warm-up review (2–3 min)

Flash b, f, l. Read 5 review words: fan, big, bag, lip, bed.

## 2. Hear it (2 min)

No new *sounds* today — ff, ll, ss, zz all say exactly the sounds already known (/f/, /l/,
/s/, /z/). This lesson teaches a **spelling rule**, not a new sound: "at the very end of a
short word, right after one short vowel, f, l, s, and z usually get doubled." (Say the letters
that follow this rule in order — F-L-O-S-S is a common memory trick, hence the name.)

## 3. Meet it (3 min)

Show pairs side by side: "bed" vs. "bell," "hi" vs. "hill" (once y/long-i patterns arrive
later, "hi" itself will make more sense — for now, just contrast the doubled-consonant word).
Show that ck, taught in L1.06, is actually the *same kind* of end-of-short-word doubling idea
applied to /k/, just spelled differently (ck instead of kk) — connecting a new rule to
something already familiar.

**The -s ending:** show "cat" → "cats" (more than one), and "sit" → "sits" ("she sits," matching
he/she/it). Both use the exact same added letter, s, saying /s/ or /z/ depending on the word.

## 4. Blend it (5 min)

**Real words (15, cumulative, mixing FLOSS words and -s endings):** doll, bell, fell, tell,
sell, tall, hill, bill, fill, mill, kiss, buzz, cats, bugs, hats
**Pseudowords (5):** lodd, fess, nuzz, hiff, tull

> **Note:** "well" is deliberately excluded from this lesson's word lists and texts — despite
> being a textbook FLOSS word, it *starts* with w, which isn't formally taught until L1.11,
> tomorrow. Using it today would break decodability. "Tall" (t-a-l-l) fills the same slot
> without that problem.

## 5. Spell it (4 min)

1. Dictate "bell" and "kiss," asking the learner to double the final letter correctly using
   the FLOSS rule just taught.
2. Dictate "cats" and "sits," adding -s to a known base word.
3. Sentence dictation: **"The bag is full."** (The: heart word; bag: /b/ /a/ /g/; is: heart
   word; full: /f/ /u/ /l/ /l/, FLOSS rule in action.)

## 6. Heart word(s) (2 min)

- **do** — d (✓) is regular. The **o** says a long /uː/ sound here, not the short /ɒ/ just
  practiced for weeks — **heart part: the "o."**
- **what** — "wh" is a two-letter spelling that isn't formally taught until Level 2, Lesson
  2.4 (and w itself isn't taught until L1.11, tomorrow). **Mark "wh" as heart for now.** The
  rest, "at," is fully regular and already known as its own word.

## 7. Read it

**Track A (child — dolls and bells):**
> Sam got ten bells. Sam got a doll. The doll sits on the bed. Sam pats the doll. Sam hugs the
> doll. Is the doll a hit? The doll is a hit!

**Track B (adult — running a small shop):**
*(Tutor context, spoken not decoded: "Dan and Nat run neighboring stalls at the market — one
sells bells, the other dolls.")*
> Dan sells bells. Nat sells dolls. Dan fills a bag. Nat tells Dan, "The bag is full."

**Questions (2 literal, 1 think):**
1. What did the main character get at the start of the text?
2. Find one word in the text that has a doubled letter at the end (FLOSS) — which letter is
   doubled?
3. Think: "hit" is used at the end of Track A to mean "a success, something people love" — not
   "to strike something." Can you think of another sentence using each meaning of "hit"?

## 8. Listen & Talk (5 min)

> **One Word, Two Meanings**
> English is full of words that look and sound exactly the same but mean completely different
> things depending on how they're used — linguists call these homonyms. "Well" can mean a deep
> hole dug for water, or it can mean feeling healthy, or it can mean "okay, so..." at the start
> of a sentence. "Bill" can be a person's name, a piece of paper telling you what you owe, or
> the beak of a bird. Context — the words around a word — is what tells a listener or reader
> which meaning is meant. This is exactly why understanding a whole sentence matters so much
> more than knowing single words in isolation.

**Tier-2 words:**
- **context** — the words, situation, or information around something that helps explain what
  it means. "Without context, that sentence was confusing." "Read the whole paragraph for
  context." Your turn: can you think of a word that changes meaning depending on context?
- **isolation** — being separate or apart from everything else, on its own. "One word in
  isolation doesn't tell you much." "She practiced the difficult passage in isolation before
  playing the whole song." Your turn: what's easier to understand with context than in
  isolation?

**Discussion questions:**
1. What are two completely different meanings of the word "bill," according to the passage?
2. Why does the passage say context matters more than knowing single words alone?

## 9. Check (2 min)

- **Real (5):** bell, kiss, cats, hats, fill
- **Pseudo (5):** lodd, fess, nuzz, hiff, tull
- **Dictated (1):** "doll"

**≥10/11 → move to L1.11.** Below that: sort review words into "doubled ending" vs. "single
ending" (bed/bell, hat/hill) as a quick visual check before re-testing — misses here are
usually a spelling-rule issue, not a sound issue, since FLOSS never introduces a new sound.

---

### Tutor notes

- **Common error: forgetting to double** (writing "bel" for "bell"). Correction: point back to
  the rule — "short word, one short vowel, ends in f/l/s/z? Double it." Not a rule to drill to
  exhaustion today; it will come up constantly in future lessons.
- **Common error: doubling everywhere**, including words that don't need it (e.g., "catt" for
  "cat"). This actually shows the rule is being *applied*, just over-applied — a good sign,
  gently correct with "only f, l, s, z double this way; other letters don't."
- **Never** treat the -s ending as something to memorize by shape — it's a rule (add s), apply
  it the same way every time.
- **For adults:** the homonym discussion ("well," "bill") is a nice moment to ask if their
  first language has similar same-word-different-meaning cases — often a genuinely interesting
  comparison.
- **For young kids:** the doll/bell story can be acted out with real toys if available —
  optional.
