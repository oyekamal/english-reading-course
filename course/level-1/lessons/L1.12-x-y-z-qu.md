# L1.12 — x y z qu

**Goal:** Read and spell x, y, z, and the qu spelling; the alphabet's sounds are now complete
(all 26 letters covered across L1.02–L1.12).
**New today:** x (usually says /ks/, two sounds in one letter), y (as a consonant, /j/, at the
start of a word — its vowel jobs come in Level 3), z, qu (always together, says /kw/) — **one
new grapheme per sitting**.
**Review:** j, v, w; words jam, van, wet.
**Heart words:** have, go (taught in the final combined sitting).
**Time:** five sittings — A (~12 min), B (~10 min), C (~10 min), D (~10 min), E (~25–30 min).
**Materials:** paper, pencil.

**Fast track (adults/older learners):** if a sitting's mini check is passed at ≥90%, combine
sittings into one session — this lesson has more sittings than most (four new graphemes), so
an adult clearing checks quickly may reasonably do the whole lesson in two sessions instead of
five. The one-new-grapheme-per-sitting pace is the default, recommended path (research/01 §5
rule 2), not the only path.

---

## Sitting A — x (~12 min)

**Warm-up:** flash j, v, w. Read 3 review words: jam, van, wet.

**Hear it:** /ks/ — x is unusual: it's really two sounds squeezed into one letter, /k/ then
/s/, like the end of "box."

**Meet it:** "This says /ks/" (x — usually at the *end* of a word). Demonstrate slowly: say /k/
then /s/ separately, then squeeze them together fast, so the learner hears it's genuinely two
sounds, not a mystery third sound.

**Blend it (with known sounds only — all prior + today's x):** box, fox, fix, mix, six.

**Spell it:** say /ks/ → write **x**. Segment and write "box" (three sounds: /b/ /o/ /ks/ —
note x counts as the last "chunk" even though it's two sounds).

**Mini check:** x on sight 5/5; blend the 5 words above at ≥4/5.

## Sitting B — y (~10 min)

**Warm-up:** flash x. Read 3 review words: box, fox, six.

**Hear it:** /j/ (consonant y) — tongue humps up toward the roof of the mouth, like the start
of "yes."

**Meet it:** "This says /j/" (y — at the *start* of a word only, for now; y's vowel jobs come
in Level 3).

**Blend it (with known sounds only — all prior + today's y):** yes, yak, yum.

**Spell it:** say /j/ → write **y**. Segment and write "yes."

**Mini check:** y on sight 5/5; blend the 3 words above at ≥3/3 (a smaller set — consonant-y
has few word-initial CVC partners, which is fine).

## Sitting C — z (~10 min)

**Warm-up:** flash y. Read 3 review words: yes, yak, yum.

**Hear it:** /z/ — like /s/ but voiced (buzzing), the start of "zip."

**Meet it:** "This says /z/" (z).

**Blend it (with known sounds only — all prior + today's z):** zip, zap.

**Spell it:** say /z/ → write **z**. Segment and write "zip."

**Mini check:** z on sight 5/5; blend the 2 words above at ≥2/2.

## Sitting D — qu (~10 min)

**Warm-up:** flash z. Read 2 review words: zip, zap.

**Hear it:** /kw/ — q is never alone in English; it always partners with u, and together they
say /kw/, like the start of "quiz."

**Meet it:** "These two always go together and say /kw/" (qu). "q never goes anywhere without u
in English — they're a package."

**Blend it (with known sounds only — all prior + today's qu):** quiz, quit.

**Spell it:** say /kw/ → write **qu**. Segment and write "quiz."

**Mini check:** qu on sight 2/2; blend the 2 words above at ≥2/2.

## Sitting E — x, y, z, qu together: heart words, Read it, Check (~25–30 min)

**Warm-up review (2–3 min):** flash x, y, z, qu; blend 5 mixed words spanning all four: box,
yes, zip, quiz, fix.

**Blend it — full cumulative list (5 min):**

**Real words (15, cumulative):** box, fox, fix, mix, six, wax, yes, yak, zip, quiz, quit, jazz,
yum, vex, fizz
**Pseudowords (6):** zox, quab, yig, fex, wux, zag

**Note:** "zag" is deliberately in the pseudoword list, not the real-word list — on its own,
outside "zigzag," it's a bound fragment rather than a standalone real word; "fizz" (a genuine
free-standing word) fills the real-word slot instead.

**Spell it (4 min):**
1. Segment and write: "quiz."
2. Sentence dictation: **"Go, fox, go!"** (Go: heart word; fox: /f/ /o/ /ks/.)

**Heart word(s) (2 min):**
- **have** — h (✓), a (✓), v (✓) are all regular. The final e usually signals "make the vowel
  before it say its long name" (a pattern called silent-e, taught properly in Level 3) — if
  that rule applied here, "have" would say "hayv." **It doesn't** — it stays short, /hæv/.
  **Heart part: the silent e breaks its own future rule in this one very common word.** Say:
  "you'll learn the real silent-e rule soon; this word is the famous exception to it."
- **go** — g (✓) is regular. The o says a long /oʊ/ here (like "go," "so," "no" — a pattern
  called an open syllable, taught properly in Level 3, L3.5). **Heart part: the "o" says its
  long sound, not the short one you know.**

### 7. Read it

**Track A (child — a pet fox and a box of pups):**
> Sam has a fox. Sam has a box. The fox naps in the box. Sam has six pups. Go, fox, go!

**Track B (adult — quitting time at the shop):**
*(Tutor context, spoken not decoded: "Dan and Nat are fixing a delivery van before the end of
their shift.")*
> Dan has a box. Nat has a box. Dan and Nat fix a van. Nat quits at six. Go, Nat, go!

**Questions (2 literal, 1 think):**
1. What did the main character have, and where did it end up?
2. What time does the action happen in Track B?
3. Think: "has" says its final s like /z/, just like "is" did back in L1.03 — can you think of
   another word ending in s that might say /z/ the same way?

### 8. Listen & Talk (5 min)

> **All 26, At Last**
> Every single letter of the English alphabet has now had its own proper introduction — some
> of them, like c and k, even share a job. It took twelve lessons to get here, on purpose:
> rushing through all 26 letters in a week or two sounds efficient, but it means most of them
> never get practiced enough to become automatic. From here, nothing new needs to be
> "discovered" — the next lessons are about combining these same 26 letters in cleverer and
> cleverer ways: two letters making one sound, sounds joining into longer words, and patterns
> repeating across hundreds of different words once you know what to look for.

**Tier-2 words:**
- **automatic** — happening without needing to think about it carefully each time, because it's
  been practiced so much. "Reading familiar words becomes automatic after enough practice."
  "Braking for a red light should be automatic for a driver." Your turn: name something you do
  automatically now that used to take real effort.
- **combining** — putting two or more things together to make something new. "Combining flour
  and water makes dough." "This lesson is about combining letters in new ways." Your turn:
  what's something good that comes from combining two things?

**Discussion questions:**
1. Why does the passage say rushing through all 26 letters quickly would actually be worse,
   not better?
2. What does the passage say comes next, now that all 26 letters are taught?

### 9. Check (2 min)

- **Real (5):** box, six, zip, quiz, yes
- **Pseudo (5):** zox, quab, yig, fex, wux
- **Dictated (1):** "fix"

**≥10/11 → move to L1.13.** Below that: check specifically whether x's two-sound-in-one
quality is the sticking point (very common) versus a straightforward letter-sound gap, and
reteach accordingly.

### Tutor notes

- **Common error: reading x as a single /s/ or /z/ sound**, skipping the /k/. Correction: slow
  down and isolate /k/ then /s/ before blending them back together at speed.
- **Common error: forgetting qu must stay together** (writing just "q"). Correction: "q never
  goes anywhere without u in English — they're a package."
- **Never** guess a word's meaning from its first letter alone — this remains true all the way
  through the alphabet, not just for the tricky letters like x.
- **For adults:** the "has/is" s-says-z pattern-transfer point in Q3 is a good moment to name
  explicitly that noticing patterns like this — rather than memorizing every word separately —
  is exactly the skill this whole course is built to develop.
- **For young kids:** finishing all 26 letters is genuinely a milestone worth marking (a
  sticker, a cheer, whatever fits) — say so explicitly; motivation matters (`research/02` §5).
