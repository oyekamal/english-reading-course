# L1.02 — s a t

**Goal:** Read and spell the letters s, a, t by sound; blend them into the only real words
they can currently make.
**New today:** letters s, a, t (sounds /s/, /æ/, /t/) — **one new sound per sitting** (see
below), not all three in a single session.
**Review:** yesterday's oral blending games (2 min recap only).
**Heart words:** a, I (taught in the final combined sitting, once s/a/t are all in).
**Time:** four short sittings — A (~10 min), B (~10 min), C (~15 min), D (~25–30 min). Run one
sitting per session (research/01 §5 rule 2: cap new-GPC introduction, mastery-gated, not
calendar-gated), or see the fast-track note below.
**Materials:** paper, pencil; optional letter tiles/magnets, tracing sheet.

**Fast track (adults/older learners):** if a sitting's mini check (see each sitting's end) is
passed at ≥90%, you may combine two or more sittings into one longer session instead of
spreading them across days. The one-sound-per-sitting pace below is the default, recommended
path — it's what the mastery-gate rule (DESIGN.md rule 4) is actually for — not the only path.

**Honest limitation, stated up front (applies to Sitting D):** with only three letters taught
by the end of this lesson, there are exactly three possible real words: *at, as, sat*. The
lesson template's "10–15 real words" target (DESIGN.md §4) is not reachable until L1.03 adds
p, i, n. This lesson's word list is short on purpose — padding it with invented "real" words
would break the no-invented-content rule.

---

## Sitting A — s (~10 min)

**Warm-up:** none — this is the level's first letter after L1.01's oral-only lesson.

**Hear it:** /s/ — hissing sound, like a snake. Say "sun...s-s-s-un."

**Meet it:** "This says /s/." (letter **s**). **Mouth cue:** teeth close together, air hisses
out. **Optional multisensory:** trace **s** in the air or in sand while saying /s/.

**Blend it:** not yet possible — s is the only letter known so far, and blending needs at
least two sounds. Just practice saying /s/ crisply and quickly, several times, isolated.

**Spell it:** say /s/ → write **s**, several times.

**Mini check:** can the learner produce /s/ instantly, on sight of the letter, 5/5 times? If
not, repeat Hear it/Meet it once more before Sitting B.

## Sitting B — a (~10 min)

**Warm-up:** flash s — "what does it say?"

**Hear it:** /a/ — short, open mouth, like the start of "apple." Say "at...a-a-at."

**Meet it:** "This says /a/." (letter **a**). **Mouth cue:** mouth drops open, jaw relaxed.

**Blend it:** now two letters exist — practice blending s+a into "sa," sliding the two sounds
together without a gap. **This is a practice syllable, not a real word yet** — say so plainly;
"sa" doesn't mean anything on its own, it's just proof the blending motion works.

**Spell it:** say /a/ → write **a**. Segment and write "sa" (a practice syllable, not scored
as a real spelling).

**Mini check:** s + a, both sounds on sight, 5/5, plus one blend of "sa." If not solid, repeat
before Sitting C.

## Sitting C — t (~15 min)

**Warm-up:** flash s, a. Blend "sa" once as a refresher.

**Hear it:** /t/ — quick tap of the tongue behind the top teeth, like a tiny click. Say
"top...t-top."

**Meet it:** "This says /t/." (letter **t**). **Mouth cue:** tongue taps the ridge behind the
top front teeth, then releases.

**Blend it:** all three letters now exist — the only three real words in English's whole
system that can be built from just s, a, t appear for the first time here:

| Real words (3) | Pseudowords / "alien words" (5) |
|---|---|
| at, as, sat | tas, ast, sta, sas, att |

Model blending explicitly, sound by sound, sliding the sounds together without stopping
between them: "/s/.../a/.../t/ → sat." Point under each letter as it's said. Have the learner
do the same for all 8 items above, in mixed order, twice through.

**Spell it:** say /t/ → write **t**. Segment and write "at," "sat" — these are the first two
real, scored spellings of the lesson.

**Mini check:** all three letter-sounds, 5/5 each; blend all 8 items above at ≥7/8.

## Sitting D — s, a, t together: heart words, Read it, Check (~25–30 min)

**Warm-up review (2–3 min):** flash s, a, t in mixed order; blend all 8 items from Sitting C
once more, faster this time.

**Heart word(s) (2 min):**
- **a** — you already learned this letter says /a/ (like in "at") when it's *inside* a word.
  On its own, as its own tiny word, it usually says a quick "uh" sound instead (e.g., "a cat").
  **Heart part: the "uh" pronunciation** — the letter shape is the one you just learned; only
  the sound-as-a-standalone-word is the irregular bit to remember.
- **I** — always a capital letter, always alone as a word, and it says its own letter *name*,
  "eye." The lowercase letter i (which says /i/ inside words like "pin") isn't formally taught
  until **L1.03**, tomorrow. For now, treat the whole word **I** as one memorized shape —
  "heart it": I = "eye," always capital, always by itself.

### 7. Read it

Only s, a, t, plus this lesson's two heart words (a, I) exist. Track A and Track B are the
same text at this lesson — there isn't yet enough vocabulary to differentiate a child register
from an adult one; that becomes possible from L1.04 (see README).

**Track A / Track B (identical at this lesson):**
> Sat. A tat. At.

Three separate one-or-two-word lines, read like three short exclamations, not one sentence
(there isn't yet a way to connect them grammatically with this letter set).

**Questions (2 literal, 1 think):**
1. Which word means someone sat down? (*"sat"*)
2. Which word starts with the /a/ sound and has just two letters? (*"at" or "as"*)
3. Think: can you say another word that has only the sounds /s/, /a/, /t/ in some order?

### 8. Listen & Talk (5 min)

Read this **to** the learner — it is well above their current decoding level on purpose.

> **Sam's First Day**
> Sam felt nervous walking into the new classroom. Everyone else already seemed to know each
> other, chatting and laughing near the window. Sam picked a desk near the back and sat down
> quietly, hoping nobody would notice how fast their heart was beating. Then a girl named Mia
> turned around and smiled. "Are you new? I sat exactly where you are on my first day too," she
> said. "It gets easier by lunchtime, I promise." Sam felt a small knot of worry loosen. Maybe
> today wouldn't be so bad after all.

**Tier-2 words:**
- **nervous** — feeling worried or a little scared about something that's about to happen.
  "I felt nervous before my first swimming lesson." "She was nervous about the job interview."
  Your turn: tell me about a time you felt nervous.
- **loosen** — to become less tight or less tense. "The worried knot in his stomach began to
  loosen." "Loosen your grip on the rope a little." Your turn: what's something that can loosen?

**Discussion questions:**
1. How did Sam feel at the very start of the story? How do you know?
2. Why do you think Mia's words helped Sam feel better?

### 9. Check (2 min)

Given the honest 3-word limit, the check reuses the same real words with a dictated item:

- **Real (repeat set of the only 3 that exist, asked out of order):** at, sat, as, at, sat
- **Pseudo:** tas, sta, ast, sas, att
- **Dictated:** "at"

**≥9/11 correct (≈≥90%) → move to L1.03.** Below that: redo Sitting C's blending drill once
more (most misses at this stage are a blending-speed issue, not a letter-sound issue — check
each individual letter sound first before reteaching the whole word).

### Tutor notes

- **Common error:** saying the letter *name* ("ess," "ay," "tee") instead of its *sound*.
  Correction: "That's its name — what does it *say*?" Never accept the name as the answer to a
  sound question.
- **Common error:** blending with a full stop between sounds ("s...a...t" with gaps) instead of
  sliding them together. Correction: model sliding your finger continuously under the letters as
  you say the sounds, without lifting it.
- **Never** say "look at the first letter and guess" — always "point, say each sound, blend."
  (DESIGN.md rule 1.)
- **For adults:** acknowledge the word list is short and say why (honesty > false richness) —
  "there are only three letters so far, so only three real words exist; tomorrow adds three
  more and it opens right up." The fast-track option above is worth naming explicitly for a
  confident adult learner rather than making them sit through four separate short sessions.
- **For young kids:** the "alien words" framing (tas, sta, etc.) is usually genuinely fun at
  this age — lean into it ("what does this alien word say?").
- **"Tat" is a real but obscure word** — it isn't a silent mystery for the learner to wonder
  about. Say what it means before or after reading the text: "a small decoration or bit of
  cheap old stuff — like tinsel or a trinket" (as in "a bit of tat"). It's used here purely
  because it's one of only three real words this lesson's three letters can build (Sitting C) —
  not because it's a word worth memorizing for its own sake.
