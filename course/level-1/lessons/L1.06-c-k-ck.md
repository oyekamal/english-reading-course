# L1.06 — c k ck

**Goal:** Read and spell the two spellings of /k/ (c and k) plus the digraph ck; unlock words
like "cat," "sick," "sack," "dock."
**New today:** c, k (both say /k/), ck (also says /k/, used at the end of a word right after a
short vowel) — **one new grapheme per sitting**, since all three spell the same sound.
**Review:** g, o; words got, dog, top.
**Heart words:** said, you (taught in the final combined sitting).
**Time:** four sittings — A (~12 min), B (~12 min), C (~15 min), D (~25–30 min).
**Materials:** paper, pencil.

**Fast track (adults/older learners):** if a sitting's mini check is passed at ≥90%, combine
sittings into one session. The one-new-grapheme-per-sitting pace is the default, recommended
path (research/01 §5 rule 2), not the only path.

---

## Sitting A — c (~12 min)

**Warm-up:** flash g, o. Read 3 review words: got, top, dot.

**Hear it:** /k/ — back of the tongue taps the soft palate, no voice buzz (unlike its voiced
twin /g/). Like the start of "cat."

**Meet it:** "This says /k/" (c). **Mouth cue:** same tongue-back placement as /g/, but no
throat buzz — contrast the two by feeling the throat with a finger.

**Blend it (with known sounds only — s, a, t, p, i, n, m, d, g, o + today's c):** cat, cot, cop,
cap, can.

**Spell it:** say /k/ → write **c**. Segment and write "cat."

**Mini check:** c on sight 5/5; blend the 5 words above at ≥4/5.

## Sitting B — k (~12 min)

**Warm-up:** flash c. Read 3 review words: cat, cot, cap.

**Hear it:** /k/ again — same sound, different letter. "c and k are like two people saying the
exact same word."

**Meet it:** "This *also* says /k/" (k). Note the light choice rule: "c is usually used before
a, o, u; k is usually used before i, e." This is a light touch, not a full spelling-rule
lesson — the goal today is reading both correctly, not mastering the rule yet.

**Blend it (with known sounds only — s, a, t, p, i, n, m, d, g, o, c + today's k):** kid, kit,
kin.

**Spell it:** say /k/ → ask "which letter today, c or k?" for "kid" vs. "cat," so the choice,
not just the sound, gets practiced.

**Mini check:** k on sight 5/5; blend the 3 words above at ≥3/3 (a smaller set than usual — k
genuinely has fewer word-initial partners at this stage, which is fine).

## Sitting C — ck (~15 min)

**Warm-up:** flash c, k. Read 3 review words: cat, kid, cot.

**Hear it:** /k/ a third time — same sound again.

**Meet it:** "This *also* says /k/" (ck — always two letters together, always right after a
short vowel, always at the end of a word). "ck is used at the end of a short word, right after
the vowel says its short sound — like 'sock' or 'sick.'"

**Blend it (with known sounds only — all prior + today's ck):** sick, tack, sack, sock, dock.

**Spell it:** say /k/ at the end of a short word → write **ck**. Segment and write "sick,"
noting the ck spelling explicitly.

**Mini check:** ck on sight (in context, e.g. inside "sick") 5/5; blend the 5 words above at
≥4/5.

## Sitting D — c, k, ck together: heart words, Read it, Check (~25–30 min)

**Warm-up review (2–3 min):** flash c, k, ck (in context) mixed; blend 5 words spanning all
three spellings: cat, kid, sock, tack, cop.

**Blend it — full cumulative list (5 min):**

**Real words (15, cumulative):** cat, cot, cop, cap, can, cog, kid, kit, kin, sick, tack, pack,
sock, dock, got
**Pseudowords (5):** cak, gack, cid, sok, dack

**Spell it (4 min):**
1. Segment and write: "cat," "sick" (note the ck spelling explicitly).
2. Sentence dictation: **"The cat sat."** (The: heart word; cat: /k/ /a/ /t/; sat: /s/ /a/
   /t/.)

**Heart word(s) (2 min):**
- **said** — s (✓) and d (✓) are both regular. The middle two letters, "ai," normally say /eɪ/
  (a sound not taught yet, coming in Level 3) — but in "said" they say a totally different,
  irregular /ɛ/ sound, like the e in "bed." **Heart part: "ai" says /ɛ/, unlike anywhere else
  it will appear later.** This is one of English's most famous irregular spellings.
- **you** — none of its letters behave as expected: y isn't taught until L1.12, and o+u don't
  spell the /uː/ sound the way "you" is pronounced through any rule taught in this course.
  **Mark the whole word as heart.**

### 7. Read it

**Track A (child — a new pet, continuing from L1.05's Track A):**
> Sam got a cat. The cat sat on a mat. Sam said, "Is the cat sick?" The cat is not sick. The
> cat sat and sat.

**Track B (adult — a bad day at the dock):**
*(Tutor context, spoken not decoded: "Dan works at a shipping dock and just got some bad
news.")*
> Dan got a cap. Dan sat at the dock. Dan said, "Is a sack on top?" The sack is not on top.
> Dan got the sack.

**Questions (2 literal, 1 think):**
1. What did Sam/Dan get at the start of the text?
2. What did the main character say out loud, in quotation marks?
3. Think: "Dan got the sack" is a well-known expression — what do you think it means, beyond
   just a physical bag?

### 8. Listen & Talk (5 min)

> **Getting the Sack**
> In everyday English, if someone says a person "got the sack," they don't mean an actual bag
> — they mean the person lost their job. Nobody agrees exactly where the phrase came from, but
> one popular idea goes back hundreds of years, when tradesmen carried their own tools to work
> in a sack. If you were let go, you packed up your sack of tools and left — hence "getting
> the sack." Whether or not that story is completely accurate, the phrase has stuck around for
> so long that most English speakers use it without ever thinking about actual sacks at all.

**Tier-2 words:**
- **expression** — a phrase people commonly use that means something different from its exact
  words. "'Break a leg' is an expression that means good luck." "'Piece of cake' is an
  expression for something easy." Your turn: do you know an expression in another language that
  doesn't translate word-for-word?
- **accurate** — correct and matching the true facts exactly. "The weather report was
  accurate — it really did rain." "Make sure your measurements are accurate before you cut the
  wood." Your turn: when does it matter a lot for something to be accurate?

**Discussion questions:**
1. What does "getting the sack" actually mean?
2. Why does the passage say we might use an expression "without ever thinking about" what it
   originally meant?

### 9. Check (2 min)

- **Real (5):** cat, kid, sock, tack, pack
- **Pseudo (5):** cak, gack, cid, sok, dack
- **Dictated (1):** "sick"

**≥10/11 → move to L1.07.** Below that: isolate c vs. k vs. ck as a sorting task (three columns,
sort review words into which spelling they use) before re-checking — this is usually a
spelling-choice issue, not a sound issue, since all three say the same /k/.

---

### Tutor notes

- **Common error: writing "k" for every /k/ sound**, ignoring the c/ck patterns. This is
  expected and not corrected harshly today — a full spelling-choice lesson isn't the goal of
  L1.06; just model the correct choice each time it comes up.
- **Common error: adding "ck" after a long vowel** (not relevant yet — no long vowels taught —
  but flag if it appears; it signals the learner is pattern-matching ahead, which is a good
  sign, just needs a light correction: "ck follows a *short* vowel sound only.")
- **Never** let a learner guess between c/k/ck by "which one looks right" from memory alone —
  always blend the sound first, then apply the light rule from Sitting B.
- **For adults:** "got the sack" is a genuine, useful piece of adult English idiom knowledge —
  worth pointing out that idioms like this are worth learning explicitly, they don't decode
  literally.
- **For young kids:** keep the c/o/u vs. k/i/e "rule of thumb" very light — a sentence, not a
  worksheet; it will be reinforced naturally over many more words.
