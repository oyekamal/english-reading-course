# L1.11 — j v w

**Goal:** Read and spell j, v, w — and, since w is now taught, retire "was" and "we" from
fully-heart status.
**New today:** letters j, v, w (sounds /dʒ/, /v/, /w/) — **one new sound per sitting**.
**Review:** FLOSS + -s; words bell, kiss, cats.
**Heart words:** they, one (taught in the final combined sitting).
**Time:** four sittings — A (~12 min), B (~12 min), C (~15 min), D (~25–30 min).
**Materials:** paper, pencil.

**Fast track (adults/older learners):** if a sitting's mini check is passed at ≥90%, combine
sittings into one session. The one-sound-per-sitting pace is the default, recommended path
(research/01 §5 rule 2), not the only path.

---

## Sitting A — j (~12 min)

**Warm-up:** flash the FLOSS rule and -s ending. Read 3 review words: bell, kiss, cats.

**Hear it:** /dʒ/ — tongue taps the ridge like /d/, then releases into a soft, buzzy "zh" —
like the start of "jam."

**Meet it:** "This says /dʒ/" (j). **Mouth cue:** contrast against /g/ (both made toward the
back/roof) — j is softer and further forward, with that buzzy release.

**Blend it (with known sounds only — s, a, t, p, i, n, m, d, g, o, c, k, ck, e, u, r, h, b, f, l,
FLOSS, -s + today's j):** jam, jog, jet, job, jab.

**Spell it:** say /dʒ/ → write **j**. Segment and write "jog."

**Mini check:** j on sight 5/5; blend the 5 words above at ≥4/5.

## Sitting B — v (~12 min)

**Warm-up:** flash j. Read 3 review words: jam, jog, job.

**Hear it:** /v/ — top teeth rest on bottom lip like /f/, but with voice buzzing — /v/ is /f/'s
"voiced twin." Like the start of "van."

**Meet it:** "This says /v/" (v). **Mouth cue:** feel the throat buzz on v, none on f — same
contrast pattern as b/p, d/t, g/k.

**Blend it (with known sounds only — all prior + today's v):** van, vet, vat.

**Spell it:** say /v/ → write **v**. Segment and write "van."

**Mini check:** v on sight 5/5; blend the 3 words above at ≥3/3 (a smaller set — v has fewer
word-initial CVC partners at this stage, which is fine).

## Sitting C — w (~15 min)

**Warm-up:** flash v. Read 3 review words: van, vet, vat.

**Hear it:** /w/ — lips round tightly then release, like blowing out a candle in slow motion,
the start of "wet."

**Meet it:** "This says /w/" (w). **Mouth cue:** show the lip-rounding clearly; it's the second
sound (after /r/) with almost no visible tongue placement to copy, so watch the lips instead.

### Urdu-speaker tip

Urdu's و (waw) covers the territory of both English /v/ and /w/, so mixing these two up is a
specific, well-documented, and completely normal confusion for Urdu-L1 speakers
(`research/04` §6) — not a general "accent" issue, a precise single gap. Now that both letters
are taught, practice these minimal pairs slowly, listening for the lip-rounding difference:
- **vet / wet**
- **vine / wine**
- **van / wan**
This is worth returning to more than once; it doesn't usually resolve from a single drill.

**Blend it (with known sounds only — all prior + today's w):** wag, wig, wet, win, well (now
decodable: w + e + ll, all taught).

**Spell it:** say /w/ → write **w**. Segment and write "wet."

**Mini check:** w on sight 5/5; blend the 5 words above at ≥4/5.

## Sitting D — j, v, w together: heart words, Read it, Check (~25–30 min)

**Warm-up review (2–3 min):** flash j, v, w; blend 5 mixed words spanning all three: jam, van,
wet, job, wig. Redo the v/w minimal-pair drill once more if Sitting C's mini check was close.

**Blend it — full cumulative list (5 min):**

**Real words (15, cumulative):** jam, jog, jet, job, van, vet, wag, wig, wet, win, web, well,
jab, jot, vat
**Pseudowords (5):** jov, wab, vob, jum, wov

**Spell it (4 min):**
1. Segment and write: "job," "wet."
2. Sentence dictation: **"Sam is wet."** (Sam: /s/ /a/ /m/; is: heart word; wet: /w/ /e/ /t/.)

**Heart word(s) (2 min):**
- **they** — "th" isn't formally taught until Level 2, Lesson 2.3, and "ey" spells an
  irregular /eɪ/ sound here rather than anything taught yet. **Mark the whole word as heart.**
- **one** — completely irregular (/wʌn/) — none of its letters behave as taught, even with w
  now known. **Mark the whole word as heart** — this is a genuine, permanent heart word, not a
  "not taught yet" placeholder like some of the earlier ones.

### 7. Read it

**Track A (child — a wet, fun afternoon):**
> Sam got jam. Sam got a jet. Sam ran. Sam is wet. They ran and ran. Sam had fun.

**Track B (adult — a new delivery job):**
*(Tutor context, spoken not decoded: "Dan and Nat both just started new jobs at the same
delivery company.")*
> Dan got a job. Dan got a van. Dan is wet. Nat got a job. Nat is a vet. They get a van at the
> dock.

**Questions (2 literal, 1 think):**
1. What got wet in the text, and how do you think that happened?
2. What did the two people in Track B each get?
3. Think: what job do you think a "vet" who also drives a delivery van might actually be doing —
   is this the same kind of vet as an animal doctor, or could "vet" mean something else here?

### 8. Listen & Talk (5 min)

> **One Word, Many Jobs**
> The word "vet" hides a small trap: most people think first of an animal doctor, but "vet" is
> also short for "veteran" — someone who has served in the military — and it's a verb too, as
> in "the company vetted every new driver before hiring them," meaning they checked the
> person's background carefully. Three different meanings, one small word, and only the
> sentence around it tells you which one is meant. Careful readers learn to slow down for a
> half-second whenever a short, familiar-looking word suddenly doesn't quite fit the sentence —
> that pause is often the first sign a word is doing a job you didn't expect.

**Tier-2 words:**
- **veteran** — someone with a lot of experience, especially someone who has served in the
  armed forces. "He's a veteran of twenty years in the fire service." "A veteran teacher
  mentored the new hires." Your turn: who's a "veteran" in your life, in either sense?
- **background** — a person's past experience, history, or where they come from. "The company
  checked her background before the interview." "His background in cooking helped him open the
  restaurant." Your turn: how would you describe your own background in one sentence?

**Discussion questions:**
1. Name the three different meanings of "vet" the passage describes.
2. What does the passage suggest a careful reader should do when a short word "doesn't quite
   fit"?

### 9. Check (2 min)

- **Real (5):** jam, van, wet, wig, job
- **Pseudo (5):** jov, wab, vob, jum, wov
- **Dictated (1):** "vet"

**≥10/11 → move to L1.12.** Below that: if misses cluster on v/w specifically, return to the
Urdu-speaker minimal-pair drill in Sitting C before re-testing rather than re-teaching the
letters from scratch — this is very often a listening-discrimination gap, not a
letter-recognition one.

---

### Tutor notes

- **Common error: v/w confusion**, in both directions — see the Urdu-speaker tip above; this
  is one of the most well-documented, specific phoneme gaps for Urdu-L1 speakers and is worth
  patient, repeated minimal-pair practice rather than one-off correction.
- **Common error: j read as /g/** (both are made in a similar mouth area) — model the softer,
  "dzh" quality of j directly against the harder g sound side by side.
- **Never** accept "it's probably v because the sentence is about a van" as a strategy — always
  decode the actual letter.
- **For adults:** "vet" having three unrelated meanings is a good excuse to talk about how
  often adult/workplace English relies on this kind of word-guessing-from-context, and why
  slowing down (not speeding up) is the right response to an unclear word.
- **For young kids:** if a jet/plane toy or picture is available, this is a fun lesson to bring
  it out for Track A.
