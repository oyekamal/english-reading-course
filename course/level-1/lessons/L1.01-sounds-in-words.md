# L1.01 — Sounds in Words

**Goal:** Hear that spoken words are made of separate sounds, and blend/segment 2–3-sound
words *orally* — no letters yet, except a 60-second preview of two shapes at the very end.
**New today:** oral phonemic awareness (blending, segmenting), mouth-position awareness.
**Review:** none — this is lesson 1.
**Heart words:** none yet.
**Time:** 15–20 minutes (shorter than later lessons on purpose).
**Materials:** nothing required; optional — a mirror, small objects/counters (buttons, blocks)
for segmenting, paper.

> **Why no letters until the very end?** Research (`research/01` §5 rule 4, §3 "Contested")
> says a brief, letters-free phonemic-awareness on-ramp is defensible **only** for true
> pre-literate beginners, and it must be brief (this one lesson) and move to letters
> immediately after — not become its own multi-week curriculum. This is that one lesson.

---

## 1. Warm-up (2 min)

No review possible (first lesson). Instead: say the learner's name slowly, sound by sound,
without stretching it into a song — e.g. "Sam" → /s/ /a/ /m/. Ask: "Did you hear three little
sounds in your name?" This previews the whole lesson's idea in one familiar word.

## 2. Hear it — oral phonemic awareness (8–10 min)

Do these as a spoken game, no print at all yet:

**a) First sound isolation.** Say a word slowly; ask which sound comes first.
"sun" → /s/ ... /s/ /ʌ/ /n/ → "what's the first sound?" → /s/.
Words to use: sun, sat, top, mat, pig, dog.

**b) Blending 2–3 sounds into a word (say the sounds separately, learner blends).**
Tutor says the sounds with a short pause between each, learner says the whole word:
- /a/ /t/ → "at"
- /s/ /a/ /t/ → "sat"
- /i/ /t/ → "it"
- /o/ /n/ → "on"
- /m/ /a/ /t/ → "mat"
This is the single most important skill in this lesson — blending is taught *before*
segmenting because it's the easier skill and the one letters will hang on next lesson
(`research/01` §2, "blending before segmenting").

**c) Segmenting (learner says a whole word; tutor or learner claps/taps one beat per sound).**
Start with the learner's own name, then: "up" (2 sounds), "at" (2), "sat" (3), "dog" (3).
Use counters or fingers, one per sound, if that helps — **optional**, never required
(DESIGN.md rule 11).

**d) Mouth-position awareness (2 min).** Say /s/, /a/, /t/, /m/ slowly in front of a mirror
(or just facing the learner) and notice: lips together for /m/, tongue behind teeth for /t/,
mouth open wide for /a/. Not a drill — just "notice what your mouth does."

## 3. Meet it — the first two letter-shapes (2 min, end of lesson only)

Show the printed letters **s** and **a** (lowercase). Say: "This shape is called 's' — and
when we read, it says /s/, like at the start of 'sun.'" Same for **a**: "This shape says /a/,
like at the start of 'at.'" That's it — no blending with print yet, no worksheet. The full
teaching of s and a (with word-building) happens in **L1.02**, tomorrow or next session. This
is a preview, not the lesson.

## 4–5. Blend it / Spell it

Not applicable at this oral-only stage — deferred to L1.02 onward, once print is introduced.

## 6. Heart word(s)

None. First heart words (a, I) appear in L1.02.

## 7. Read it

## 8. Listen & Talk (5 min)

Read this short passage **to** the learner (don't ask them to read any of it):

> **A Big Noisy Day**
> Sam woke up to a loud bang. It was not thunder — it was the neighbor fixing his old truck
> again! Sam's little sister covered her ears and laughed. "That truck is louder than a
> drum," she said. Their mother peeked out the window and shook her head. "Every single
> morning," she sighed, but she was smiling. Outside, the truck coughed, sputtered, and
> finally started with a satisfying roar. Sam waved at the neighbor, who waved back, covered
> in oil and grinning proudly.

**Tier-2 words to teach** (friendly definition + 2 examples + "your turn"):
- **sputtered** — made short, uneven, coughing sounds (like an engine that won't start
  smoothly). "The old car sputtered before it finally started." "The candle sputtered and
  went out." Your turn: can you think of something else that sputters?
- **satisfying** — giving a feeling of pleasure because something worked out or finished well.
  "It was satisfying to finally fix the leaky tap." "Eating a good meal after a long day feels
  satisfying." Your turn: tell me one thing that feels satisfying to you.

**Discussion questions:**
1. What was the loud noise, really?
2. How do you think the mother really felt about the noise — annoyed, amused, both? Why do
   you think that?

## 9. Check (2 min)

Not a decoding check yet (no letters formally taught for word-reading). Instead, a 5-item oral
check:
1. Blend /a/ /t/ → ?
2. Blend /s/ /a/ /t/ → ?
3. What's the first sound in "sun"?
4. What's the first sound in "mat"?
5. Segment "at" into its two sounds.

**≥4/5 correct → move to L1.02.** Below that, repeat step 2's blending games one more short
session before moving on — do not skip straight to letters if blending itself isn't happening
yet, since the letter work in L1.02 depends on it.

### Tutor notes

- **Common "errors" at this stage aren't errors** — a learner who blends slowly, or needs a
  sound repeated, is doing exactly what's expected in lesson 1. Don't rush.
- **Never** ask the learner to guess a word from a picture or "what would make sense" here or
  anywhere else in this course (DESIGN.md rule 1) — there is no word-guessing task in this
  lesson at all, only sound-blending, which is a different, legitimate skill.
- **For adults:** skip nothing, but move faster through the game format — frame it briefly as
  "this is how all reading programs start, even for adults relearning this; it takes five
  minutes and we won't repeat it once letters start tomorrow." Adults sometimes feel this step
  is beneath them; the honest answer is it's brief, evidence-based, and over after this one
  lesson.
- **For young kids:** keep every game short (2–3 minutes) and let attention drive when to
  move to the next mini-game rather than running the full time budget in one block if focus is
  fading.
