# L1.08 — r h

**Goal:** Read and spell r, h; unlock "rat," "hat," "run" — and, since both letters are now
taught, turn yesterday's two heart words ("are," "he") fully or partly decodable.
**New today:** letters r, h (sounds /r/, /h/) — **one new sound per sitting**.
**Review:** e, u; words cut, ten, sun.
**Heart words:** she, we (taught in the final combined sitting).
**Time:** three sittings — A (~15 min), B (~15 min), C (~25–30 min).
**Materials:** paper, pencil.

**Fast track (adults/older learners):** if a sitting's mini check is passed at ≥90%, combine
sittings into one session. The one-sound-per-sitting pace is the default, recommended path
(research/01 §5 rule 2), not the only path.

> **Word-choice note for this lesson:** r as a plain onset consonant (rat, run, rid) is taught
> today. r-*controlled* vowels — the "ar" in "car," "tar," "far" — are a completely different,
> much later grapheme (not taught until Level 4). No word in this lesson uses r after a vowel
> in the same syllable, on purpose; every r-word here is r-at-the-start only.

---

## Sitting A — r (~15 min)

**Warm-up:** flash e, u. Read 3 review words: cut, ten, sun.

**Hear it:** /r/ — lips round slightly, tongue curls back without touching the roof of the
mouth, like the start of "run."

**Meet it:** "This says /r/" (r). **Mouth cue:** /r/ is the first sound so far made with the
tongue not touching anything — it just curls.

**Blend it (with known sounds only — s, a, t, p, i, n, m, d, g, o, c, k, ck, e, u + today's r,
as an onset only):** rat, ran, rug, run, rid.

**Spell it:** say /r/ → write **r**. Segment and write "run."

**Mini check:** r on sight 5/5; blend the 5 words above at ≥4/5.

## Sitting B — h (~15 min)

**Warm-up:** flash r. Read 3 review words: rat, ran, run.

**Hear it:** /h/ — just breath, no tongue or lip shaping at all, like a soft sigh, the start of
"hat."

**Meet it:** "This says /h/" (h). **Mouth cue:** the first sound made with pure breath and
nothing else — put a hand in front of the mouth and feel the puff of air with no buzz.

**Blend it (with known sounds only — all prior + today's h):** hut, hug, hat, hen, hop.

**Spell it:** say /h/ → write **h**. Segment and write "hat."

**Mini check:** h on sight 5/5; blend the 5 words above at ≥4/5.

## Sitting C — r, h together: heart words, Read it, Check (~25–30 min)

**Warm-up review (2–3 min):** flash r, h; blend 5 mixed words spanning both: rat, hop, hat,
run, hen. Also re-read yesterday's two heart words ("are," "he") — point out that "he" is now
fully decodable (h + e, both taught).

**Blend it — full cumulative list (5 min):**

**Real words (15, cumulative):** rat, ran, rug, run, hut, hug, ham, hop, hat, hen, rot, rid,
hog, rip, hid
**Pseudowords (5):** rup, hac, gir, kur, dor

**Spell it (4 min):**
1. Segment and write: "run," "hat."
2. Sentence dictation: **"We ran and ran."** (We: heart word; ran: /r/ /a/ /n/; and: fully
   decodable heart word.)

**Heart word(s) (2 min):**
- **she** — "sh" is a two-letter spelling for one sound (a digraph) that isn't formally taught
  until Level 2, Lesson 2.1 — a little further ahead than most heart words reach back to.
  **Mark "sh" as heart for now**; the e is regular. Say: "'sh' is its own sound, a bit like c/k
  or ck were their own — you'll get the full lesson on it soon."
- **we** — w isn't taught until **L1.11**. **Mark w as heart for now**; the e is regular. Like
  "he" was yesterday, this word will fully graduate off the heart-word list once w arrives.

### 7. Read it

**Track A (child — a hot day):**
> Sam ran. Mom ran. The sun is hot. Is Sam hot? Is Mom hot? We are hot! She got a hat.

**Track B (adult — a hot shift at the dock):**
*(Tutor context, spoken not decoded: "Dan and Nat both work outdoors at the dock on a very hot
day.")*
> Dan ran to the dock. Nat ran to the dock. The sun got hot. Is Dan hot? Is Nat hot? We are
> hot. She got a mug.

**Questions (2 literal, 1 think):**
1. What got hot in the text?
2. Who got a hat, or a mug, at the end?
3. Think: what are some other ways people cool down on a very hot day at work or at play?

### 8. Listen & Talk (5 min)

> **The Morning Rotation**
> Before smartphones and ride-share apps existed, neighbors organized a shared morning
> school run with nothing more than a notebook taped inside someone's kitchen cabinet. Every
> family wrote down which mornings they could take the children, and everyone else simply
> trusted the schedule. It sounds old-fashioned, but the system worked because everyone had
> something to lose if they broke the trust — miss your turn, and you'd better believe your
> neighbors would remember. These days, apps handle the scheduling, but the core idea hasn't
> changed at all: a group of people agreeing to share both the effort and the waiting, so no
> single person carries the whole burden alone.

**Tier-2 words:**
- **schedule** — a plan that says when things will happen, especially times and dates. "Check
  the bus schedule before you leave." "Our morning schedule is taped to the fridge." Your turn:
  what's on your schedule today?
- **burden** — something heavy or difficult that a person has to carry or deal with, not always
  a physical weight. "Sharing the school run took some of the burden off Mom." "Debt can be a
  heavy burden." Your turn: what's one way people can share a burden?

**Discussion questions:**
1. How did the old-fashioned rotation keep people honest, without any app?
2. What is the "core idea" the passage says hasn't changed, even with new technology?

### 9. Check (2 min)

- **Real (5):** rat, hop, hat, rug, rid
- **Pseudo (5):** rup, hac, gir, kur, dor
- **Dictated (1):** "run"

**≥10/11 → move to L1.09.** Below that: check specifically whether r or h is the weaker sound
(they're acoustically very different, so a miss on one usually isn't linked to the other) and
drill that one alone before re-testing.

### Tutor notes

- **Common error: /r/ produced with lip contact or over-rounding**, sometimes carried over from
  languages where the closest sound is trilled or tapped differently. Correction: model the
  English /r/ slowly, tongue curling back without touching anywhere, and let the learner watch
  your mouth (there's very little visible mouth shape to copy for /r/, which is itself worth
  saying out loud).
- **Common error: dropping /h/ entirely** ("at" for "hat") — common across several language
  backgrounds where /h/ is weak or absent in certain positions. Correction: model the breathy
  /h/ exaggeratedly at first, hand in front of mouth to feel the air.
- **Never** accept a silent first sound as "close enough" — /h/ must be audible.
- **Do not reach for "car," "for," "her," or any other r-controlled word this lesson** — that
  entire grapheme family is Level 4 territory; every r-word here keeps r at the very start of
  the word.
- **For adults:** the shared-morning-run story is a good LEA opening — ask if they've ever
  organized something similar informally (a rotation for anything: rides, cooking, childcare).
- **For young kids:** if hot weather is relevant locally, connect Track A's "the sun is hot"
  directly to their own real experience.
