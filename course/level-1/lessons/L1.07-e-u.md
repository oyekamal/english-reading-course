# L1.07 — e u

**Goal:** Read and spell e, u; open up a large new set of CVC words (cut, ten, sun, den…).
**New today:** letters e, u (sounds /ɛ/ — short e, /ʌ/ — short u) — **one new sound per
sitting**.
**Review:** c, k, ck; words cat, sick, sock.
**Heart words:** are, he (taught in the final combined sitting).
**Time:** three sittings — A (~15 min), B (~15 min), C (~25–30 min).
**Materials:** paper, pencil.

**Fast track (adults/older learners):** if a sitting's mini check is passed at ≥90%, combine
sittings into one session. The one-sound-per-sitting pace is the default, recommended path
(research/01 §5 rule 2), not the only path.

---

## Sitting A — e (~15 min)

**Warm-up:** flash c, k, ck. Read 3 review words: cat, kid, sock.

**Hear it:** /ɛ/ (short e) — mouth slightly open, tongue mid-height, like the start of "egg" or
"get."

**Meet it:** "This says /ɛ/" (e). **Mouth cue:** /ɛ/ and the /i/ from L1.03 are close
neighbors in the mouth — say "pit" then "pet" back to back and notice the jaw drops slightly
more for /ɛ/.

### Urdu-speaker tip (part 1 of 2)

English packs more short-vowel distinctions into this space than Urdu does, so near-neighbor
sounds like /ɪ/ and /ɛ/ can genuinely sound alike to an Urdu-L1 ear at first — this isn't
carelessness, it's a real difference between the two sound systems (`research/04` §6). Try the
minimal pair **bit / bet** a few times, listening for the jaw-drop difference, before the word
list below.

**Blend it (with known sounds only — s, a, t, p, i, n, m, d, g, o, c, k, ck + today's e):** get,
ten, pen, den, pet.

**Spell it:** say /e/ → write **e**. Segment and write "get."

**Mini check:** e on sight 5/5; blend the 5 words above at ≥4/5.

## Sitting B — u (~15 min)

**Warm-up:** flash e. Read 3 review words: get, ten, pet.

**Hear it:** /ʌ/ (short u) — mouth relaxed and slightly open, a quick, flat "uh," like the
start of "up" or "cut."

**Meet it:** "This says /ʌ/" (u). **Mouth cue:** /ʌ/ and the /o/ from L1.05 are also close
neighbors — say "cot" then "cut" back to back.

### Urdu-speaker tip (part 2 of 2)

Same pattern as /ɪ/-/ɛ/: /ʌ/ and /ɒ/ are another near-neighbor pair many Urdu-L1 learners
genuinely hear as more similar than English treats them. Try **cut / cot** a few times,
exaggerating the lip-rounding difference, before the word list below — a listening-
discrimination warm-up, not a test.

**Blend it (with known sounds only — all prior + today's u):** cut, cup, sun, nut, mug.

**Spell it:** say /u/ → write **u**. Segment and write "cup."

**Mini check:** u on sight 5/5; blend the 5 words above at ≥4/5.

## Sitting C — e, u together: heart words, Read it, Check (~25–30 min)

**Warm-up review (2–3 min):** flash e, u; blend 5 mixed words spanning both: get, cut, ten,
sun, pet.

**Blend it — full cumulative list (5 min):**

**Real words (15, cumulative):** cut, cup, tug, dug, mug, gum, gut, nut, net, ten, men, pen,
den, sun, ton
**Pseudowords (5):** gup, kec, nud, pem, dut

**Spell it (4 min):**
1. Segment and write: "ten," "cup."
2. Sentence dictation: **"He got a nut."** (He: heart word; got: /g/ /o/ /t/; a: heart word;
   nut: /n/ /u/ /t/.)

**Heart word(s) (2 min):**
- **are** — none of its parts behave as taught: r hasn't been formally taught yet (that's
  **L1.08**, tomorrow), and even once it is, "are" doesn't sound like "a-r-e" blended the usual
  way — it says /ɑːr/. **Mark the whole word as heart for now**; note that r's formal lesson is
  next.
- **he** — h hasn't been formally taught yet either (also L1.08, tomorrow) — but once it is,
  "he" will become **fully decodable**: h + e, both regular sounds. **Heart part today: just
  the untaught letter h** — say explicitly, "tomorrow this word graduates off the heart-word
  list in every way that matters."

### 7. Read it

**Track A (child — snack time):**
> Sam got a cup. He got a nut. He sat. The nut is in the cup. Is the nut in the cup? The nut is
> in the cup.

**Track B (adult — a ten o'clock break):**
*(Tutor context, spoken not decoded: "Dan takes a short coffee break at ten most mornings.")*
> Dan got a cup. He got a mug. He sat at ten. The sun is up. Is Dan at the dock? Dan is not at
> the dock. Dan sat and sat.

**Questions (2 literal, 1 think):**
1. What two things did the main character get, one after the other?
2. Where did the story say the character was, or wasn't?
3. Think: why might someone take a short break at exactly the same time every day?

### 8. Listen & Talk (5 min)

> **The Ten O'Clock Rule**
> On busy construction sites, supervisors learned something surprising: workers who took one
> proper ten-minute break mid-morning, every single day at the same time, made fewer mistakes
> in the afternoon than workers who pushed through without stopping. It seemed backwards at
> first — how could stopping work make people more productive? But the body and brain both
> need small pauses to reset concentration, especially doing careful, repetitive tasks in the
> heat. Now some companies build the "ten o'clock rule" right into the schedule: everyone
> stops together, drinks water, and stretches — no exceptions, not even for the boss.

**Tier-2 words:**
- **productive** — getting a lot of useful work done, not just being busy. "She had a
  productive morning and finished three tasks." "Short breaks can actually make you more
  productive." Your turn: what makes your day feel productive?
- **concentration** — the ability to focus your attention on one thing without being
  distracted. "Loud noise breaks my concentration." "He read for an hour with total
  concentration." Your turn: what helps you concentrate?

**Discussion questions:**
1. What did supervisors notice about workers who took a proper break?
2. Why do you think the passage says "no exceptions, not even for the boss"?

### 9. Check (2 min)

- **Real (5):** cut, ten, sun, mug, net
- **Pseudo (5):** gup, kec, nud, pem, dut
- **Dictated (1):** "cup"

**≥10/11 → move to L1.08.** Below that: redo the Urdu-tip minimal-pair drill (whichever
sitting applies) if the misses cluster around e/i or u/o confusions specifically; otherwise
reteach the individual letter-sound.

---

### Tutor notes

- **Common error: e/i confusion** (pet↔pit) or **u/o confusion** (cut↔cot) — see the Urdu tips
  above; this is a genuine cross-linguistic pattern, not carelessness, for many learners.
  Correction: minimal-pair listening practice, not repetition drilling of the word in
  isolation.
- **Never** let the learner guess a vowel from the surrounding consonants' "shape" — always
  isolate and blend the actual vowel sound.
- **For adults:** the construction-site break story is a good real-world hook — ask if their
  own workplace has (or should have) something like a "ten o'clock rule."
- **For young kids:** if snack time is imminent, this is a natural moment to actually act out
  Track A's cup-and-nut scene with a real cup and a real small snack, if available — optional,
  not required.
